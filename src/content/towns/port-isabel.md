Port Isabel is the town that most reliably catches out contractors working from assumptions. Two of the things everybody thinks they know about it are wrong, and both errors get built into specifications before anyone checks.

It is not in the Seaward wind zone, despite sitting on the water opposite an island that is. And the ground is not sand, despite a soil series named after the place that sounds like it should be. Getting either wrong produces a specification that is confidently incorrect.

## What kind of town is this in construction terms?

A working waterfront with a resort economy attached, which produces an unusually mixed building stock in a small footprint.

There are working-waterfront cottages, mid-century frame and block houses, newer canal-front construction, marina commercial buildings and the industrial structures of a commercial fishing port. Within a few streets you can move from a 1950s block house to a metal-clad boatyard building to a dense waterfront resort community on filled ground.

That mix matters because it means no single specification serves the town. The right roof for a shrimp basin building is not the right roof for a cottage near the lighthouse, and the ground conditions under a canal-front lot differ from those a few blocks inland.

The economy behind it is commercial shrimping and the working waterfront, marine services and boatyards, and tourism spillover from the island across the causeway. Those are three different clients with three different tolerances for downtime, and the industrial ones cannot simply stop operating for a roof.

What the whole town shares is exposure. Everything here lives in salt air, on ground with a high water table, in a hurricane landfall corridor, under a certification regime. The variation is in what sits on top.

## Why is Port Isabel Inland I rather than Seaward?

Because the zone boundary is a drawn line, not a measurement from the water.

In Cameron County the windstorm zone line follows U.S. Highway 77. It is a legal boundary rather than a physical description of exposure, and it produces results that look counterintuitive from a boat. South Padre Island is the county's only Seaward assignment. Port Isabel, directly across the water, sits in Inland I alongside Brownsville and Los Fresnos.

This surprises people, and it should be treated as a fact to be looked up rather than reasoned about. A contractor who describes your zone by pointing at how close the bay is has not read the map.

The practical effect is on the certification specification rather than on whether certification applies. It applies either way — a full roof replacement here is a certified job, with the application filed before work begins. What the zone changes is the design basis the work is held to.

One derived requirement flattens the difference people expect to find. Code raises the shingle fastening requirement from four to six per strip in high-wind zones at and above a defined threshold, and since the lowest zone assigned anywhere in Cameron County already meets that threshold, the six-fastener pattern governs the whole county. Nobody in Port Isabel gets a lighter pattern for being Inland rather than Seaward.

The [wind zones](/compliance/seaward-vs-inland-wind-zones/) page covers the assignments in full.

## What is actually under the ground here?

Saline clay, and the naming is the trap.

The Point Isabel series is a clay, not a sand. It is a fine, mixed, active, hyperthermic Typic Calciustepts formed on vegetated dunes, with slow permeability, electrical conductivity ranging from 4 to 40 dS/m and sodium adsorption ratios from 11 to 95. Anyone writing "Point Isabel fine sand" on a soils report is describing something that does not exist, and it is worth querying rather than accepting.

That matters because sand and clay behave in opposite ways under a pad. Sand drains and compacts predictably. This clay is slow-draining, salt-affected and, on the bay margins, part of a landscape where the water table sits within a few feet of the surface.

Lomalta clay appears here too, and it is the bay-margin soil in its purest form: a Typic Natraquerts, poorly drained, semi-marshy, sitting only slightly above sea level, with electrical conductivity from 12 to 50 dS/m and exchangeable sodium above 15 percent in every horizon.

Chargo silty clay adds the shrink-swell dimension, with COLE values from 0.09 to 0.14 putting it firmly in very high shrink-swell territory.

Between them, those three describe ground that is slow to drain, chemically aggressive, prone to volume change, and frequently wet. None of that makes it unbuildable. It makes it engineered rather than assumed, which is the argument set out on the [building pads](/services/building-pads/) page.

One honest limit: corollaries about what this chemistry does to buried concrete or metal are engineering inference, and we do not publish them without a local geotechnical source. It is a real question for your engineer.

## What does the salt actually do at this distance?

Everything relevant to metal, and Port Isabel is unambiguously inside the exclusion.

Manufacturer warranties draw the line at 1,500 feet from a salt water environment. Within that band, Galvalume and galvanized steel carry no substrate warranty, and manufacturer guidance is direct that neither will stand up to coastal conditions. Aluminium is the substrate that holds long-term coastal paint warranties there.

Measure that against the Laguna Madre and the ship channel rather than against the Gulf alone, and essentially the whole town falls inside it. This is not a marginal call requiring a tape measure on most properties here.

The mechanism explains where the damage concentrates. Airborne chlorides are hygroscopic — they hold moisture against a surface long after the rain has stopped — so corrosion tracks deposition and wash-off rather than distance alone. On a real building that means the sheltered, unwashed parts fail first: undersides of overhangs, eave returns, the lee side of equipment, anywhere rain does not scour.

Fasteners follow the same logic. For aluminium panels the coastal answer is 300-series stainless, typically 304. The 410 stainless commonly stocked is explicitly not coastal-rated. Electroplated zinc is not an exterior fastener, and galvanized fasteners against aluminium cladding are not recommended.

The Laguna Madre itself is worth understanding as context: a hypersaline lagoon of roughly 280,910 acres averaging about four and a half feet deep. Shallow, poorly flushed and salt-concentrated, sitting immediately upwind of the town for much of the year. The [metal roofing](/services/metal-roofing/) page covers the substrate decision in detail.

## Which building code applies here?

The 2018 I-codes, and Port Isabel is the one jurisdiction in this county where we can say that from a primary source.

That is unusual enough to be worth stating plainly. For Brownsville, for South Padre Island and for unincorporated Cameron County we do not publish an adopted edition, because we could not confirm current adoptions. Port Isabel we can.

It also sets up the clearest example in the region of the two-track problem. The city reviews and inspects against its adopted edition. The windstorm certificate is issued against the edition the state framework is currently running, which for work begun on or after 1 April 2026 is the 2024 IRC and IBC.

So a Port Isabel project today is reviewed locally against one edition and certified against a newer one. Nobody in either process is responsible for telling you that, and the gap between them is where owners fall.

The practical instruction is straightforward: where the two produce different requirements, build to the stricter. Meeting the higher standard satisfies both. Meeting the lower satisfies the city and fails the certificate, and the certificate is the one your insurer cares about.

The [Cameron County permits](/compliance/cameron-county-permits/) page sets out how jurisdiction and code adoption work across the county.

## How high does anything have to sit?

Higher than the ground suggests, and the answer comes from the city rather than from a contractor.

Port Isabel is bay-front and low-lying, with surge exposure from the Laguna Madre and the ship channel driving elevation requirements. In a mapped Special Flood Hazard Area the lowest floor must sit at or above base flood elevation plus whatever freeboard the community has adopted.

Where a property falls into a coastal high-hazard zone, the rules change categorically rather than incrementally: participating communities must prohibit the use of fill for structural support of buildings, elevation comes from piles or columns, and enclosures below have to be breakaway construction.

That single rule determines whether a site preparation job here is a pad job or something else entirely. It is why a bid written on mainland assumptions can be wrong before anyone arrives.

We do not publish the flood zone extents or freeboard figures for Port Isabel, because we could not verify current values from a primary source. Your floodplain administrator holds both, and the [flood elevation](/compliance/fema-flood-elevation-and-pad-height/) page explains how the determination works and what to ask for.

What we can say is that on ground this low, with a water table this high, elevation and drainage are the same conversation rather than two separate ones.

## What does the older housing stock actually need?

Attention to attachment and to details, more than to material choice.

The mid-century frame and block houses here were built to standards that predate the current framework, and many have been re-roofed at least once under varying regimes. What that produces is a stock where the deck condition and the fastening pattern are the unknowns, and both are only knowable once the covering comes off.

That is the argument for the deck unit rate being agreed in writing before a tear-off starts. On houses of this age in this atmosphere, a worse-than-expected deck is common enough that it should be priced rather than discovered.

The working-waterfront cottages carry the same issues with less structure to work with, and on the smaller ones the honest answer is sometimes a repair rather than a replacement. A localised failure on a sound assembly is a repair, as set out on the [roof repair](/services/roof-repair/) page.

Newer canal-front construction is a different proposition: generally better attached, more likely to carry documentation, and more likely to be in a flood zone with elevation already addressed. There the questions are about substrate and fastener grade rather than about structure.

Across all of it, the certification question arises the moment a project becomes a full replacement, which is covered on the [windstorm certification](/compliance/windstorm-certification-wpi-8/) page.

## What does the working waterfront need that houses do not?

Systems that survive constant salt load, and phasing that respects an operating business.

The shrimp basin and boatyard buildings are industrial metal structures under about the harshest exposure available in this county. They are close to the water, frequently open or partially open, and they contain operations that generate their own moisture and chemistry.

On those buildings the substrate decision is not a preference. Steel-based panels have no substrate warranty at this distance from saltwater, and the sheltered undersides that never get rinsed corrode faster than the exposed field.

Exposed-fastener panel is common and often correct on this kind of structure, at adequate slope. What it asks in return is inspection roughly twice a year, because pinned panels work their fasteners loose as thermal movement is restrained. On a working building that inspection is frequently nobody's job, which is why backed-out fasteners are the classic finding here.

Phasing matters more than on a house. A boatyard or a processing building cannot stop for a roof, so the sequencing gets agreed before mobilisation rather than negotiated at seven in the morning. The [commercial](/commercial/) pages cover that side.

Low-slope sections on these buildings bring their own rules, including that ponding is an absolute exclusion on Galvalume warranties.

## What differs between the parts of town?

Three areas, three different problems.

Historic Port Isabel, near the lighthouse, holds the older frame and block stock. Smaller structures, more previous repairs, more unknowns under the covering, and the tightest constraints on access.

The Long Island Village area is a dense waterfront resort community built on filled ground. Filled ground raises its own questions about what is underneath and how it was placed, which matters for anything structural and for how drainage behaves.

The shrimp basin and working waterfront is the industrial end: metal buildings under constant salt load, operating businesses, and the most demanding substrate and fastener requirements in town.

What they share is the wind zone, the code adoption, and the salt. What they do not share is the building type, the ownership pattern or the tolerance for disruption, and a contractor treating the town as one condition will get at least one of those wrong.

The Port Isabel Lighthouse State Historic Site anchors the older part of town — an 1852 structure standing 72 feet, the last Texas lighthouse open to the public, with its Fresnel lens reinstalled in 2022. It is a useful landmark for directions and a reminder of how long buildings here have been dealing with this atmosphere.

## What do the storms on record mean for this shoreline?

That the failure mode is the roof covering, consistently, and that Category 1 events do serious damage here.

Beulah came ashore in 1967 as a Category 3 between the mouth of the Rio Grande and Brownsville. Allen made a Category 3 landfall near Port Mansfield in 1980 with a 12-foot surge. Both reshaped the barrier island across the water.

More instructive are the recent ones. Dolly in 2008 was a Category 1 at mainland landfall with about $1.05 billion in damage, described in the final report as mainly moderate structural damage, primarily to roofs, on South Padre Island. Hanna in 2020 was also a Category 1, roughly $1.2 billion, with a peak gust of 103 miles per hour recorded at Rincon del San Jose and hundreds of poorly built structures suffering roof damage.

Neither was a major hurricane at landfall. Both produced billion-dollar losses, concentrated in coverings on weaker construction rather than in collapsed engineered buildings.

For a town in this position, that is the argument for spending on attachment and detail rather than on appearance. It is also the argument for the certification sequence, because storm urgency is exactly when the filing gets skipped, and after close-in only an engineer can certify.

Day to day the threat is smaller and more frequent. This region averages fewer than 30 thunderstorm days a year with severe conditions on under a third of those, and downbursts are the primary wind threat.

## What are we most often asked for here?

Re-roofs on the older stock, metal on the waterfront buildings, and drainage on low-lying lots.

Re-roofing dominates on the residential side, which fits a housing stock of this age. The recurring questions are deck condition, fastening pattern, and whether the substrate specified is right for the exposure.

On the commercial and industrial side it is metal — repairs, fastener replacement, and full replacements where corrosion has gone past repair. The substrate conversation is the one that matters, and it is the one most often skipped by whoever quoted previously.

On the ground side it is elevation and drainage on low, wet lots, frequently with a flood determination and sometimes with a drainage district involved. The [drainage and site shaping](/services/drainage-and-site-shaping/) page covers how that works on flat, slow-draining ground.

And after any significant weather, storm assessment — where the most valuable thing we do is sometimes telling somebody a repair will serve.

## What should a Port Isabel owner establish first?

Five answers, and four of them come from the city rather than from a contractor.

Your flood zone and the elevation it requires. This is the determination that decides whether a site preparation job is a pad job or a pile job, and it is not something to infer from what a neighbour did.

Your distance from open water, measured against the bay and the channel rather than the Gulf. On most properties here the answer puts you inside the metal substrate exclusion, but knowing it settles the specification conversation before it starts.

Whether the property sits inside the city limits. Port Isabel's own building department applies its adopted 2018 I-codes within the city, and unincorporated county ground nearby answers to the county instead. That single answer decides who reviews the work.

What documentation the property already holds — previous roof work, any windstorm certificate, any survey or elevation certificate. On a town with a housing stock this age, that record frequently exists somewhere and is rarely looked for until it is needed.

And, if you are on the waterfront or on filled ground, whether a drainage district easement touches the tract.

None of that requires expertise. It requires a morning of phone calls, and it changes what any bid you receive actually means.

## How does the causeway affect getting work done?

Logistically more than technically, and it is worth knowing before a schedule is agreed.

The link to the barrier island is formally the Queen Isabella Memorial Bridge, though Queen Isabella Causeway is normal local usage and nobody will misunderstand you. It carries essentially all traffic between the mainland and the island.

For construction that means material and crews serving island work route through Port Isabel, and during peak tourism periods that route is busy. Deliveries of long material — metal panels in particular — need planning around it rather than assuming.

It also means Port Isabel functions as the staging point for a good deal of island work, which affects yard space, laydown and how a project's logistics get organised.

For an owner the practical consequence is modest but real: schedules here should account for access, and a contractor who has worked the area will raise it without being asked.

## What we will not tell you about Port Isabel

Several things, deliberately.

We do not publish this town's flood zone extents or its freeboard requirement, because we could not confirm either from a primary source. Those belong to the city's floodplain administrator.

We do not publish a current design wind speed. That is determined per structure under the applicable engineering standard rather than read from a table, and any figure offered for a whole town cannot be supported.

We do not publish inferences about what the saline, sodic ground does to buried concrete or metal. That needs a local geotechnical source we do not have.

And we do not issue windstorm certificates or make floodplain determinations, because no contractor can. Certification comes from the department, an appointed qualified inspector, or a licensed professional engineer.

What we do is read the published assignments, build to what they require, sequence the filings correctly, and say plainly when an answer belongs to somebody else.
