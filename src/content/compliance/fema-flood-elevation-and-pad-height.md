Almost every argument about pad height on this coast is really an argument about a number somebody has not looked up yet. The elevation requirement is not a matter of judgement or local custom. It is a mapped figure, plus whatever margin your community has adopted on top of it, and it is knowable before anyone quotes the work.

What varies is who tells you, what your zone permits you to do about it, and whether the ground underneath will hold what you build.

## What does base flood elevation actually mean on the ground?

It is the height floodwater is predicted to reach in the mapped flood event, expressed as an elevation above a vertical datum rather than as a depth above your yard.

That distinction causes most of the confusion. Base flood elevation is not "two feet of water here." It is a surveyed height on a common reference system, and comparing it to your lot requires knowing your lot's elevation on the same system. That comparison is a surveyor's work, not an eyeball assessment.

In a mapped Special Flood Hazard Area, the federal requirement for A and AE zones is that the lowest floor of new construction, or of a substantially improved structure, sits at or above that elevation. Communities that participate in the flood insurance programme adopt and enforce that as a minimum.

Note the phrase "lowest floor." It includes more than people assume. An enclosed area below an elevated structure can count, which is why the treatment of garages, storage and mechanical spaces beneath a raised building is a design question rather than an afterthought.

What this means practically is that pad height is derived, not chosen. Somebody establishes the required elevation, a surveyor establishes what you have, and the difference plus the structure's own build-up determines how much material moves.

## What is freeboard, and who decides it?

Freeboard is a margin of safety added above the flood level, and it is a local decision layered on the federal minimum.

FEMA describes it as a factor of safety usually expressed in feet above a flood level. Communities that adopt freeboard commonly choose between one and three feet. It exists because the mapped elevation is a model output rather than a guarantee, and because building slightly higher is far cheaper than the alternative.

Because it is local, it varies between jurisdictions that are minutes apart. A city may require more than the county surrounding it, or the reverse. Nothing about your neighbour's project two roads over reliably tells you what applies to your lot if the two sit under different authorities.

We do not publish freeboard figures for South Padre Island or for Cameron County on this page, because we could not confirm current values from a primary source. That is an acknowledged gap rather than an oversight. Your floodplain administrator holds the number and will give it to you.

Insurance responds to freeboard even where code does not require it. Building above the minimum can affect how a structure is rated, which is a conversation worth having with your carrier before the pad height is fixed, not after.

## Why can you not build a fill pad on the Gulf side?

Because in coastal high-hazard areas the rule is not about height. It is about what may carry the load.

In V and VE zones, participating communities must prohibit the use of fill for structural support of buildings. Elevation is achieved with piles or columns. The lowest horizontal structural member has to be at or above the base flood elevation, and anything enclosed below has to be breakaway construction designed to fail without taking the structure with it.

The reasoning is wave action. These zones are mapped where waves are expected during the base flood, and a fill pad in that environment is not a foundation — it is material waiting to be scoured out from under a building. Removing the fill removes the failure mode.

For site work, this changes the scope entirely rather than adjusting it. A bid built on mainland assumptions, pricing imported material and compaction to raise a pad, is answering a question that does not apply. The earthwork on such a site is about access, drainage, and preparing for a pile-supported structure, not about building a platform.

South Padre Island is where this bites hardest in Cameron County. It is also the only part of the county in the Seaward wind zone, so the two strictest regimes in the area apply to the same ground, administered by different authorities that do not check each other's work.

## What does an A or AE zone allow that a V or VE does not?

Fill, subject to conditions, and a lower floor reference point.

In A and AE zones the requirement attaches to the lowest floor rather than to the lowest horizontal structural member, and elevation on properly engineered fill is generally an available method. That is why so much of mainland construction here involves building a pad up rather than putting a house on piles.

The conditions matter, though. Fill supporting a structure has to be engineered fill — placed, moisture-conditioned and compacted to a specification, not simply dumped and graded. Federal guidance on structures built on fill treats the two common compaction standards as roughly equivalent at defined percentages and caps lift thickness, and Texas engineering practice states plainly that foundations are not to be supported by non-engineered fill.

Communities can and do impose additional requirements on fill: how far it must extend beyond the structure, how it is protected from erosion, and what it does to neighbouring drainage. Raising your lot pushes water somewhere, and somewhere is usually your neighbour.

The practical summary is that A and AE give you a method, not a licence. The method has an engineer attached to it on anything structural.

## How does a local ordinance change the number?

By adding requirements the federal minimum never mentions, usually tied to something visible on the street.

Harlingen publishes an example worth knowing because it is concrete and locally citable. Under its subdivision ordinance, foundations in Zone X sit no less than 18 inches above top of curb; inside a Special Flood Hazard Area the minimum rises to 24 inches above top of curb; and a lot within 100 feet of a drainage inlet has its finished floor set six inches higher than neighbouring lots.

Notice what that does. It sets a requirement even in Zone X, which is outside the mapped high-risk area entirely, and it references the curb rather than a datum. Somebody building in Harlingen who checked only the flood map would satisfy the federal rule and miss the local one.

Most jurisdictions here have an equivalent answer even when it is not published on a website. Somebody at the counter knows it, and asking directly is faster than reasoning from what a contractor did on a nearby job last year.

The general lesson is that the flood map is the floor, not the specification. The local ordinance is where the number you actually build to comes from.

## What does "substantially improved" mean, and why does it catch people?

It is the threshold at which an existing building stops being grandfathered and starts being treated much like new construction.

The concept exists because floodplain rules would be meaningless if a structure could be rebuilt indefinitely under the standards of the year it was first put up. Once improvement or repair reaches a defined proportion of the building's value, the elevation requirements apply.

This catches people because renovation budgets grow. A project scoped below the threshold that expands mid-stream can cross it, and crossing it late is considerably more expensive than planning for it. The same applies to repair after damage, where the cost of restoring a building can reach the threshold without anyone having intended a major project.

The determination belongs to your floodplain administrator, and the figures involved are theirs to apply. What matters for planning is knowing the threshold exists before you scope the work, so the question is asked while it is still cheap to answer.

If you are buying a property in a mapped area with the intention of renovating, this is a due-diligence item rather than a construction detail. It can change what the project is.

## How does the ground here complicate elevation?

Because raising a structure and supporting a structure are different problems, and the soils in this region are unhelpful with the second.

Much of the mainland sits on expansive clays that change volume with moisture. Those soils swell when wet and crack when dry, with cracks in the more active series staying open for a large share of the year. A pad built to the right elevation on ground that moves seasonally has solved the flood question and left the foundation question open.

Drainage compounds it. The poorly drained series here have runoff ranging from very slow to ponded, and seasonal high water tables sit within a few feet of the surface for much of the year. Building up on ground that does not shed water means the fill sits in a wet environment, which affects how it behaves and how it should be placed.

Nearer the bay the chemistry becomes a factor as well, with sodic and saline profiles common at the margins. We do not publish inferences about what that does to buried concrete or metal, because that is an engineering determination requiring a local geotechnical source we do not have. It is a real question to put to your engineer.

The honest summary is that elevation is the easy half. Whether the ground will hold what you build at that elevation is a geotechnical question, and on this ground it is usually worth answering properly.

## What does engineered fill actually require?

A specification, testing, and somebody accountable for both.

Compaction is stated as a percentage of a laboratory maximum for the specific material being placed, which is why the same percentage describes different densities on different soils. Lift thickness belongs in the specification alongside it, because material placed too deep does not densify through its full depth however many passes the roller makes.

Material selection matters as much as effort. What is described as select fill in Gulf Coast practice is typically a low-plasticity sandy clay or an imported caliche blend placed in shallow lifts. That is a description of engineering practice rather than a code definition — there is no statewide codified definition of select fill in Texas, and no code-mandated cap depth. It is specified per project by an engineer.

Testing is what turns a specification into evidence. Density testing during placement produces a record that the fill was built as designed, and that record is worth having when the structure is later financed, insured or sold.

For an owner comparing bids, the useful questions are what material is being placed, to what compaction standard, in what lift thickness, and who is testing it. A bid silent on all four is cheaper because it is smaller.

## How do you find out what applies to your lot?

Three sources, in order, and none of them is a contractor.

The flood map first, which is publicly available and shows the mapped zone for a location. That tells you the category you are in and whether a base flood elevation is published for your area.

Your local floodplain administrator second. This is the person who applies the rules for your jurisdiction, knows the local freeboard and any ordinance layered on top, and issues floodplain development permits where they are required. They are also the right person to ask about substantial improvement thresholds.

A surveyor third, because comparing the required elevation to your actual ground requires measurement on a common datum. On new construction this usually produces an elevation certificate at completion documenting where the finished structure sits, which is commonly requested for insurance rating and at sale.

Do this before the site plan is final. The most expensive version of this sequence is the one where grading has been designed, material has been priced, and the elevation requirement arrives afterwards.

Note that the flood question is entirely separate from the windstorm question. Different authority, different map, different determination. Confirming one tells you nothing about the other, and a property can be straightforward on one and difficult on the other.

## What happens if you get it wrong?

The consequences land on the building rather than on the contractor, which is why owners should care about it more than anyone.

A structure built below the required elevation in a mapped area is a compliance problem for the community that permitted it and an insurance problem for whoever owns it. Flood insurance rating responds directly to elevation, and a building sitting lower than it should be is rated accordingly for as long as it stands.

At sale it becomes a disclosure and a due-diligence item. Buyers in mapped areas ask for elevation certificates, and a certificate showing a deficiency is a negotiating position for the other side.

Remedies after the fact are limited and expensive. Raising an existing structure is possible and costly. Adding fill around a building that is already too low does not change where its lowest floor sits.

And in a coastal high-hazard zone, a building supported on fill where fill is prohibited is not a paperwork problem. It is a structural exposure in exactly the event the rule was written for.

The pattern across all of these is the same as with certification: the cheap moment to get it right is before anything is built, and every later moment is dramatically more expensive.

## What does all this do to a dirt work bid?

It determines whether the bid is even answering the right question.

On a mainland A or AE site, the flood requirement sets the target elevation, which sets the volume of material, which is usually the largest single cost on the job. Haul distance then determines what that volume costs to deliver. Change the target elevation by a foot and the whole number moves.

On a V or VE site, the fill approach is off the table and the earthwork scope becomes something else entirely.

In both cases the approvals sit upstream of the price. A floodplain development permit may be required, drainage district approval may be required for what your site discharges, and larger disturbances bring stormwater obligations. Those get resolved before the site plan is finalised, because each can change the design.

This is why a firm price given before the zone, the required elevation and the approvals are known is not a favour. It is a number with assumptions inside it, and the assumptions surface later as changes.

## What should you have in hand before anyone quotes the work?

Four things, and gathering them takes phone calls rather than money.

The flood zone for the parcel, taken from the map rather than from memory or from what applies next door. If the lot sits near a boundary, get the administrator to confirm which side it falls on.

The required elevation, meaning the base flood elevation where one is published plus the local freeboard your community has adopted. Those are two separate numbers from the same office, and asking for one often does not produce the other.

A survey, or at least a known elevation for the existing ground on the same datum the requirement uses. Without it, nobody can tell you how much material moves, and any volume in a bid is an estimate dressed as a price.

Any existing reports on the property. A geotechnical report, a prior survey, an old elevation certificate, a plat showing drainage easements. These frequently exist in a file somewhere from a previous owner or a previous project, and they are worth more to an accurate quote than any amount of description.

Worth establishing at the same time, because it decides who you are dealing with for everything else: whether the parcel sits inside a city or in unincorporated county. Inside a city, the municipal floodplain administrator applies the local ordinance. Outside, the county does, and the two frequently adopt different requirements on the same stretch of ground.

If a drainage district easement crosses the tract, add that to the list. Raising a lot changes where water goes, and the district that governs the receiving channel has a view on it.

None of this is a prerequisite for a conversation. It is what separates a conversation that ends in a real number from one that ends in a range.

## Where does our authority stop on flood questions?

At every determination, which is most of this page.

We do not make floodplain determinations, establish base flood elevations, set freeboard, decide substantial improvement thresholds, or issue floodplain development permits. Those belong to your community's floodplain administrator and to the federal mapping behind them. We do not perform surveys or issue elevation certificates, which are a surveyor's or engineer's work. And we do not provide the geotechnical recommendation that decides what fill your structure needs.

What we do is build the site to the elevation and specification those determinations produce, place and compact fill to a stated standard where fill is the right method, and tell you plainly when the answer belongs elsewhere.

Where a figure could not be verified from a primary source, this page says so rather than filling the gap. The freeboard requirements for South Padre Island and for Cameron County are the clearest examples, and asking your administrator directly is both faster and more reliable than any number a contractor could offer.
