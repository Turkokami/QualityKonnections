Erosion control is the part of a construction site that looks like an afterthought and functions as a legal obligation. Silt fence and rock entrances get treated as a formality, right up to the point where an inspector arrives, or a neighbour photographs a plume of Valley clay leaving a site after a storm.

On this coast there is a further reason to take it seriously: the water it reaches is already failing to meet its standards, and the soils here are close to the worst case for sediment control.

## When does a site actually need authorisation?

Generally when construction disturbs one or more acres — and the acre is measured more broadly than owners expect.

The threshold counts total disturbed area across the project, not the footprint of a building. Clearing, grubbing, stockpiling, haul roads, laydown areas and staging all count as disturbance. A modest structure on a site with extensive clearing and access work can cross the line.

The larger trap is the common plan of development. A half-acre lot that forms part of a larger subdivision or phased project can be caught by the total disturbance of that larger plan, even though the individual parcel is small. Phased projects are where this surfaces most often, because each phase feels like a separate job and the regulation does not treat it as one.

Where the threshold is met, the project operates under the state construction general permit, which brings a set of obligations: site-specific planning, controls installed and maintained, inspection on a defined schedule, records kept, and notification requirements at the start and at completion.

Below the threshold, the general permit may not apply, but local requirements frequently do, and the practical duty not to send mud onto a neighbour's land or into a public channel does not disappear.

The right time to establish which category you are in is before clearing starts, because the plan has to exist and the controls have to be in place before the ground is opened.

## What is a stormwater pollution prevention plan?

The site-specific document that says what will be done, where, by whom and how often.

It identifies the site and its drainage, the areas to be disturbed, and where water leaves. It specifies the controls: what type, in what location, to what standard. It names who is responsible for inspecting them, on what schedule, and what happens when something is found deficient. And it establishes the record-keeping, because compliance here is demonstrated through documentation as much as through condition.

A plan is not a template with an address typed into it. Its value is in being specific to the ground it covers — where the low points are, which direction the site drains, where the receiving water is, what the soils do.

It also changes as the site does. A plan drawn for the clearing phase does not describe the site during utility installation or after grading is complete, and it is expected to be updated as conditions change rather than filed once and forgotten.

The practical test of a good plan is whether somebody unfamiliar with the site could arrive, read it, walk the ground and tell whether the site is in compliance. If it cannot support that, it is a document rather than a plan.

## What controls actually go on a site here?

A layered set, because no single measure does the job.

Perimeter controls intercept sediment attempting to leave: silt fence, fibre logs, berms or similar, sited along the down-gradient boundary and around anything that drains off site.

Inlet protection keeps sediment out of drainage structures, which matters enormously where a site drains toward a district facility or a street inlet.

Stabilised construction entrances address the largest single source of off-site sediment on most jobs, which is not sheet flow at all — it is mud carried onto the road by tyres and then washed into the storm system by the next rain.

Stockpile management, because exposed piles of fine material are highly erodible and frequently sited without much thought.

Slope and channel protection where water concentrates, since concentrated flow does more damage than sheet flow over the same area.

And stabilisation, which is the one most often skipped: establishing cover over disturbed ground as areas are completed rather than leaving the whole site bare until the end.

Sequencing matters as much as selection. Controls installed before disturbance work. Controls installed after the first storm are a response to damage already done.

## Why does the ground here make this harder?

Because fine clay behaves differently from the soils most erosion control guidance was written around.

Much of this region is heavy clay, and clay particles stay in suspension far longer than sand or silt. That has two consequences. Sediment travels much further from the site than intuition suggests, and it passes through barriers that would trap coarser material effectively. A silt fence that would handle a sandy site can pass a substantial fraction of what a clay site produces.

The behaviour of the ground itself compounds it. The expansive clays here crack open as they dry, and cracks in the more active series stay open for a large part of the year. The poorly drained series have runoff ranging from very slow to ponded, so water sits, then moves as a slug when it finally does move.

Wet clay is also unworkable in a way that affects the schedule directly. A site that receives a real rain does not simply resume the next morning, which is why the sequencing of disturbance and stabilisation deserves planning rather than optimism.

Near the bay margins there is a further wrinkle: sodic and saline soils, with the water table within a few feet of the surface. Establishing vegetative cover on that ground is genuinely difficult, which pushes stabilisation toward other methods.

None of this changes the obligation. It changes what a competent plan looks like, and it means specifications imported from elsewhere in Texas frequently underperform here.

## What does the weather pattern mean for planning?

That the risk arrives in a small number of sharp events rather than as steady background rain.

This region averages fewer than 30 thunderstorm days a year, with severe conditions on under a third of those, and roughly 21 hail and wind events a year recorded across a long window, peaking in May. Downbursts are the primary wind threat and occur far more often than tornado damage.

For erosion control that pattern is a mixed blessing. Long dry stretches mean controls sit unused and are easy to neglect, and it is precisely the neglected silt fence that fails when a high-intensity event finally arrives. Intense short-duration rainfall generates concentrated flow quickly, which overwhelms measures designed for gentler conditions.

The planning response is to treat every quiet week as preparation rather than as evidence the controls are unnecessary. Inspection schedules exist for exactly this reason, and rainfall-triggered inspection requirements exist because the condition of a site after an event is different from its condition before one.

Tropical systems are the extreme case. Regional storm history shows rainfall totals well beyond what routine controls contemplate — over 13 inches at Brownsville during Dolly and more than 15 inches at Santa Rosa during Hanna. A site left open going into a named storm is a different risk category, and securing it in advance is a scheduling decision rather than an emergency response.

## What is the receiving water, and why does it matter?

Because the systems this region drains into are already impaired, and that raises the stakes on what leaves a site.

The Arroyo Colorado carries formal impairments: a dissolved oxygen impairment on its tidal segment and a bacteria impairment on the segment above it. Those designations mean the water body is not meeting standards for its designated uses before any new discharge is considered.

The wider receiving environment is unusually sensitive. The Laguna Madre is a hypersaline lagoon of roughly 280,910 acres averaging about four and a half feet deep — shallow, poorly flushed, and ill-suited to absorbing sediment load. Groundwater across the lower Valley is mostly brackish.

Between a site and those waters sits the district network of canals, drains and channels described on the [drainage districts](/compliance/drainage-district-and-irrigation-canal-crossings/) page. Sediment that leaves your site does not disappear into a natural system; it settles in somebody's channel and becomes part of a maintenance burden already measured in millions of cubic yards.

That is the honest reason to take erosion control seriously beyond the regulatory one. On this landscape, sediment leaving a site has a specific destination and a specific person who has to deal with it.

## Who is responsible, and what does enforcement look like?

Responsibility is assigned in the authorisation, and it can sit with the owner, the contractor, or both.

That is why it belongs in the contract rather than in an assumption. On projects with multiple contractors, the question of who maintains which controls, who inspects, and who keeps the records is precisely the kind of thing that goes unassigned until something goes wrong.

What an inspection looks at is straightforward: whether the plan exists and is current, whether the controls described in it are actually installed where it says, whether they are in working condition, whether inspections have happened on schedule, and whether the records support it.

The most common findings are unglamorous. Controls not installed before disturbance began. Silt fence undermined, overtopped or never trenched in properly. Construction entrance degraded to the point of uselessness. Inlet protection removed for convenience and not replaced. Stabilisation not established on completed areas. Records missing for periods when work clearly happened.

Consequences run from corrective action through to penalties, and there is a reputational dimension in a market this size. There is also a practical one: a site that loses material is a site that has to buy and place that material again.

## How does this interact with the other approvals?

It runs alongside them, on its own track, with its own authority.

Stormwater authorisation is environmental regulation. Drainage district approval governs discharge into a district's facility and is a different authority with different rules. Floodplain requirements govern elevation and are administered by your community's floodplain administrator. A municipal or county permit governs construction itself.

None of them substitutes for another, and satisfying one tells you nothing about the others. A project can hold a building permit, have district approval for its outfall, and still be out of compliance on stormwater.

The efficient approach is to identify all of them at the same time, at the start, rather than discovering them in sequence as each becomes an obstacle. On a site of any size in this region the likely list is: municipal or county permit, floodplain determination, district approval for discharge and any crossing, and stormwater authorisation.

Each has its own lead time, and several run on meeting schedules rather than on demand. Started together they overlap; started one at a time they add up.

## What does this add to a dirt work bid?

Real cost, and it should be visible rather than buried.

Materials and installation for perimeter controls, inlet protection, a stabilised entrance and stockpile management. Maintenance across the life of the job, which is labour rather than a one-off. Inspection and record-keeping time. Stabilisation at completion. And, on larger sites, the preparation of the plan itself.

A bid that omits all of this is not cheaper than one that includes it. It has either not read the site or is planning to install the minimum and hope.

For an owner, the useful questions are whether the project crosses the disturbance threshold, who is preparing the plan, who is named as responsible, who inspects and how often, and what stabilisation is included at the end. Those answers distinguish a contractor who has run permitted sites from one who has not.

There is also a schedule consequence worth pricing honestly: on clay this wet, work stops after significant rain, and a programme that assumes continuous dry working is a programme that will slip.

## What does stabilisation actually mean here?

Establishing something that holds the ground once the equipment leaves — and on this ground that is harder than the word suggests.

Stabilisation is the end state the whole exercise is working toward. Controls are temporary; cover is permanent. A site is not finished when grading is finished, it is finished when disturbed areas are no longer producing sediment, and the obligation to maintain controls runs until that point.

Vegetative cover is the usual answer and the cheapest when it works. The difficulty here is that it does not always work. Near the bay margins the soils are sodic and saline, with high exchangeable sodium and wide-ranging conductivity, and establishing vegetation on that ground is genuinely difficult rather than merely slow. Seasonal high water tables and long dry stretches between rains compound it.

Where vegetation is unreliable, stabilisation shifts to other methods: aggregate, matting, mulch, or hard surfacing on areas that were always going to be paved. The choice belongs in the plan rather than being improvised at the end of a job when the budget is spent.

Timing is the part most often mishandled. Stabilising completed areas progressively, rather than leaving the entire site bare until the last day, reduces both the exposure and the amount of control maintenance required throughout. It is cheaper as well as more compliant, which is unusual enough to be worth noticing.

The failure to avoid is pulling controls at substantial completion, before cover has established. That is a common finding, it is easy to avoid, and the storm that exploits it tends to arrive in the gap.

## What records should the site actually keep?

Enough that compliance can be demonstrated months later by somebody who was not there.

The plan itself, current, on site and accessible. A plan in an office drawer three towns away does not satisfy anyone, and it does not help the crew either.

Inspection records at the required frequency, including the rainfall-triggered ones. Each should record what was looked at, what was found, and what was done about anything deficient. An inspection log with nothing ever found is less credible than one showing problems identified and corrected.

Rainfall records, since inspection obligations are frequently tied to events above a threshold. A simple on-site gauge and a log is enough.

Photographs, dated. They cost nothing and they establish condition at a point in time, which is precisely what is disputed when something goes wrong.

Records of changes to the site and to the plan. As phases progress and controls move, the plan should show it.

Notification records at commencement and completion, and confirmation of who holds responsibility.

Delivery and material records are worth keeping alongside them on larger jobs, because they establish when stockpiles appeared and when they were removed. That timeline answers questions about exposure duration that are otherwise reconstructed from memory.

The reason to be disciplined about this is that condition is transient and records are not. A site can be in perfect order on the day of an inspection and unable to demonstrate it was in order for the preceding four months.

## Which mistakes cost sites the most?

A short list, and most of them are sequencing rather than technique.

Clearing before controls are installed. The first storm after disturbance and before protection does more damage than anything later, and it is entirely avoidable.

Treating the construction entrance as optional. Tracked mud on the public road is the most visible failure a site can produce, it is the one neighbours photograph, and it is the one that puts a site on an inspector's list.

Silt fence installed on the surface rather than trenched in. Water goes under it, the fence stays upright, and the site looks protected while functioning as though it were not.

Leaving stockpiles unprotected. Fine material in a loose pile is the most erodible thing on most sites and frequently sits closest to the boundary.

Removing controls at the wrong moment — either early, before stabilisation, or leaving them in place so long they degrade past usefulness.

Assuming the dry season means the controls do not matter. The long quiet stretches here are exactly what produces neglected measures, and the events when they arrive are intense.

Designing the controls from a standard detail sheet rather than from the site. A layout that ignores where the ground actually falls will protect the wrong boundary competently.

And the paperwork one: nobody named as responsible, so inspections happen inconsistently and records exist for some periods and not others. That is a contract problem rather than a site problem, and it is settled before mobilisation or not at all.
