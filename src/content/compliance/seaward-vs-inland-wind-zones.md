The single most common mistake about wind zones on this coast is assuming they measure distance from water. They do not. They follow a drawn line, and that line does things a shoreline would never do — putting a bayfront town in the same category as an inland one, and assigning a city by name regardless of where the boundary runs past it.

Getting the zone right matters because it sits underneath the specification, the fastening and the certification. Getting it wrong is not a rounding error.

## Why is the boundary a highway rather than a shoreline?

Because it has to be unambiguous, and a coastline is not.

A zone boundary defined as a distance from water would require somebody to decide which water, measured from what point, at what tide, and how bays, channels and the Laguna Madre count. That produces argument on every parcel near the margin. A boundary drawn along a highway produces an answer you can check on a map without a surveyor.

In Cameron County that line runs along U.S. Highway 77. It is a legal boundary rather than a physical description, which is why intuition about proximity to the Gulf is an unreliable guide to which side of it a property falls on.

Layered on top of the line are specific assignments that override geography entirely. The City of Brownsville is assigned Inland I status by rule, whatever the boundary does relative to the city. That is a decision recorded in the zone documentation, not something derived from the map by inspection.

The practical instruction that follows is simple and worth repeating to anyone quoting your job: read the map, do not estimate from the coastline. A contractor who describes your zone by gesturing at how far you are from the beach has not looked it up.

## Which towns fall into which zone?

Three groupings in this county, and the surprises are all in the first two.

The Seaward zone contains South Padre Island alone. Nothing on the mainland carries a Seaward assignment, which regularly catches out owners on the bay shore who assume the water outside the window settles the question.

Inland I is the larger coastal-facing group. It takes in Brownsville, along with Port Isabel, Los Fresnos, Laguna Vista and Laguna Heights, Rio Hondo, Bayview, Arroyo City, Boca Chica, Indian Lake, Lozano, Southmost and Port Brownsville.

Inland II sits further back and covers Harlingen and San Benito, plus La Feria, Combes, Primera, Palm Valley, Rancho Viejo, Olmito, Santa Rosa, Los Indios, Santa Maria and Stuart Place.

Two entries in that middle group deserve attention. Port Isabel is Inland I, which is counterintuitive for a town on the water directly opposite a Seaward area. And Brownsville appears in Inland I through the special assignment rather than through the line.

If your property is not on any of those lists — a rural parcel, an unincorporated area, a subdivision that postdates the naming — that does not mean you have no assignment. It means the parcel has to be located against the actual map rather than matched to a town name.

## What do the two different sets of mph numbers mean?

They describe the same buildings in two measurement conventions, and the gap between them is the source of a great deal of confusion.

Under the legacy 2006 code, using the nominal wind speeds of the older engineering standard, the zone figures were 130 miles per hour for Seaward, 120 for Inland I and 110 for Inland II. When the profession moved to ultimate design wind speeds under the revised standard, the equivalent figures for those same buildings became 168, 155 and 143.

Nothing about any building changed. The unit changed. A homeowner told 155 by one source and 120 by another is not hearing a dispute about safety; they are hearing two conventions without being told which is which.

This matters practically in two ways. First, when reading anything written before the transition — older specifications, existing certificates, product literature — you need to know which convention the number belongs to before comparing it with anything current. Second, a contractor who cannot say which convention a figure comes from is repeating a number rather than using one, and that is a reasonable prompt for more questions.

Neither set should be treated as a design requirement for a new project. They are historical zone assignments, useful for understanding what an existing building was held to.

## Why will nobody give you a current design wind speed?

Because there is no longer a single per-zone figure to give.

Design wind speed for a project being permitted today is determined for the specific structure under the applicable engineering standard. It depends on the building's characteristics, its exposure category, its height and its site. It is an engineering determination, not a lookup by town.

That is why this page does not publish a current mph figure for any zone, and why you should treat any site that hands you one with caution. A single number covering every structure in a town cannot account for the variables the standard actually uses, so it is either a legacy figure being presented as current or an average being presented as a requirement.

What that means for you as an owner is that the number belongs to your engineer, and asking for it early is reasonable. On most residential re-roofing the question resolves through prescriptive code paths rather than a bespoke calculation. On commercial work, on unusual geometry, and anywhere an engineer is already engaged, the figure is theirs to produce.

The zone still matters enormously. It just is not a speed you can read off a table any more.

## What does your zone actually change about the building?

The assembly, the attachment, and the scrutiny.

Attachment is where it shows up most concretely on a roof. Fastening requirements intensify as design speeds rise, with tighter patterns at perimeters and corners where uplift concentrates. Those requirements are not cosmetic; they are what the wind rating on a system depends on, and they are inspected.

There is one derived conclusion for this county that is worth carrying, because it flattens a difference people expect to find. Code requires a minimum of four fasteners per strip shingle and increases that to six in high-wind zones at and above a defined threshold. Since the lowest zone assigned anywhere in Cameron County already sits at that threshold, the six-fastener pattern governs the entire county. There is no part of this county where the lighter pattern is correct, and an owner in Inland II should not expect a discount on that basis.

Material selection responds to zone as well, though on this coast corrosion often drives it harder than wind does. Systems have tested uplift performance, and the standards behind those numbers differ in what they include. One common test is pass/fail against a threshold. Another loads to failure but leaves the deck out of the assembly, which matters on a re-roof where the deck is frequently the limiting element. A third produces ratings that already carry a safety factor of two, so the headline figure and the allowable load differ by half. A fourth tests the complete assembly to failure including anchors and panel deflection, and is generally considered the most reliable basis for standing seam design.

Shingle wind ratings deserve the same care. One standard runs to a class at 110 miles per hour; another, measuring uplift force against resistance, reaches classes up to 190. Both standards state plainly that their results do not directly correlate with wind speeds experienced in service. A high class is evidence of tested performance, not a promise about a particular storm.

## What happens at the county line and beyond it?

The framework changes twice, in different directions.

To the north, Willacy County is inside the designated catastrophe area, so the certification framework applies there in the same way. We do not publish zone assignments for Willacy towns, because the primary map was not something we could verify, and publishing a zone we have not confirmed would be worse than acknowledging the gap. Raymondville, Lyford, Sebastian, San Perlita and Port Mansfield are all in the programme; their specific assignments should come from the department or from the engineer certifying the work.

To the west, the framework stops. Hidalgo County is not part of the designated catastrophe area — it is second-tier, which is a different classification that sounds adjacent. There are no Seaward or Inland assignments there because the zone system does not extend to it.

That produces the sharp discontinuity this region is known for among people who work across it. A structure in San Benito carries certification obligations. A comparable structure in Weslaco does not. The buildings can be identical, built the same year by the same builder, and the compliance path diverges entirely.

For anyone comparing bids across that line, this is the thing to hold onto: two quotes that differ substantially may both be correct for their respective jurisdictions. The scopes are genuinely different.

## How do you confirm your own zone?

Against the published map for your county, using the parcel rather than the mailing address.

Mailing addresses are administrative. A property can carry a city's postal address while sitting outside its limits, and rural addressing follows routes rather than boundaries. For zone purposes what matters is where the land is, which is why the parcel and the map are the right pairing.

Ask the department directly if the parcel sits near the line. That is a legitimate question to put to the authority that draws the map, and it produces an answer you can rely on rather than a contractor's estimate.

Ask your engineer if one is already engaged, since they will need the assignment to do their work and will have obtained it properly.

And record the answer with your project documents. Zone assignment is the kind of fact that gets re-established from scratch at every subsequent project on the property because nobody wrote it down the first time.

Worth doing at the same time, since you are already looking things up: establish whether the parcel sits inside a city or in unincorporated county. That determines which building authority reviews the work, and it is a separate question from the zone with a separate answer. Owners frequently discover the two do not line up the way they assumed, particularly on the fringes of Brownsville and Harlingen where city limits are irregular.

One caution on precedent: do not infer your zone from a neighbouring project, particularly an older one. Maps and standards are revised, assignments can be specific, and a boundary can run between two properties that look identical from the street.

## What does exposure add on top of the zone?

The zone is regional. Exposure is about your particular site, and two properties in the same zone can carry different loads because of it.

The engineering standard that governs design wind speed does not treat every site in a zone identically. It accounts for the surrounding terrain and what stands between open wind and your building. A structure with open water or flat, unobstructed ground upwind experiences wind differently from one surrounded by other buildings and mature trees. The standard has categories for that, and the category assigned changes the loads the design has to resist.

We are not going to publish category values or tell you which one your property is in, because that assignment is part of the engineering determination and depends on conditions in each direction from the building. What is worth understanding as an owner is simply that it exists, because it explains something otherwise puzzling: why an engineer might specify more for your building than for a neighbour's in the same town.

Height and geometry feed in too. Taller buildings see higher wind. Roof shape, overhangs and parapets change how pressure distributes across a surface, and the corners and edges of any roof carry higher uplift than the middle regardless of zone.

The practical consequence is that "what zone am I in" is a necessary question but not a sufficient one. It is the starting point of a determination rather than the whole of it, and on anything larger or more exposed than an ordinary house, the rest of that determination is worth paying an engineer to make properly.

## How does the wind zone interact with the flood requirements?

They overlap most severely in exactly one place, and it happens to be the same place.

South Padre Island carries the county's only Seaward assignment. It is also where the coastal high-hazard flood zones sit, and those bring a rule that reshapes site work entirely: in V and VE zones, participating communities must prohibit the use of fill for structural support of buildings. Elevation is achieved with piles or columns, and enclosures below have to be breakaway construction.

So on the island a project is simultaneously subject to the strictest wind assignment in the county and to a flood regime that forbids the most common method of raising a building. Those two constraints are administered by different authorities, neither of which is checking the other's work.

On the mainland the picture separates. A property can sit in Inland II, well back from the water, and still be in a mapped flood hazard area because of a resaca, a drainage channel or simply low ground. Wind zone and flood zone are independent determinations, and being comfortable on one says nothing about the other.

The failure mode to avoid is assuming a single answer covers both. An owner who has confirmed their wind zone has confirmed one thing. The flood question, the drainage district question and the municipal permit question are all still open, and each has its own authority.

## What does any of this mean for an existing building?

It tells you what standard the building was held to, which is not the same as what would be required today.

Most structures in this county predate the current framework in some respect — an older code edition, an older version of the engineering standard, or a period before the current zone documentation. The legacy zone figures are useful precisely here: they describe what the building was designed against, which helps explain how it has performed and where it is likely to be weakest.

That is genuinely useful when deciding what to do with an older roof. A building designed to an older standard, in a zone with a lower legacy figure, with an original covering approaching the end of its service life, is a different proposition from a recent structure with documented certification.

Re-roofing is the natural moment to improve the situation, because the assembly is open and the incremental cost of a better attachment is small relative to doing the work at all. It is also when the certification question becomes live, since a full replacement is not covered by the small-repair exemption.

What re-roofing does not do is retroactively bring the whole structure to a current standard. The roof covering and its attachment get addressed. The framing, the connections down through the building and the openings do not, unless they are separately scoped. Anyone implying that a new roof upgrades a building's overall wind performance to a modern standard is overselling.

## What should you ask a contractor about your zone?

Four questions, and the answers should be specific.

Which zone is this parcel in, and where did that come from? The acceptable answer names the published map or the department. An answer that reasons from distance to the coast is a guess.

What fastening pattern are you using, and why? In this county the heavier shingle pattern applies everywhere, so a bidder who offers the lighter one as a saving has either not checked or is hoping you will not.

What is the specification at the perimeters and corners compared with the field? Uplift concentrates at edges, and a bid that treats the whole roof uniformly has priced an average rather than a design.

Does this project need an engineer's determination, and if so, when? Getting that answer at bid stage rather than mid-project is the difference between a planned cost and a stoppage.

If a contractor cannot answer those without checking, that is fine and honest — checking is the correct response. What should concern you is confident answers delivered instantly with no reference to any source.

## Where does our authority stop on zone determination?

We do not assign zones, and we do not determine design wind speed.

Zone assignment belongs to the Texas Department of Insurance, published on its maps and in its zone documentation. Design wind speed for a specific structure belongs to a Texas-licensed professional engineer working under the applicable standard. Neither is a contractor's determination, and neither becomes one because a contractor has worked in the area for a long time.

What we do is read the published assignment for your parcel, build to a specification appropriate to it, and coordinate the inspection so the work can be certified. Where a project needs an engineer's determination, we will say so before the specification is written rather than after the materials arrive.

We also will not fill gaps in the published record. Where we could not verify an assignment from a primary source — Willacy County being the clear example — this site says so instead of estimating.
