Most of what goes wrong with windstorm certification is not a construction failure. It is an ordering failure — a form that went in after the tear-off instead of before it, or an inspection requested once the deck was already covered.

The work itself is usually fine. The evidence that it was fine is what gets lost, and evidence is the entire product here.

## What does the filing sequence actually look like?

Application, inspection, certificate. In that order, with the application genuinely first.

The application reaches the Texas Department of Insurance before work begins. That timing is set by statute rather than by convention, and it exists so the project enters the inspection system while the assembly can still be observed.

Inspection then happens at the stage where the things being certified are visible. On a re-roof that means the deck, the fastening pattern, the flashing laps and the attachment details — not the finished surface. An inspector arriving after the covering is complete is being asked to certify something they cannot see.

The certificate issues after the inspection is satisfied. It is a Certificate of Compliance, and it belongs to the structure rather than to the contractor or the current owner.

The reason to be pedantic about this order is that it is not recoverable by paying more later. Filing late does not cost a late fee. It changes which professionals are legally able to certify the work at all, and it moves the job from a routine inspection into an engineering exercise.

A contractor who proposes to "handle the paperwork at the end" has either not done this before or is planning to hand you a problem. Ask when the filing goes in, and expect the answer to be before demolition.

## Which document is which, and what does each one do?

The numbering is opaque, and knowing which form does what makes the whole process legible.

The application is the WPI-1. It is what you or your contractor files with the department before work starts, identifying the structure and the proposed work. Nothing downstream is available without it.

The certificate is the WPI-8. It is the Certificate of Compliance, issued by TDI once the work has been inspected and found to conform. This is the document that TWIA looks for.

The post-construction route has its own pair. Where work has already been closed in, a Texas-licensed professional engineer submits under the WPI-2E process, which can lead to a WPI-8-E certificate. The trailing E marks it as the engineered, after-the-fact version. It certifies the same structure to the same standard, by a different and more expensive method.

And one form that no longer exists in practice: the WPI-3. TWIA stopped accepting it on 31 May 2020. It still appears on Texas roofing company websites presented as live guidance, which is a useful signal about how recently those pages were reviewed and how current the advice on them is likely to be.

Getting the names right matters when you are talking to an insurer, a title company or a buyer's inspector, because those parties will ask for a document by number and will not necessarily explain what they mean.

## Who is permitted to certify, and who is not?

Three parties may certify. A contractor is not among them.

Certification comes from an inspector employed by the department, from a TDI appointed qualified inspector, or from a Texas-licensed professional engineer. That is the complete list, and it is not a matter of interpretation or local practice.

This needs saying directly because self-certification is a confident and common claim in this market. No roofing company in Texas issues its own windstorm certificate. A company that offers to is either describing a process it does not understand or describing a document that will not exist when an insurer asks for it.

What a contractor legitimately does is narrower and still decisive: build to a specification that can pass, file in the right order, and schedule the inspection while inspection remains possible. Most lost certificates are lost at that third point rather than at the first two.

There is a related trap in how work is sold. Because Texas does not license roofing contractors at all, the word "licensed" beside a company name carries no state meaning in this trade. It is worth reading any certification claim from a contractor with that in mind, and asking who specifically will be performing the inspection.

## What exactly is exempt, and what is not?

The exemption is real, narrow, and constantly misquoted.

The exemption reaches repairs below 100 square feet, provided the work leaves decking and framing members untouched. Replace a whole roof and you are outside it.

The unit is square feet. Not roofing squares. A roofing square is 100 square feet, so "100 squares" would describe 10,000 square feet — a factor of one hundred larger than the actual threshold, and comfortably larger than most roofs in the region. That misreading circulates widely and always in the direction that lets somebody skip a step.

The second half of the test matters as much as the area. Even below the threshold, work that touches decking or framing members is outside the exemption. A small repair that turns into deck replacement once it is opened has changed category, and the honest response is to stop and reassess rather than to finish quickly.

There is no dollar-value version of this threshold, despite it being described that way online. The test is area and whether structural elements are involved.

Where a repair sits genuinely near the line, the conservative reading is the cheap one. Treating a borderline job as requiring inspection costs you an inspection. Treating it as exempt when it was not costs you an engineer.

## What does the process cost, and who charges what?

Less than most owners expect from the state, and more than they expect from getting the order wrong.

Department inspectors charge no fee for the inspection itself. If a bid presents a line item as a state inspection fee, that is worth a direct question about what the charge actually covers.

Real costs attach elsewhere. Where an engineer is required — for a post-construction certification, or for a determination specific to your structure — that is professional engineering work billed accordingly. The assembly specified carries its own cost, since a roof built to pass in a high-wind zone uses more fasteners and better details than the minimum. And any corrections identified during inspection have to be made before the certificate issues.

The expensive scenario is the avoidable one. A completed roof with no application on file has to be certified by an engineer working backwards, which means opening finished work, documenting what is underneath, or both. You pay for the investigation, for any remediation it turns up, and for the certification. On a roof that was built correctly the whole time, that is money spent entirely on sequence.

Worth budgeting for separately: if the deck turns out to be in worse condition than assumed, it has to be brought up to standard before the assembly above it can be certified. That is ordinary construction cost rather than a certification cost, but it surfaces during this process and catches owners who budgeted only for the covering. A stated unit rate for deck repair, agreed before the tear-off, keeps it from becoming a dispute.

One further piece of arithmetic worth knowing: once TDI issues a certificate, it may not rescind it. The document is durable in a way that few construction records are, which is part of what makes obtaining it properly worth the discipline.

## What happens if the work is already closed in?

You lose the inexpensive routes and keep one costly one.

Inspectors from the department and appointed qualified inspectors can no longer inspect work that has been closed in. That is not reluctance, it is the limit of what an inspection can establish. Once a covering is on, the fastening pattern, the deck condition and the flashing details are no longer observable.

The remaining path runs through a Texas-licensed professional engineer using the post-construction process. The engineer has to establish, after the fact, what exists beneath a finished surface — through documentation, selective opening, or both — and then certify to it under their own seal.

Owners occasionally ask whether photographs taken during the work can substitute. Sometimes they help, and they are always worth taking, but they are supporting evidence for an engineer rather than a replacement for the engineer. The determination is professional judgement carrying professional liability, and nobody signs that on the strength of a phone gallery alone.

This scenario arises most often after storms, which is precisely when it is hardest to avoid. Urgency pushes crews to start immediately, and the filing is the step that gets deferred. If your roof is damaged badly enough that replacement is likely, raising the certification question in the first conversation is the single highest-value thing you can do.

## What does a certificate actually do for you later?

It travels with the structure, and it gets asked for at moments you do not choose.

For insurance, it is the evidence TWIA looks for. The association's own position is that without a certificate it lacks evidence the structure conforms to applicable building codes, and the structure may be considered uninsurable and ineligible for coverage. Read the hedge in that carefully: the language is "may be considered." It is not a statement that an existing policy terminates the moment a certificate is absent, and any contractor selling a roof on that stronger claim has upgraded the source.

What the hedge actually describes is exposure. You are relying on a documentation gap remaining unexamined, in a region where wind claims are the reason the insurer exists.

At sale, the certificate is a due-diligence item. Buyers, their inspectors and their lenders ask about coastal windstorm status, and an owner who cannot produce documentation is negotiating from a weaker position at exactly the wrong moment.

There is also a code-edition dimension worth recording. Work begun on or after 1 April 2026 is certified to the 2024 IRC and IBC; work between 1 September 2020 and that date was certified to the 2018 editions. Knowing which edition your certificate reflects tells you what standard the structure was actually held to.

Keep the certificate with the property records rather than with the roofing file. It outlives the contractor relationship by decades.

## What does an inspector actually look at?

The attachment, not the appearance.

The question being answered is whether the assembly will stay on the building under load, so the inspection concentrates on the load path. The deck itself: what it is, what condition it is in, and whether it is sound enough to hold fasteners at their rated values. The fastening: type, size, spacing and pattern, and whether penetration depth is achieved. The details where uplift concentrates: perimeters, corners, edge metal, and every penetration through the surface.

Perimeter and corner zones carry meaningfully higher uplift loads than the middle of a roof, which is why fastening patterns tighten there. A roof fastened uniformly at field spacing across its whole area has been installed to an average rather than to the design.

On strip shingles there is a fastening requirement worth knowing in advance, because it applies across this entire county without exception. Code sets a minimum of four fasteners per shingle and raises it to six in high-wind zones at and above a defined threshold, and since the lowest zone assigned anywhere in Cameron County already meets that threshold, the six-fastener pattern governs county-wide. Placement rules ride along with it: each nail has to catch both the overlay and the underlay portions, heads and penetration depths have minimums, and no fastener may land within two inches of a joint or cutout in the shingle beneath.

None of that is visible once the next course is laid. That is precisely why the inspection has to happen during the work, and why photographs taken as you go are worth the two minutes they cost.

## How does this sit alongside your city permit?

As a parallel track that shares a building and nothing else.

The municipal permit and the windstorm certificate answer to different authorities, follow different schedules, and are satisfied by different evidence. Passing a city final inspection produces no certificate. Obtaining a certificate does not satisfy a city.

They can also sit on different editions of the code simultaneously. The certification edition is set at state level and turns over on its own date. A city's adopted edition is set by local ordinance and changes when that city decides to change it. Port Isabel, for example, adopted the 2018 I-codes, so a project permitted there today is reviewed locally against one edition while being certified against a newer one.

When the two produce different requirements, the practical answer is to build to the stricter. Meeting the higher standard satisfies both; meeting the lower satisfies one and fails the other, and the one it fails is the one that matters to your insurer.

Nobody in the permit process is tasked with telling you this. A city inspector is not evaluating your windstorm position, and it is not their job to. The gap between the two tracks is where owners fall, and it is entirely avoidable by treating them as two separate obligations from the first day rather than assuming one covers the other.

## What if you are buying a property here?

Ask about the certificate before you are under contract rather than during the option period.

For any coastal structure, the useful questions are whether a certificate exists, which structure and which work it covers, and what code edition it reflects. A certificate covering a 2015 re-roof tells you about that roof and nothing about the addition built afterwards.

Partial coverage is the common and confusing case. Certificates attach to work, so a property can carry documentation for one roof replacement and nothing for a later repair, an added structure or an enclosed patio. Establishing what is covered is more useful than establishing that something exists.

If no certificate exists and the roof is intact, understand what you are inheriting: the post-construction engineering route is the only path available, and its cost falls to whoever owns the building when the question becomes urgent. That is a negotiable item, and it is much easier to negotiate before closing.

Ask the seller for the permit history as well. Work done without a permit is not automatically defective, but it means the local record will not corroborate what you are told, and it raises the likelihood that windstorm filings were skipped alongside.

None of this requires you to become an expert. It requires you to ask three questions early, at a point where the answers can still change the price.

## Which mistakes actually cost people the certificate?

A short list, and almost none of them are about workmanship.

Starting before filing. The most common by a wide margin, and the only one that cannot be fixed by doing something differently the next day. Once the covering is off and back on, the inexpensive routes have closed.

Assuming the contractor filed. Responsibility for the application should be assigned to a named party in writing before work starts. "I thought they were doing it" is a conversation that happens after the fact, and the department does not resolve it.

Letting a small repair grow. A job scoped under the exemption that turns into decking work has changed category mid-stream. The right response is to pause and re-scope, not to press on because the crew is already there.

Scheduling the inspection for convenience rather than for stage. An inspection has to occur while the assembly is observable, which sometimes means holding a crew for a day. That day is cheaper than an engineer.

Treating storm damage as an exception. Urgency is when this process is most often skipped and least often recoverable, because everyone in the county is calling the same trades in the same week.

Losing the document. A certificate that exists but cannot be produced functions, at a closing table, much like one that was never issued. Store it with the deed and the survey rather than in a folder of receipts.

Relying on a contractor's assurance instead of the document itself. Ask to see the issued certificate at close-out. A company that has genuinely obtained one will hand it over without hesitation.

None of these require technical knowledge to avoid. They require the question being asked at the start, by somebody who knows the sequence is not negotiable.
