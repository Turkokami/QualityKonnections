The first question on any project here is not what you are building. It is who has authority over it, and that answer changes several times inside one county.

Cameron County contains a large number of incorporated cities alongside substantial unincorporated territory. Each city runs its own building department, adopts its own edition of the codes on its own schedule, and sets its own requirements around contractors and inspections. Outside those limits, the county takes the role. Layered across all of it are floodplain administration, drainage districts, and the state windstorm framework, none of which report to your building inspector.

## Which authority actually permits your project?

The one whose jurisdiction the land sits in, which is a question about the parcel rather than about the address.

Mailing addresses are administrative. A property can carry a city's postal address, receive mail through that city's post office, and sit entirely outside its limits. Rural addressing follows routes. On the fringes of Brownsville and Harlingen especially, city limits are irregular in ways that surprise people who have lived there for years.

Inside a city, that city's building department reviews plans, issues permits and inspects the work. Outside, the county handles it, generally with a narrower scope of review than a municipal department.

The practical instruction is to establish jurisdiction before anything else — before design, before bidding, certainly before anyone quotes a price. The county appraisal district record, a plat, or a direct call to the city will settle it in minutes.

It matters because almost everything downstream follows from it: what is required, who inspects, what the fees are, whether contractor registration applies, and what code edition the work is held to. Two identical houses a quarter mile apart can be built under materially different requirements.

## Why does the code edition matter, and why is it not listed here?

Because the edition is a local adoption decision, and because we only publish what we verified.

Cities adopt a version of the International Residential Code and International Building Code by ordinance, and they update on their own timetable. One city may be several editions ahead of its neighbour. That affects real requirements: fastening, connections, energy provisions, and details that change what a project costs.

We can confirm one edition in this county from a primary source. Port Isabel adopted the 2018 I-codes. That is stated here because it is verified.

For Brownsville, for South Padre Island, and for unincorporated Cameron County, we do not publish an adopted edition. The sources we needed were not machine-readable and we could not confirm current adoptions. South Padre Island adopted the 2015 I-codes under an ordinance in 2018, but that may well have been superseded since, so we present it as unconfirmed rather than as current.

That is a real gap and we would rather name it than fill it. An owner or contractor building to the wrong edition is a genuine problem, and a website's guess is not a defensible basis for a specification. Your building department will tell you their current adoption in one phone call.

## How does the permit differ from the windstorm certificate?

Different authority, different purpose, different evidence, and at present frequently a different code edition.

The building permit is a construction authority function. A city or the county reviews your plans against its adopted code, inspects the work, and signs off. It asks whether the structure is built correctly under local law.

The windstorm certificate is an insurance function administered by the state, covered in full on the [windstorm certification](/compliance/windstorm-certification-wpi-8/) page. It exists so a structure can be insured against wind in the designated catastrophe area.

The two do not communicate. Passing your final inspection produces no certificate. Holding a certificate satisfies no city.

The edition mismatch makes this concrete. Certification currently runs on the 2024 codes for work begun on or after 1 April 2026, having run on the 2018 codes for the period before that. A city's adopted edition is set locally. Port Isabel's confirmed 2018 adoption means a project there today is reviewed by the city against one edition and certified by the state against another.

Where the two produce different requirements, build to the stricter. Meeting the higher standard satisfies both. Meeting the lower satisfies one, and the one it fails is the one your insurer cares about.

Nobody in the permit process is responsible for telling you this. It is not the inspector's job, and the gap between the tracks is where owners fall.

## What else needs permitting alongside the building permit?

Frequently more than owners expect, and the applications are separate even when the counter is the same.

A floodplain development permit applies to development in a mapped flood hazard area, and much of this county qualifies. That is a distinct approval with its own criteria, covered on the [flood elevation](/compliance/fema-flood-elevation-and-pad-height/) page.

Drainage district approval governs what your site discharges and how you cross district facilities, described on the [drainage districts](/compliance/drainage-district-and-irrigation-canal-crossings/) page. A district is a separate authority under state water law, and a municipal permit does not speak for it.

Stormwater authorisation applies to larger disturbances, with erosion controls installed and maintained, as set out on the [stormwater](/compliance/stormwater-and-erosion-control/) page.

Utility connections, driveway or access permits where a road authority is involved, and platting where land is being divided all bring their own processes.

The efficient approach is to identify every applicable authority at the start and run them in parallel. Each has its own lead time and several run on meeting schedules. Started together they overlap; discovered one at a time they queue.

## What does a permit application actually require?

Enough detail for somebody to review the work against a code before it is built.

Typically: identification of the property and the applicant, a description of the scope, drawings or plans proportionate to the work, and the fee. On structural work, engineered drawings where the jurisdiction requires them. On anything in a flood hazard area, elevation information. Where a contractor is involved, whatever registration or insurance evidence that jurisdiction requires.

The level of detail scales with the project. A re-roof application, where one is required, is a different document from a new commercial building.

Two things reliably slow applications down. Incomplete submissions, which go back for more information and lose their place. And scope described too vaguely to review, which invites questions that could have been pre-empted.

Two things reliably speed them up. Establishing what the department wants before submitting, which is a phone call. And having the other determinations already in hand — flood zone, required elevation, district position — so the application does not stall waiting on somebody else.

Ask how the department prefers to receive submissions, and in what format. Some accept digital sets, some want paper, and some want both. It is a trivial question that occasionally costs a week.

Ask as well what triggers a requirement for engineered drawings in that jurisdiction. The threshold varies, and discovering it after a submission has been rejected means paying for engineering on a schedule you did not plan for rather than one you did.

## How long does it take?

Long enough that it belongs in the schedule as a real dependency rather than a formality.

Simple residential permits in a well-run department can move quickly. Commercial review takes longer, and anything requiring input from more than one authority takes as long as the slowest of them.

The compounding factor here is the number of parallel tracks. A commercial site might need a building permit, a floodplain determination, district approval for discharge and a crossing, and stormwater authorisation. Sequential handling of that list can consume months. Parallel handling consumes weeks.

Inspections during construction add their own rhythm. Work has to stop at defined points until an inspection happens, and inspection availability is not infinite. A crew that arrives expecting to close something in the same day, on a job where an inspection is required first, loses the day.

The windstorm inspection runs on its own schedule again, and it has to happen while the assembly is open. Coordinating it with the construction sequence is planning work, not paperwork.

Build float into the programme for all of it. Projects here that slip usually slip on approvals rather than on labour.

## Does the county require contractor registration?

It varies by jurisdiction, which is the recurring answer on this page.

Some Texas cities require contractors to register locally, post a bond, or both, before pulling permits. That is a municipal requirement rather than a state licence — Texas issues no state roofing licence at all, as covered on the [contractor rules](/compliance/texas-roofing-contractor-rules/) page.

The practical effect is that a contractor properly registered in one city may not be registered in the next. On a property near a boundary, or for an owner who used the same company on a previous house elsewhere, that is worth confirming rather than assuming.

Ask the department that will review your permit what they require of a contractor, then ask your bidder whether they meet it. Two calls, and they pre-empt a rejected application.

Unincorporated county work generally has no municipal registration requirement, which occasionally gets presented as meaning there are no requirements. That is not the case. The county's processes apply, and the floodplain, drainage and stormwater authorities apply regardless of city limits.

## What happens if work is done without a permit?

The consequences attach to the property, which is why they outlast whoever did the work.

An authority can require work to be exposed for inspection, corrected, or removed. Fees can be higher retroactively. Certificates of occupancy can be withheld on work that requires one.

The larger effect is on the record. Unpermitted work leaves no public trail, so nothing corroborates what was done or to what standard. That surfaces at sale, during a refinance, during an insurance review, or after damage, when somebody asks for documentation and none exists.

It also correlates with other gaps. A project that skipped a permit frequently skipped the windstorm filing too, which is the more expensive of the two to remedy, because certification after close-in requires a licensed engineer.

For buyers, this belongs in due diligence. Ask for the permit history. Unpermitted work is not automatically defective and it is not automatically a reason to walk, but it is a reason to look harder and a legitimate negotiating point.

For owners considering skipping a permit to save time: the time saved is real and the exposure lasts as long as you own the building.

## How does this differ across the county's towns?

Enough that experience in one is a poor guide to another.

The county contains cities of very different sizes and capacities, from Brownsville and Harlingen down to small municipalities with limited departments, alongside South Padre Island with its particular coastal circumstances and substantial unincorporated territory in between.

That produces real variation in adopted editions, in what triggers a permit, in review times, in fees, in contractor requirements and in how much plan detail is expected. A contractor who works across the county carries a mental map of those differences; one who works mainly in a single city may not.

The pattern that matters most for this site's readers: what is required for a re-roof differs across jurisdictions here. So does whether a floodplain permit accompanies a pad. So does whether an owner can pull their own permit.

We have not published a jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction table of these requirements, because compiling one from unverified sources would produce exactly the kind of confident, stale reference this section exists to avoid. The reliable answer is always the department covering your parcel.

What we can say with confidence is which questions to ask, and that list does not change from town to town. The answers change constantly; the questions do not, which is why this page is built around them rather than around a table that would be stale within a year.

## What should you establish before anyone quotes the work?

Six answers, all obtainable by phone, none requiring a professional.

Whether the parcel is inside a city or in unincorporated county.

Which department therefore reviews the work, and what they require for your scope.

What code edition they have currently adopted.

Whether the property is in a mapped flood hazard area, and if so what elevation and freeboard apply.

Whether a drainage district easement touches the tract, and whether your project changes where water leaves it.

Whether contractor registration is required, and whether your bidder holds it.

Gathering that takes a morning and it changes what a bid means. A quote produced without those answers has assumptions inside it, and the assumptions surface later as changes, delays, or a specification built to the wrong standard.

If a replacement roof is in scope, add the windstorm question: who files, and when. That one has no cheap remedy if it is missed.

## Who pulls the permit, you or the contractor?

Whoever the contract says, and it should say.

In most commercial and residential work the contractor pulls it, which is normal and appropriate — they know the department, the process and the submission requirements. Some jurisdictions allow an owner to pull a permit on owner-occupied property, and a few require it in defined circumstances.

The decision carries consequences worth understanding before making it. The permit holder is the party the authority deals with, and responsibility for compliance follows. An owner who pulls a permit to save a fee, on work performed by somebody else, has taken on a position they may not have intended.

There is also a signal in a contractor's preference. A company that resists pulling a permit, or suggests the work does not need one when the department says otherwise, is proposing that you carry a risk on their behalf. That is worth noticing regardless of how the conversation is framed.

Settle it in writing, alongside who is responsible for the windstorm filing where that applies. Both belong in the contract rather than in an assumption, and both are cheap to agree at the start and expensive to argue about later.

## What do inspections during construction actually check?

Progress against the approved plans, at points chosen because they are the last moment something can be seen.

The pattern is consistent even where the details differ. Work stops at defined stages, an inspector confirms what has been built matches what was approved, and the next stage proceeds. Foundations before they are covered. Framing, and the mechanical, electrical and plumbing rough-in, before they are concealed. Final inspection at completion.

The logic is the same as the windstorm process: once something is closed in, it cannot be observed, and reconstructing what is behind a finished surface is expensive. Everything about the inspection calendar follows from that.

Failed inspections are ordinary rather than shameful, and how a contractor handles them is informative. A correction identified and fixed is the process working. A contractor who covers work before an inspection, or who treats a correction as an argument, is telling you something.

For an owner, the useful habits are simple: know which inspections your project requires, know that the schedule depends on them, and do not accept pressure to proceed past a stage that has not been signed off. The saved day is borrowed against a much larger risk.

Keep the inspection records. They form part of the documentation that gets requested at sale or during an insurance review, and they are considerably easier to keep than to recover.

## What does the permit actually buy you?

More than permission, though permission is how it is usually described.

It buys a second set of eyes on the design before it is built, by somebody with no financial interest in the outcome. On a market with no state licensing filter for roofing or earthwork contractors, that independent review is one of the few structural protections an owner has.

It buys a public record. Years later, the permit history is evidence that work was done, when, and to what standard — which is exactly what a buyer, a lender, an insurer or an adjuster asks for, and exactly what unpermitted work cannot produce.

It buys the inspection sequence described above, which catches a category of error that is invisible once the work is closed.

And it buys the ability to transact cleanly. Property with a coherent permit history sells more easily than property with gaps, because the gaps become questions and the questions become discounts.

Set against a fee and some schedule time, that is a reasonable return. The owners who most regret skipping it are rarely the ones who had a problem during construction; they are the ones who tried to sell or claim afterwards.

## Where does our authority stop on permitting?

Short of every determination on this page.

We do not decide which authority has jurisdiction over your parcel, determine what code edition applies, issue permits, perform inspections, make floodplain determinations, or represent you before a board or a department. Those belong to the authorities themselves, and to the engineers and surveyors whose work supports an application.

What we do is establish which jurisdiction a project sits in before scoping it, build to the requirements that jurisdiction and the state framework produce, coordinate inspections so they happen at the right stage, and tell you plainly when an answer belongs to a department rather than to us.

Where we could not confirm a fact from a primary source, this page says so. The adopted code editions for Brownsville, South Padre Island and unincorporated Cameron County are the clearest instance: those are one phone call away for you, and a guess from us would be worth nothing.

This is not legal advice, and the page states the rules only as of the review date shown below. Jurisdiction, adopted code edition, permit requirements and contractor registration should be confirmed with the department covering your property before you rely on them.
