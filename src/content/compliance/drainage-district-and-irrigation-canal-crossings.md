Almost every developable tract in this region drains into something somebody else owns and maintains. That is unusual, and it catches out anyone whose experience comes from elsewhere in Texas.

The consequence is that where your water goes is not purely your decision, and the entity with the final word on it is frequently one most owners have never heard of until the site plan is already drawn.

## Why is there a district between you and the water?

Because the Valley's drainage is engineered infrastructure rather than natural hydrology, and somebody has to run it.

The land here is flat, low and agricultural in origin. Moving water off it, and delivering water to it, required building a grid of canals, laterals, drains and channels over more than a century. That grid does not maintain itself, and it does not respect property lines, so the state created districts with the authority and the funding to operate it.

Under state water law these districts hold genuine rulemaking power over their easements. That is not an advisory role. A district can determine what you may build near its facility, how you may cross it, what you may discharge into it, and on what terms.

The river's own history reinforces the point. Flood control works, including Falcon Dam, severed the Rio Grande's former channels and prevented new ones forming, which is why the resacas around Brownsville are a fixed inherited system rather than a living floodplain. What drains where is a designed outcome, maintained deliberately.

So the district is not an obstacle inserted between you and a natural watercourse. In most cases it is the reason there is anywhere for your water to go.

## Which districts operate here?

Several, with overlapping geography, and a tract can fall under more than one.

Cameron County contains drainage districts numbered 1, 3, 4, 5 and 6. Alongside them run irrigation districts including Bayview, Brownsville, Cameron County numbers 2, 6 and 16, Harlingen, Hidalgo and Cameron Counties number 9, and La Feria.

The distinction between the two types matters. Drainage districts move water off the land. Irrigation districts deliver water to it. Their facilities sit in the same landscape and sometimes in the same corridor, and both carry easements that constrain what you can do near them.

A rural tract can therefore have an irrigation lateral along one boundary and a drainage channel along another, each with its own district, its own rules and its own approval process. Neither speaks for the other.

Beyond the districts sits the regional system. The Arroyo Colorado is an ancient distributary of the Rio Grande running roughly 90 miles from Mission to the Laguna Madre, draining a watershed of about 706 square miles and functioning as an integral part of a major floodway system. Of 105,000 cubic feet per second diverted at Anzalduas, about 21,000 goes to the Arroyo. It is a major piece of flood infrastructure, not a large ditch.

Identifying which districts touch your tract is a first-week question, answerable through the county, a surveyor, or the plat.

## What does a district actually control?

Four things, and each can change a site design.

How much water may leave your site, and at what rate. Districts set an allowable discharge rate for land in their area and a permitted increase for development. Those two figures together determine whether your project needs on-site detention, which is one of the larger cost and layout consequences in site development.

How a connection to their facility is made. The elevation, the method and the protection of any outfall are the district's to specify. This is not a detail left to the contractor.

How a crossing is constructed. Utility lines crossing an easement are subject to requirements on the angle of crossing, the depth below the existing flowline, and how the line is protected.

What may be built near the facility at all, including how much right-of-way must be preserved and what access the district retains for maintenance. A channel that needs to be dredged periodically needs somewhere for the equipment to work from.

The through-line is maintenance. Almost every rule makes more sense when read as protecting the district's ability to keep the system functioning for everyone on it, rather than as an arbitrary constraint on you.

## What is a run-out rate, and why does it decide your site plan?

It is the rate at which the district's system is designed to accept water from an acre of land, and it is the number your engineer designs against.

Undeveloped land absorbs and delays runoff. Roofs, pavement and compacted pads do not. Development therefore increases both the volume and the speed of water leaving a site, and if every tract sent its increase downstream unchecked the system would be overwhelmed by the cumulative effect.

Districts manage that by setting an allowable rate and a permitted increase, usually differentiated by development density. Lower-density residential development on larger lots is typically allowed a smaller increase than higher-density development, reflecting the difference in impervious cover.

Where your projected discharge exceeds what is allowed, the difference has to be held on site and released slowly. That means detention — a pond, an underground system, or storage designed into the grading — and it consumes land, changes the layout, and costs money.

This is why the district conversation belongs at the start. A site plan designed without the discharge limit, then tested against it, frequently has to be redrawn. A site plan designed around it usually works the first time.

## What does crossing an easement involve?

Specific geometry, specific depth, and specific protection.

Districts require utility crossings to run close to perpendicular to the facility rather than at an oblique angle. The reason is practical: a near-perpendicular crossing affects the shortest possible length of channel and leaves the district's maintenance operations workable. An oblique crossing complicates dredging and repair along a long stretch.

Depth is specified relative to the existing flowline of the channel, so a crossing has to pass beneath the channel bottom with a stated clearance rather than merely beneath the ground surface. That is a meaningful difference on a site where the channel is already several feet below grade.

Protection is required as well, typically encasement, so the line survives the maintenance work that will eventually happen above it.

None of this is negotiable at the point of construction. It is designed in, approved in advance, and inspected. A crossing installed to a contractor's judgement and discovered afterwards is a removal-and-reinstallation problem.

## Why are open ditch connections prohibited?

Because they are impossible to maintain and they fail at the junction.

Open ditch connections to existing channels are prohibited outright by district rule. An unengineered open connection erodes at the confluence, undercuts the channel bank, deposits sediment where it is least wanted, and creates a maintenance liability at exactly the point where the district's equipment has to operate.

The alternative is an engineered outfall: a structure of specified size, at a specified elevation, with erosion protection, designed to discharge at the permitted rate without damaging the receiving channel.

This is one of the more common surprises for owners who have watched water leave their land the same way for decades. The historic arrangement may predate any of the current rules, and it does not establish a right to replicate it.

Sediment is the reason the district cares so much. The scale of the maintenance burden here is considerable — the utilities board in Brownsville manages more than 50 miles of the city's historic resaca waterways holding something like four million cubic yards of sediment, under a maintenance plan measured in decades. Every unmanaged connection adds to that.

## What happens if you build first and ask later?

You discover that the district controls the thing your site cannot do without.

The leverage is structural rather than punitive. Your project needs somewhere for water to go, and in most of this region that somewhere is a district facility. A district that has not approved your connection is under no obligation to accept your discharge, and it can require an unapproved connection or crossing to be removed or corrected.

The cost is rarely the fee. It is the rework: regrading a site designed around an assumption that turned out to be wrong, retrofitting detention that was not in the layout, or excavating and reinstalling a crossing that was installed at the wrong angle or depth.

Timing compounds it. District approvals run on meeting schedules rather than on demand, with submissions required a set period in advance. A project that discovers the requirement late does not simply pay for it late; it waits.

There is also a downstream dimension. Sending more water than permitted affects everyone on that system, and the neighbours who experience it have a district to complain to. That is not a comfortable position to occupy in a small market.

## How do the resacas complicate this in Brownsville?

By adding a water body that is simultaneously an amenity, a drainage facility and somebody else's asset.

The resacas are former Rio Grande channels, now a fixed system of roughly 3,500 acres against an estimated 70,000 before agricultural expansion in the 1870s. The Corps of Engineers evaluated 66 of them near Brownsville, ranging from under an acre to over 50 and averaging about 18. Three cross the urban core: Resaca de la Guerra, Resaca del Rancho Viejo and the Town Resaca. Further out sit Resaca de los Fresnos at San Benito, Resaca de los Cuates between Los Fresnos and Bayview, and Resaca de la Palma.

They carry irrigation supply, raw water, stormwater and recreation at the same time, which means several interests attach to the same water. A lot backing onto one is not simply a lot with a view.

For an owner, the practical effects are that alteration of the bank or the adjacent ground is constrained, discharge into the resaca is governed rather than assumed, and maintenance access has to be preserved. The system is actively managed under a long-term sediment plan, and that management requires the ability to reach the water.

## What does this do to your project timeline?

It adds a dependency that runs on somebody else's calendar.

District review is not same-week work. Submissions are typically required a defined number of days before a scheduled meeting, decisions are made at those meetings, and a resubmission after comments waits for the next one. Two rounds of comments can consume a couple of months without anyone behaving unreasonably.

That timeline sits upstream of things owners think of as the start of the project. Grading cannot be finalised until the discharge limit is known. Detention cannot be designed until the increase is calculated. The construction schedule follows from both.

The way to compress it is to front-load rather than to hurry. Identify the districts, request current requirements, and get the engineer working with real numbers before the layout is committed. Most of the delay in these approvals comes from redesign after a first submission built on assumptions.

Where a project also triggers floodplain requirements or stormwater authorisation, those run in parallel and are worth starting together. The [flood elevation](/compliance/fema-flood-elevation-and-pad-height/) page covers the first of those.

## What condition is the receiving water actually in?

Not good, and it explains why discharge quality is scrutinised alongside quantity.

The Arroyo Colorado carries documented impairments. Its tidal segment has a dissolved oxygen impairment, and the segment above it a bacteria impairment. Those are formal designations, and they mean the water body is already failing to meet standards for its designated uses before your project adds anything.

That context changes how a district and a regulator read a new discharge. A system already under strain has less tolerance for additional load, and the scrutiny applied to what leaves a construction site reflects that.

The receiving environment beyond it is unusual too. The Laguna Madre is a hypersaline lagoon of roughly 280,910 acres averaging about four and a half feet deep — shallow, poorly flushed and sensitive to what arrives in it. Groundwater across the lower Valley is mostly brackish, and near the bay margins the ground is ponded, sodic and hypersaline with the water table within a few feet of the surface.

For a construction project the practical consequences are sediment control and timing. Fine sediment from a graded site is the most likely thing to leave it, the clays here stay in suspension a long time, and the receiving waters are exactly the sort that suffer from it. That is the subject of the [stormwater and erosion control](/compliance/stormwater-and-erosion-control/) requirements, which run alongside district approval rather than instead of it.

## What should you ask the district, and when?

Six questions, asked before the layout is committed rather than after.

Which facilities of yours touch this tract, and where exactly do the easements run? Get it in a form your surveyor can work from.

What is the allowable discharge rate for this land, and what increase is permitted for the development I am proposing? Those two figures determine whether detention is needed.

What do you require at the outfall — size, elevation, protection?

What are your requirements for a crossing, if my utilities have to pass your facility?

What are the current fees, what right-of-way do you require, and what is your submission deadline relative to the meeting schedule?

How long does an approval remain valid, and what happens if the project is delayed?

Ask for the document those answers come from, and note which version you were given. That is not pedantry — it is precisely how this page ended up unable to publish figures, because the plan behind the numbers in circulation has been superseded.

If the tract has been developed or platted before, ask whether prior approvals exist. Older approvals sometimes carry conditions that still run with the land.

One more worth raising early, because it is the question owners least expect: what does the district require of the land immediately adjacent to its easement, as opposed to within it? Setbacks, restrictions on fill, limits on planting and requirements to preserve maintenance access frequently extend beyond the easement line itself. A layout that respects the easement boundary exactly, and treats everything outside it as unconstrained, can still fail review.

Establish as well whether the district wants to see the plan before or after the city or county does. Sequencing between authorities varies, and submitting in the wrong order occasionally means doing one of them twice.

## Does any of this apply to a straightforward residential job?

Sometimes, and the trigger is not the size of the building.

A roof replacement does not involve a district. Neither does most work that leaves the ground alone. What brings a district into a residential project is any change to where water goes: regrading a lot, filling to raise a pad, adding a driveway or a slab that increases impervious cover, or trenching a utility line across an easement at the back of the property.

On an individual house, the increase in runoff is usually small enough that detention is not the issue. The issues are the connection and the crossing. If your regrading changes how the lot discharges, or your utility trench crosses a lateral, that is district territory regardless of how modest the project feels.

Rural tracts carry the higher likelihood, because irrigation infrastructure is everywhere in agricultural land and easements frequently cross properties that have long since stopped farming. An owner who bought a five-acre parcel to build one house may well have a lateral running through it and no awareness of the fact until a plat is pulled.

The efficient move on any project that disturbs ground is a single early question: does a district easement touch this tract, and does what I am planning change where water leaves it. Two answers, obtained in a week, and the rest of the project either proceeds normally or gets designed properly from the start.

## Where does our authority stop on district matters?

We build what is approved. We do not decide what gets approved.

We do not set discharge rates, calculate allowable increases, design detention, engineer outfall structures or determine easement boundaries. Those are engineering and surveying determinations, and the requirements they answer to belong to the district. We do not represent you before a district board, and we do not interpret district rules on their behalf.

What we do is construct the grading, drainage shaping, trenching and crossings to the approved design, at the specified elevations and with the specified protection, and tell you early when a district is going to be involved so it lands in the schedule rather than in the middle of it.
