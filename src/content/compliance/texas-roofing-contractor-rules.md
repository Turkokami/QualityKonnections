In most trades, the first question an owner asks is whether the contractor is licensed. In Texas roofing that question has no useful answer, because there is nothing to be licensed by.

That is not a loophole or an oversight to be outraged about. It is simply the structure of the market here, and it shifts a job onto you that a licensing board would otherwise do. This page is about doing that job efficiently.

## What does "no state licence" actually mean?

That the state has not created a licensing programme for roofing, so no roofing company in Texas holds, or can hold, a state roofing licence.

The Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation administers a long list of regulated occupational programmes. Roofing is not one of them. There is no state exam, no state registry to search, no state complaint process specific to roofing, and no licence for a regulator to suspend.

The consequences follow logically. There is no state body verifying competence before someone begins selling roofs. There is no minimum insurance requirement imposed by a licensing statute. And there is no official database where you can confirm standing, which is the tool owners in licensing states reach for first.

What this does not mean is that roofing is unregulated in every respect. Contract law applies. Deceptive trade practices law applies. Insurance regulation applies to anything touching claims. And on this coast the windstorm certification framework applies with real force, administered by the Department of Insurance rather than by a trades regulator.

So the accurate summary is narrow: no occupational licence exists, and the vetting a licence would represent has to be performed by you instead.

## Why does the word "licensed" keep appearing in advertising?

Because it is effective, and because one credential contains the word in its name.

The Roofing Contractors Association of Texas operates a programme whose title uses the word licensed. It is a voluntary industry credential from a trade association, not a state authorisation, and the distinction is easy to lose in a yard sign.

The credential itself is substantive. It has two trade categories, residential and commercial, and two tiers, Principal and Manager. It carries insurance minimums of $300,000 for residential and $500,000 for commercial. Candidates must pass an examination at 70 percent, and holders must complete eight continuing education units each year.

Those are real requirements and a company that meets them has done something. The problem is only in how the name gets used — placed beside a company name without qualification, it reads to a homeowner as a state licence, which it is not.

The only honest way to state it is with the qualification attached: a voluntary certification from the Roofing Contractors Association of Texas, alongside the fact that Texas does not issue a state roofing licence. Any presentation that omits the second half is trading on a misunderstanding.

Where a company claims the credential, you can ask for the certificate and the category and tier it covers. A residential credential does not evidence commercial capability.

## What can a city still require?

Local registration, and it varies.

Cities in Texas retain authority over construction within their limits, and some require contractors to register locally, post a bond, or both, before pulling permits. That is a municipal requirement rather than a state licence, and it applies to work inside those city limits.

This creates a practical trap for owners on the edges of jurisdictions. A contractor properly registered in one city may not be registered in the next, and a property with a city mailing address may sit in unincorporated county where a different authority applies entirely.

The reliable move is to ask the building department that will actually review your permit what they require of a contractor, and then ask your bidder whether they meet it. Two short calls, and they resolve a question that otherwise surfaces when a permit application is rejected.

Unincorporated county work has its own path, usually through the county and, where drainage or floodplain issues arise, through additional authorities. The absence of a city building department does not mean the absence of requirements.

## What should you verify before signing anything?

Five things, all of which produce documents.

Insurance, verified at source. Ask for certificates of insurance sent directly from the agent or insurer rather than forwarded as a file. General liability is the baseline. Workers compensation matters more than most owners realise, because an injury to an uninsured worker on your property can become your problem. Confirm the policy is current, not merely that a certificate exists.

A physical presence. A local address that is not a mailbox, a phone number that is answered, and a business that existed before this month. On this coast, a business that existed before the most recent storm is a meaningfully stronger signal than one that appeared after it.

References with specifics. Not a list of names, but jobs you can locate: what was done, when, and whether the owner would use them again. Work from more than a year ago is more informative than work from last month, because roofing problems take time to appear.

A written scope. Not a price on a page. The system by manufacturer and product, the attachment method, what happens to the deck if it is worse than assumed and at what rate, who is responsible for permits, and who files the windstorm application and when.

Certification responsibility, assigned by name. On this coast this is the single most consequential line in a roofing contract, and its absence is not neutral.

## What does the contract need to say?

Enough that a disagreement six months later has a document to resolve it.

Scope, in the specificity described above. A contract that identifies the work as "replace roof" has not defined the product being purchased.

Price and payment schedule, tied to progress rather than to dates. Modest deposits for material ordering are ordinary. Large payments before material has arrived or work has started are the pattern associated with jobs that stall.

Change order procedure, in writing, with a mechanism for the deck. Deck condition is the most common legitimate reason a roofing price changes, and handling it with a pre-agreed unit rate converts a dispute into arithmetic.

Warranty terms, both of them. The manufacturer warranty covers the product under that manufacturer's conditions and often requires registration. The workmanship warranty is the contractor's own obligation. These are separate documents from separate parties, and a single figure quoted without saying which one it refers to is not a term.

Windstorm filing responsibility and timing, stated explicitly.

Clean-up, disposal, and what happens to your landscaping and driveway. Minor, until it is not.

An unqualified promise of coverage for life should raise your eyebrows rather than your confidence. A durable warranty is defined by its written terms and its exclusions, and any company offering one will hand you the document without being pressed.

## How do you spot an operation that will not be here later?

By looking for permanence rather than for polish.

After a significant storm, this region draws crews from well outside it. Some are competent and some are not, and the difference is not visible from a truck. The useful signals are boring: how long the entity has existed, whether the address resolves to a real place, whether the phone is answered by the company rather than a call centre, and whether they can name jobs in this county from before the storm.

Pressure is the clearest warning. Urgency about signing today, a discount that expires this afternoon, or a request for substantial payment before anything is delivered. Legitimate contractors are busy after storms and do not need you to decide within the hour.

Insurance-claim entanglement deserves particular care. A contractor who offers to handle your claim, to cover your deductible, or to negotiate with your carrier on your behalf is proposing arrangements that range from unwise to unlawful. Document the damage, involve your carrier directly, and keep the roofing contract and the insurance claim as two separate relationships.

Ask who will actually be on the roof. Subcontracting is normal and not a problem in itself, but you should know whether the crew works for the company you signed with, and whether the insurance you verified covers them.

Watch for the estimate that arrives without anyone having been on the roof. On a re-roof, slope, deck condition, penetration count and flashing detail all have to be seen to be priced, and a number produced from a satellite image and a driveway conversation has assumed its way past the variables that matter. Some of those assumptions will be generous to you and some will not, and you will find out which once the work is open.

Be equally cautious about the reverse pattern: an inspection that finds catastrophic damage invisible from the ground, documented only by photographs you cannot independently place. Ask to see the damage yourself where it is safe, or ask for images that clearly show location and context rather than close-ups that could be any roof.

And apply the certification test. Ask when the windstorm application will be filed. A contractor unfamiliar with the sequence, or vague about who does it, is telling you something important about how many coastal jobs they have completed properly.

## What about certification claims specifically?

Treat any claim that a contractor certifies windstorm compliance as disqualifying, because no contractor can.

Certification on this coast comes from an inspector employed by the Department of Insurance, from a TDI appointed qualified inspector, or from a Texas-licensed professional engineer. A roofing company is none of those things, and a company presenting itself as able to issue certification either misunderstands the process or is willing to say things that are not true.

Manufacturer certifications are a different category and are legitimate. Manufacturers run programmes that authorise contractors to install their systems and, in some cases, to offer enhanced warranties. Those are real, verifiable, and worth asking about — with the caveat that they are specific to a manufacturer and a system rather than general endorsements of quality.

Where a manufacturer credential is claimed, the manufacturer can confirm it. That is a reasonable thing to check on a large job.

There is a related distinction worth holding onto when an enhanced manufacturer warranty is being offered as a selling point. Those warranties typically depend on the whole system coming from that manufacturer and being installed by an authorised contractor to their published specification, and they generally require registration after completion. A warranty described verbally at the point of sale and never registered afterwards is not a warranty. Ask who registers it, when, and what document you will receive proving it was done.

Be alert to credential inflation generally: association memberships presented as qualifications, certificates of attendance presented as certifications, and general business registrations presented as trade licensing. Asking what a credential required to obtain usually clarifies quickly.

## Does the absence of licensing change what things cost?

It widens the spread, which is why bids here diverge more than owners expect.

In a licensing state, entry costs something. An exam, a bond, insurance minimums and continuing education all filter who can operate, and that filter compresses the bottom of the market. Remove it and the bottom extends further down, because anyone with a truck and a ladder can quote.

The result is that a low bid in Texas roofing carries less information than a low bid elsewhere. It might reflect genuine efficiency. It might reflect no insurance, no overhead, no intention of being reachable in two years, or a scope that quietly omits the deck, the permit or the certification filing.

That is why the comparison method matters more here than in a licensing state. Put two bids side by side and mark every item one contains that the other does not. Most gaps in price turn out to be gaps in scope, and once they are itemised the remaining difference is usually small enough to judge on other grounds.

It also means the middle of the market is where the useful comparison happens. The highest bid is not automatically the most thorough, and the lowest is not automatically a bargain. What distinguishes them is whether the document describes the work in enough detail to be held to.

## What recourse exists if something goes wrong?

Less convenient recourse than a licensing board, and more than nothing.

There is no state roofing regulator to complain to, so the routes are the general ones. Your contract is the primary instrument, which is the practical argument for having a real one. Texas consumer protection law addresses deceptive practices. Civil claims remain available. Where insurance is involved, the insurance regulator has jurisdiction over carrier conduct, though not over your roofer's workmanship.

Mechanic's lien law is worth understanding from the other direction, because it can affect you even when you have paid. Unpaid subcontractors and suppliers may have lien rights against your property, which is why it is reasonable to ask for lien waivers as payments are made on larger jobs.

The realistic assessment is that recourse after the fact is slow, costly and uncertain regardless of who is right. That asymmetry is the reason this page is weighted toward vetting rather than toward remedies. An hour of checking beforehand is worth considerably more than a strong legal position afterwards.

One practical protection costs nothing: photograph the work yourself while it is open. A dozen images of the bare deck, the underlayment and the fastening pattern, taken from ground level or a window, give you a record nobody can dispute later. They are also genuinely useful to an engineer if certification ever has to be established after the fact.

Keep a paper trail as you go: the contract, change orders in writing, photographs during the work, and the close-out documents. If a dispute does arise, the party with contemporaneous records is in a materially better position than the party with recollections.

## Is dirt work regulated any differently?

Similarly at contractor level, and quite differently where engineering is involved.

There is no state licence for excavation, grading or site preparation contractors either, so the same vetting logic applies: insurance verified at source, real presence, references, written scope.

What is regulated is the engineering. Foundation design, geotechnical recommendations and any determination requiring a professional seal are the province of a licensed professional engineer, and that licensure is real and checkable. Texas engineering practice is explicit that foundations are not to be supported by non-engineered fill, which means on any structural pad there is a licensed professional somewhere in the chain whose credentials you can verify.

Authorities add another layer that functions as de facto vetting. Drainage districts control what may be built near and discharged into their facilities. Floodplain administration governs elevation. Larger disturbances trigger stormwater authorisation with erosion controls that have to be maintained and can be inspected. A contractor who does not know which of these apply to your site is revealing their experience level before any dirt moves.

So the useful dirt-work question is not "are you licensed" but "who is the engineer, which districts have jurisdiction here, and what approvals are we waiting on." Confident answers to those correlate strongly with competence.

## What should a good contractor tell you without being asked?

The things that reduce their own scope, which is exactly why it is a signal.

That a repair will do, when it will. That part of what you asked for is unnecessary. That a question belongs to an engineer or a building department rather than to them. That the cheaper system is the right one for your building, where it is. That the timeline you want is not achievable and why.

They should also volunteer the sequence: when the filing goes in, when inspection happens, what happens if the deck is worse than expected, and what you will be holding at the end.

None of that is generosity. It is what competence sounds like when it is not being managed for effect, and in a market with no licensing filter it is one of the few reliable signals available to you.
