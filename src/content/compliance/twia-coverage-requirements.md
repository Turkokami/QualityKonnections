Insurance is where the windstorm rules stop being paperwork and start being money. Everything about filing sequence, inspection and certificates exists because of one downstream question: whether a structure can be insured against the thing most likely to damage it here.

This page is about that question from the owner's side — what the association looks for, what a gap actually costs, and when in a property's life it is cheapest to fix.

## What is TWIA, and why does it exist?

It is the state-created insurer of last resort for wind and hail damage along the Texas coast.

Coastal wind risk is concentrated and correlated. A single storm can damage tens of thousands of structures at once, which is a poor fit for ordinary insurance economics. Private carriers respond by limiting or declining wind and hail coverage in exposed areas, and that leaves owners who cannot buy protection for the one peril that most threatens them.

The association exists to fill that gap, operating in the designated catastrophe area under its own statutory framework. Cameron and Willacy counties sit inside that area. Hidalgo County does not.

Being an insurer of last resort shapes how it behaves. It is not competing for your business, and it is not in a position to absorb structures it has no evidence about. That is why documentation carries more weight here than it would with a carrier that had priced your risk individually.

The practical consequence for a property owner is that the association's requirements are not negotiable in the way a private carrier's underwriting sometimes is. The evidence is either there or it is not.

## What does the association actually require before it will insure a roof?

Evidence that the structure conforms to the applicable building code — which, for most work in the catastrophe area, means the certificate produced by the inspection process.

That framing is worth holding precisely. The requirement is about evidence rather than about quality. A roof can be built impeccably and still fail this test, because the test asks what can be demonstrated rather than what is true. The certificate is the demonstration.

Which is why the sequence covered on the [windstorm certification](/compliance/windstorm-certification-wpi-8/) page matters so much here. The application goes in before work begins, the inspection happens while the assembly is open, and the certificate issues on that basis. Skip the order and the evidence has to be reconstructed by an engineer at your cost.

One document to strike from your mental list: the WPI-3 has not been accepted since 31 May 2020. It continues to circulate on Texas roofing websites as though it were current, which tells you something about how stale that advice is.

The association's position also explains why contractors cannot self-certify. Evidence supplied by the party who did the work, about their own work, is not independent evidence. The certification comes from the department, an appointed qualified inspector, or a licensed professional engineer, and that independence is the point.

## What does "may be considered uninsurable" really mean?

Less than the sales pitch, and more than nothing.

The association's own language is that without a certificate it lacks evidence that the structure conforms to applicable building codes, and that the structure may be considered uninsurable and ineligible for coverage.

Read the hedge carefully, because it is doing real work. The phrasing is conditional. It is not a statement that an existing policy terminates the moment a certificate is missing, and a contractor who tells you your insurance will be cancelled has upgraded the source to close a sale. We are not going to do that, because the accurate version is persuasive enough.

What the language actually describes is exposure to a determination that has not been made yet. You are relying on a documentation gap remaining unexamined, in a programme that exists specifically because wind claims here are frequent and large.

The practical framing is optionality rather than catastrophe. A certificate obtained in the right sequence is comparatively cheap. Reconstructed afterwards it is expensive. Absent entirely, it becomes somebody's problem at the least convenient possible moment, and the person holding the building at that moment is the one who pays.

There is also a surcharge question. The association has stated that a surcharge applies to certain uncertified residences built within a defined period. We could not locate the underlying rate rule from a primary source, so we do not publish a percentage here. Ask your agent for the current figure rather than trusting one you read anywhere, including from a roofer.

## How does a certificate travel with the property?

It attaches to the structure and the work, not to the owner or the contractor.

That is a genuinely useful property. A certificate obtained today still describes the building in fifteen years, under a different owner, long after the company that installed the roof has changed hands or ceased trading. Once the department issues one, it may not rescind it.

The limit is that it describes the work it covered. A certificate for a 2019 roof replacement says nothing about an addition built in 2023, an enclosed patio, a detached garage, or a later repair that went beyond the exemption. Coverage documentation is per structure and per project.

That is why "does the property have a certificate" is the wrong question and "what does the certificate cover" is the right one. Partial documentation is the normal state of affairs on older properties, and knowing which parts are documented tells you where the exposure sits.

Store it accordingly. A certificate filed with roofing receipts tends to disappear at the point of sale; one filed with the deed, the survey and the title paperwork tends to survive.

## What happens to your position when you re-roof?

It is the moment the question becomes live, and the moment it is cheapest to resolve.

A full roof replacement is not covered by the small-repair exemption, so it brings the certification process into play regardless of what the building's history looks like. That is inconvenient framing for a homeowner who simply wanted a new roof, and it is also the opportunity.

An older structure that has never carried certification can come out of a re-roof with documentation for the most exposure-critical component of the building. The incremental cost of doing it in the correct order, at a moment when the roof is coming off anyway, is far smaller than the cost of establishing the same thing later.

The failure mode is the rushed replacement. Storm season produces urgency, urgency produces crews starting immediately, and the filing is the step that gets deferred. Deferring it converts a routine inspection into an after-the-fact engineering exercise, and it happens most often to the owners least able to absorb it.

So the practical rule is simple: if a replacement is on the table, raise certification in the first conversation, not the last. Ask who files, and when. A contractor who cannot answer that clearly is telling you how many coastal jobs they have completed properly.

## What about older buildings that never had one?

They are common, and their position is stable until something disturbs it.

Much of the housing stock in this region predates the current framework or was built without documentation surviving. Nothing about that changes on its own. Owners live in those houses for decades without the question arising.

What disturbs it is one of three events: a re-roof, a sale, or a claim. Each of those brings somebody who wants evidence, and each of them arrives on a timeline you do not fully control.

Of the three, the re-roof is the only one you choose. That makes it the sensible place to resolve the gap, and it argues for treating a planned replacement as a documentation project as well as a construction one.

If the roof is sound and a replacement is years away, the post-construction engineering route exists but is rarely worth commissioning speculatively. What is worth doing now is establishing what documentation the property actually holds, so you are not discovering it during a transaction. That is an afternoon of looking through files, not an expense.

## How does this affect buying or selling?

It becomes a due-diligence item, and it moves money.

A buyer of coastal property, or their inspector or lender, will ask about windstorm documentation. A seller who can produce a certificate covering recent roof work is answering a question that otherwise becomes a discount. A seller who cannot is negotiating from the weaker side.

For buyers, the questions are narrow and worth asking before you are under contract: does a certificate exist, what structure and what work does it cover, and what code edition does it reflect. A certificate covering only part of the property is normal and needs to be understood rather than treated as a pass.

Where nothing exists and the roof is intact, understand what you are taking on. The engineering route is the only one available, and its cost lands on whoever owns the building when the question becomes urgent. That is negotiable before closing and not afterwards.

Ask for the permit history as well. Unpermitted work is not automatically defective, but it means the public record will not corroborate what you are told, and it raises the odds that windstorm filings were skipped alongside.

## What will you be asked for after a storm?

Documentation you either gathered beforehand or did not.

Storm claims are handled at volume, under time pressure, by people who were not there before the event. What they can evaluate is evidence: what the building was, what condition it was in, what was done to it and when.

The most valuable items are the ones that cost nothing and have to exist in advance. Dated photographs of the undamaged building. Records of previous roof work, including what system was installed. The certificate, where one exists. Any inspection or condition report predating the event.

After the event, add to it immediately: dated photographs of the damage before anything is disturbed, a record of temporary protection and when it was done, and the details of anyone who worked on the property.

The reason this matters here specifically is what regional storms actually do. Across the major events on record, the documented pattern is loss of roof coverings on lower-quality construction rather than structural collapse. That means a lot of storm-damaged roofs genuinely need work, and it also means arguments about pre-existing condition are common. The owner with contemporaneous records is in a materially stronger position than the owner with recollections.

## What does none of this cover?

Several things owners assume are included.

Certification is not a warranty. It records that work was inspected and found to conform; it says nothing about how long the roof will last and creates no obligation on anyone if it fails early. Your warranty position comes from the manufacturer's document and the contractor's, which are separate instruments.

It is not flood coverage. Wind and water are different perils with different insurers and different rules, and the flood question runs through a completely separate framework covered on the [flood elevation](/compliance/fema-flood-elevation-and-pad-height/) page. A structure can be fully certified for windstorm and entirely exposed on flood.

It is not a maintenance record. A certificate from 2015 does not describe the roof's current condition, and no insurer treats it as though it does.

And it is not a substitute for reading your own policy. Deductibles for wind and hail are frequently structured differently from other perils, and the difference is not small on a coastal property. That is a conversation with your agent, not with a roofer.

## What should you ask your agent?

Five questions, and none of them require you to understand insurance.

Whether your current policy covers wind and hail at all, and through whom. Some coastal owners assume they are covered and are not.

What your wind and hail deductible is, in actual dollars on your structure rather than as a percentage in the abstract.

Whether your property's certification status affects your rating today, and whether it would change anything if documentation were obtained.

What the carrier will want after a storm, so you can assemble it now rather than then.

And whether anything about your property — an addition, a converted space, a detached structure — sits outside what is currently documented.

Agents answer these routinely. The reason to ask before a re-roof rather than after is that the answers sometimes change what you should specify, and specification decisions are cheap to make in advance and expensive to revisit.

## Why is the programme as strict as it is?

Because the losses it exists to absorb are genuinely large, and they arrive all at once.

The recent record makes the case without any embellishment. Dolly in 2008 produced around $1.05 billion in damage, with the National Hurricane Center's final report describing mainly moderate structural damage, primarily to roofs, on South Padre Island. Hanna in 2020 ran to roughly $1.2 billion, with a peak gust of 103 miles per hour recorded at Rincon del San Jose, more than 250,000 customers losing power, and hundreds of poorly built structures suffering roof damage.

Neither was a major hurricane at landfall. Both were Category 1 events at the coast, and both produced billion-dollar loss totals. That is the shape of the risk here: not rare catastrophes, but frequent moderate events hitting a large number of vulnerable roofs simultaneously.

Hail adds to it inland. The 2012 mid-Valley storm damaged more than a thousand structures and ran to roughly $600 million insured, and regional wind and hail losses since then exceed $750 million.

Set against those numbers, a requirement that a structure demonstrate code compliance before being insured stops looking bureaucratic. The programme is pricing a correlated risk across an entire coastline, and evidence about individual buildings is most of what it has to work with.

It also explains why the strictness falls where it does. The documented failure mode is the roof covering, so that is where the certification framework concentrates.

## How does the county line change your options?

Sharply, and it is worth knowing which side you are on before you shop for coverage.

Cameron and Willacy counties are inside the designated catastrophe area. Property there sits within the association's territory, and the certification framework applies with all the consequences described above.

Hidalgo County is not inside it. It is second-tier, which sounds adjacent and is a materially different position. The windstorm certification framework does not extend there, and wind coverage works through ordinary channels instead.

The result is a hard discontinuity across a line on a map. Two comparable houses, built in the same year by the same builder, can face entirely different insurance processes depending on which county they landed in. An owner in San Benito and an owner in Weslaco are not having the same conversation with their agents.

For anyone comparing contractor bids across that line, this is the thing to hold onto: the scopes genuinely differ. A quote that includes filing, inspection coordination and certification is pricing work that a quote on the other side of the county line has no reason to include.

Willacy County deserves one caveat. It is confirmed inside the catastrophe area, but we do not publish its per-town wind zone assignments because we could not verify them against the primary map. The certification framework applies; the specific zone is a question for the department.

## What actually improves your position?

Four things, roughly in order of how much they return for the effort.

Get the sequence right on any replacement. Filing before work begins is the single highest-value action available, it costs nothing extra at the time, and it is irreversible if missed.

Establish what documentation the property already holds. An afternoon with the files tells you whether you have a gap, which parts of the structure it covers, and whether it is worth addressing at the next re-roof. Most owners have never checked.

Build above the minimum where the increment is small. The roof being open is the cheapest moment in a building's life to improve the attachment, and on this coast attachment is what fails. That is a specification conversation to have before the work is scoped, not a change order during it.

Keep a dated photographic record. Of the undamaged building now, and of the work while it is open. It costs nothing, it cannot be created retroactively, and it is useful to an engineer, an adjuster and a buyer alike.

None of that requires expertise. It requires the questions being asked early, which is the theme running through every page in this section.

## Where does our authority stop on insurance questions?

Firmly short of anything to do with your policy or your claim.

We do not adjust claims, negotiate with carriers on your behalf, determine coverage, advise on policy selection, or offer to absorb, waive or discount a deductible. Some of those are outside our competence and some are arrangements no reputable contractor should be proposing. If a roofing company offers to handle your claim, treat it as a signal rather than a service.

We do not issue windstorm certificates or perform inspections, for the reasons set out on the [windstorm certification](/compliance/windstorm-certification-wpi-8/) page. Certification comes from the department, an appointed qualified inspector, or a licensed professional engineer.

What we do is build to a specification capable of passing inspection, sequence the filings so the inspection can happen, and document the work thoroughly enough that you have something to hand an adjuster later. Then we stay out of the relationship between you and your carrier, which is where a contractor belongs.

Where a figure could not be confirmed from a primary source — the surcharge rate being the clear example on this page — we say so instead of publishing a number that sounds authoritative.
