Metal is the right answer for a great many buildings on this coast, and it is sold badly here more often than it is sold well. The category gets treated as a single product when it is really several, and the decision that matters most is invisible from the ground: what the panel is made of, not what shape it is pressed into.

Get the substrate wrong within sight of saltwater and you have bought a roof with no material warranty behind it. Get it right and you have bought something that can outlast the mortgage.

## What does a metal roof actually consist of?

Four layers, and the panel is only the one you can see.

The deck comes first. It is what everything fastens to, and on a re-roof its condition frequently determines what the assembly can achieve. Fastener holding values assume sound material, and a deck that has been wet for years does not deliver them.

Underlayment sits on the deck. On a coastal roof this is not an afterthought. It is the secondary water barrier that does the work when wind drives rain sideways under a panel lap, and high-temperature products matter under metal because the assembly runs hot.

The attachment layer is next: clips on a standing seam system, or fasteners driven through the panel face on an exposed-fastener system. This is where wind performance actually lives.

Then the panel, which has a metallic substrate and a coating. Two distinct things that get conflated constantly. The substrate is the metal itself and governs corrosion behaviour. The coating is the finish and governs colour retention, chalk and fade. A panel can carry an excellent coating on a substrate that is wrong for the location, and the coating warranty will not save it.

Trim, flashings and closures finish the job, and they are where most leaks begin.

## Which system suits which building?

Standing seam and exposed-fastener panel solve different problems, and neither is a premium version of the other.

Standing seam has no exposed fasteners in the field. Panels attach with concealed clips and the seams are mechanically locked or snapped together. Because the clips allow movement, the panel expands and contracts without fighting its own fixings. It suits residential roofs, commercial buildings, long panel runs, and anywhere appearance carries weight.

Exposed-fastener panel, commonly R-panel here, is screwed through the face into structure or purlins, with a washer sealing each penetration. It installs faster, costs considerably less, and is entirely appropriate on agricultural buildings, barns, workshops, structural applications and simpler residential roofs at adequate slope.

The honest position, which is not always the profitable one: R-panel is the correct specification for a lot of buildings in this region, and pushing an owner toward standing seam on a barn is selling rather than specifying.

What R-panel asks in return is attention. Because it is pinned rather than floating, restrained thermal movement works against the fasteners over time, and those roofs want inspection on the order of twice a year. An owner who will not do that should buy the system that does not need it.

## What slope can each system go down to?

Code sets the floor, and for metal it is lower than most people assume.

Standing seam runs down to a quarter of an inch per foot. Metal panels with lapped, non-soldered seams and no sealant need three inches per foot. Those same lapped-seam panels with sealant in the laps can go to half an inch per foot.

Those are minimums drawn from the code, not recommendations. Below a system's minimum you are outside its own installation instructions, which is the point at which warranty conversations stop being productive.

The practical consequence here is that standing seam opens up low-slope residential and light commercial roofs that shingles cannot serve at all, since asphalt shingles need at least two inches per foot and require double underlayment through the shallow part of their range.

Slope also interacts with drainage in a way that matters for metal specifically. Ponding is an absolute exclusion on Galvalume substrate warranties. Not a caveat, an exclusion. A low-slope metal roof that does not drain decisively is a warranty problem as well as a leak problem.

## Which substrate actually survives this coast?

Aluminium, near the water. This is where most local advice has it backwards.

Galvalume is a coated steel, an aluminium-zinc alloy coating over a steel core, and it is an excellent, cost-effective product inland. Within 1,500 feet of a saltwater environment it carries no substrate warranty at all. Manufacturer guidance is blunt that neither Galvalume nor galvanized steel will stand up to the conditions associated with a coastal environment.

Aluminium is the oceanfront and bayfront substrate. It holds long-term coastal paint warranties, commonly 25 years, in exposures where the steel-based products hold nothing.

The reason is electrochemical rather than commercial. Aluminium forms a stable passive oxide layer that tolerates chloride exposure far better than zinc-based coatings over steel, and once a steel-cored panel is compromised at a cut edge or a scratch, the exposed steel becomes the problem.

So the specification question on any coastal metal roof is not metal against shingle, and not standing seam against R-panel. It is what this panel is made of and how far it sits from saltwater. A bid naming a profile and a colour but not a substrate has not answered the question that decides the roof's life.

## Why is 1,500 feet the number that matters?

Because it is the figure written into the actual warranty documents, and nearly everything published about it online has drifted.

McElroy's Kynar 500 paint finish warranty is void for material installed on property 1,500 feet or fewer from a salt water environment. Galvalume substrate warranties exclude goods installed within 1,500 feet of a salt-water or other marine environment. Other manufacturers apply the same rule, and the Metal Construction Association's definition of a highly corrosive environment includes coastal marine exposure within 1,500 feet.

Feet. Not miles. You will find claims about five miles from the coast and similar across the internet, and they appear to be drift from this figure rather than any manufacturer's actual term.

Getting the unit right matters in both directions. A property well inland of the barrier island is often outside the exclusion and can use a steel-based product with its warranty intact, which saves real money. And a bayfront or oceanfront property is unambiguously inside it, with no room for optimistic interpretation.

Measure against the actual water, including bay and channel rather than only the Gulf. On South Padre Island, in Port Isabel, along the Laguna Madre shore and out toward Boca Chica, the answer is rarely in doubt.

## What does aluminium cost you in trade-offs?

Three things, and an honest bid says so up front.

It moves more. Aluminium expands roughly twice as much as steel for the same temperature change, which on longer panel runs makes expansion clips a design requirement rather than a refinement. On a hot roof in this climate that movement is real and daily.

It dents more easily. Hail, dropped tools, ladders and foot traffic mark aluminium more readily than steel. On a roof that will be walked for equipment servicing, that is worth weighing.

It costs more, in the region of 15 to 25 percent above the steel-based alternatives.

Set against those, inside the exclusion zone there is no competing option with a substrate warranty behind it. The trade-offs are the price of having coverage at all, not a matter of preference.

Outside 1,500 feet the calculation genuinely changes and Galvalume becomes a sensible, well-warranted choice. Which is why the distance question comes before the material conversation rather than after it.

## Why do the fasteners decide the outcome?

Because a panel that outlives its fixings has not outlived anything.

The rule is to match the fastener to the substrate rather than to habit. For aluminium panels in coastal exposure the answer is 300-series stainless, typically 304. That is the specification, and it is not what most suppliers stock by default.

The 410 stainless commonly carried is explicitly not coastal-rated, with manufacturers listing it for urban, rural and industrial exposure. Electroplated zinc screws are not exterior fasteners at all. Galvanized fasteners against aluminium cladding are not recommended, because pairing dissimilar metals in a wet, salty environment is how galvanic corrosion starts. Where organic-coated steel screws are used, the benchmark is no red rust after 500 hours of salt-spray testing.

There is a genuine counter-example worth knowing, because it stops this becoming a slogan: at least one manufacturer of steel roofing systems discourages stainless fasteners with its own product. Always stainless is not a universal rule. Match the fastener to the system in front of you.

For an owner comparing bids this is among the most revealing questions available. What fastener grade, and why that one for this panel at this distance from the water. A bidder who has thought about it answers immediately. A bidder who has not will talk about the panel instead.

## What actually corrodes first, and why?

The parts the rain never reaches, which is the opposite of what most people expect.

Airborne chlorides are hygroscopic. They attract and hold moisture, so a salt-contaminated surface stays electrolytically active long after the weather has cleared. Corrosion therefore tracks deposition and wash-off rather than distance alone.

The consequence on a real building is that the open field of the roof, which rain scours regularly, frequently outlasts the sheltered areas. Undersides of overhangs, eave returns, the lee side of equipment curbs, anywhere in the shadow of a parapet. Salt accumulates there and never rinses.

That reframes maintenance. Fresh-water rinsing of sheltered metal is a legitimate maintenance item on a coastal roof rather than an upsell, and it is cheap compared with what it prevents.

It also affects detailing. Trim, closures and flashings in sheltered positions deserve the same substrate discipline as the panel, and pairing a correctly specified aluminium panel with incorrectly specified trim is a common and avoidable error.

Cut edges matter too. Field-cutting exposes the substrate at the cut, so where and how panels are cut and handled on site affects how the roof ages.

## How does a metal roof handle thermal movement?

By floating, if it is standing seam, and by resisting, if it is not.

Standing seam panels attach with clips that let the panel slide as it heats and cools. Long runs, dark colours and a South Texas summer produce meaningful daily movement, and the clip system accommodates it without stressing the fixings.

Exposed-fastener panels are pinned through the face. That restrains the movement, and restrained load has to go somewhere. Over years it works fasteners loose, which is why backed-out screws are the classic finding on an older R-panel roof.

This is not a defect in exposed-fastener systems. It is a characteristic, and it converts directly into a maintenance obligation that should be disclosed at the point of sale rather than discovered in year six.

Aluminium raises the stakes because of its greater movement, which is why expansion clips beyond roughly 15 to 20 feet of run become a requirement on aluminium standing seam.

Oil canning, meaning visible waviness in the flat of a panel, is related and largely cosmetic. It is a function of panel width, the flatness of what sits beneath, and how the panel is handled and fixed. Worth understanding before it is discovered, because it is a common source of disappointment on an otherwise sound roof.

## How long does a standing seam roof actually last?

Long, and the useful evidence names what fails first.

A field study by the Metal Construction Association examined 14 low-slope unpainted aluminium-zinc alloy coated standing seam roofs, some in service up to 35 years, and projected service life in excess of 60 years. The limiting component it identified was the butyl seam sealant, at around that same mark.

That is more useful than a marketing lifespan, because it tells you what to watch and what will eventually need attention. It also carries a caveat: those were unpainted low-slope roofs in a study, not a promise about your building in a chloride-rich atmosphere.

We are not going to publish a side-by-side table of expected years for standing seam against R-panel against shingle. Those tables circulate widely and none of them traces to research. They are contractor marketing, and the variables that actually drive service life here, meaning substrate, distance from saltwater, fastener grade and maintenance, swamp the category difference entirely.

What can be said honestly is that a correctly specified metal roof on this coast is a long-horizon asset, that fasteners and sealants are the consumable parts, and that maintenance materially changes the outcome.

## What do the uplift ratings mean?

Four different tests, quoted selectively in bids, so it pays to know which is which.

UL 580 is pass or fail against a threshold. It tells you a system cleared a bar and nothing about margin.

UL 1897 loads to failure, which is more informative, but it excludes the deck from the assembly. On a re-roof, where the existing deck is frequently the limiting element, that is a meaningful omission.

FM 4471 ratings already incorporate a safety factor of two, so the headline figure and the allowable load differ by half. A 1-90 rating corresponds to 45 pounds per square foot allowable, and quoting the headline as though it were the working load overstates the system twofold.

ASTM E1592 tests the complete assembly to failure including anchors and panel deflection, and is generally regarded as the most reliable basis for designing standing seam panels.

The single most useful question about any quoted rating is which standard produced it and whether the deck was included. That one question moves a conversation from marketing to engineering.

## How does the job run, start to finish?

In a fixed order, and the paperwork comes first.

Assessment establishes slope, existing condition, deck access, distance from saltwater and what the building actually needs, with measurements taken for panel runs and trim.

Filing follows, before anything is removed. On a full replacement inside the catastrophe area the windstorm application goes to the department first. Permits are confirmed with whichever authority has jurisdiction, which the [Cameron County permits](/compliance/cameron-county-permits/) page covers.

Material is ordered to the specification, with substrate, gauge, coating, fastener grade and trim confirmed in writing. Panels for longer runs may be roll-formed, which affects lead time and site logistics.

Tear-off exposes the deck. This is when deck condition becomes known, which is why an agreed unit rate for deck repair belongs in the contract rather than in a later conversation.

Dry-in happens the same day the deck is opened, sized so nothing sits exposed overnight in a region where storms arrive quickly.

Then panels, clips or fasteners, followed by trim, flashings, closures and penetration details. The inspection has to happen while the assembly is still observable, so it is coordinated into this sequence rather than after it.

Close-out delivers the documents: the certificate where applicable, permit records, manufacturer paperwork for what was actually installed, and warranty registration completed rather than assumed.

## What moves the number on a metal roof?

Six things, and none of them is a number we will publish without seeing the roof.

Substrate and gauge, the largest single material variable, driven by distance from saltwater.

System type, since standing seam with concealed clips costs materially more than exposed-fastener panel in both material and labour.

Roof complexity. Hips, valleys, dormers, curbs, penetrations and changes of plane all multiply trim and flashing work, which is where metal roofing labour actually concentrates. A simple gable and a cut-up roof of the same area are not the same job.

Deck condition, unknown until tear-off, which is why the unit rate matters.

Access and panel length, because long panels need somewhere to be handled and lifted.

And the compliance track, meaning filing, inspection coordination and the specification needed to pass it.

Competing on the lowest number is not what this business does. What it commits to is a scope written specifically enough that the figure at the end matches the figure at the start.

## Which metal roof failures are avoidable?

Five failure modes, all specified out rather than worked around.

Wrong substrate for the exposure, invisible on day one and terminal by year seven. Prevented by measuring the distance to saltwater and specifying accordingly.

Wrong fastener grade, or dissimilar metals paired without thought. Prevented by matching fastener to substrate and to the system's own instructions.

Movement not accommodated, meaning too few expansion clips on a long aluminium run, or an exposed-fastener system sold to an owner who will never inspect it.

Detail failures at penetrations, curbs, valleys and transitions, which is where the large majority of metal roof leaks begin rather than in the field of the panel.

And sequence failures, where the roof is closed in before inspection, converting a routine certification into an engineered one after the fact.

## What will you be asked to provide?

Less than you might expect, and it speeds everything up.

The address, and whether the property sits inside a city or in unincorporated county, since that decides the permitting authority.

Roughly how far you are from open water, including bay and channel. If you are unsure we will establish it, but your answer is a useful starting point.

Any documentation you hold on the existing roof: when it went on, who installed it, any previous repairs, and any windstorm certificate.

Access constraints, anything on the property that cannot be disturbed, and whether the building has to keep operating during the work.

And a view on how long you intend to hold the property, because it legitimately changes what is worth specifying.

## When is metal the wrong answer?

More often than a metal roofing page usually admits.

When the slope falls below the system's minimum and cannot be corrected.

When the budget genuinely does not reach a correctly specified coastal metal assembly. In that case a properly installed shingle roof at the right fastening pattern beats a cheap metal roof on the wrong substrate, comfortably.

When the building is low-slope commercial carrying heavy equipment traffic, where a membrane system is usually the better fit.

When the existing roof needs a repair rather than a replacement. A localised failure on a sound assembly is a repair, and routing it into a full re-roof is behaviour this market already has too much of.

And when the owner will not maintain an exposed-fastener system they were sold on price.
