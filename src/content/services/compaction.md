Compaction is the step where a site either becomes buildable ground or becomes a problem with a slab on top of it. It is also the step most easily skimped, because a poorly compacted pad and a properly compacted one look identical the day the equipment leaves.

The difference appears later, unevenly, in the part of the building that happens to sit over the loose material.

## What is compaction actually doing?

Removing air, not water, and increasing the density of placed material so it behaves predictably under load.

Soil as delivered or excavated contains voids. Under the weight of a structure those voids close over time, and the surface above them settles. If that settlement were uniform across a footprint it would be tolerable; it never is, because the material and its placement vary across a site. Differential settlement is what cracks slabs and walls.

Compaction closes those voids in a controlled way before the structure is built, so the settlement happens under a roller in a week rather than under a house over five years.

It is not the same as consolidation, which is the slow expulsion of water from saturated fine soils under sustained load, and it is not the same as stabilisation, which changes the material's properties chemically. Those distinctions matter because contractors sometimes use the words interchangeably and they describe different work.

What compaction cannot do is make an unsuitable material suitable. Densifying a highly plastic clay produces dense highly plastic clay, which still swells and shrinks with moisture. Material selection comes first, and that is an engineering decision.

## What does the specified percentage mean?

Density measured against a laboratory maximum for the specific material being placed.

Ninety-five percent standard Proctor means 95 percent of the maximum dry density established by ASTM D698 for that soil. The laboratory takes the actual material, compacts it at a series of moisture contents under a defined effort, and identifies the peak density and the moisture content that produces it. The field target is a percentage of that peak.

Which means the percentage on its own is close to meaningless. The same figure on two different soils describes two different densities, and a specification that names a percentage without identifying the test and the material is incomplete.

The modified test, ASTM D1557, applies substantially greater compactive effort and produces a higher maximum. Percentages against it are not interchangeable with the standard test, and confusing the two is a genuine and consequential error. FEMA's technical guidance for fill supporting buildings treats 95 percent standard as roughly equivalent to 90 percent modified, which is the cleanest available reference for the relationship.

For an owner reading a specification, the four things that make it complete are the standard, the percentage, the lift thickness and who tests. Any of those missing leaves room for the work to be done to a lower standard without anyone being technically wrong.

## Why does moisture content decide the outcome?

Because soil reaches its maximum density at a specific moisture content, and misses it in both directions.

Too dry and the particles resist rearranging into a dense packing, because there is not enough water to lubricate the movement. Too wet and water occupies the void space the compaction is meant to close, so the roller is compressing an incompressible fluid rather than densifying soil.

The laboratory test identifies the optimum moisture content along with the maximum density, and field work aims for a range around it.

On clay this matters more than on granular material, because the workable range is narrower and the penalty for missing it is larger. Material that is significantly wet of optimum cannot be compacted to specification no matter how many passes are made, and continuing to roll it can make things worse by remoulding the structure of the soil.

Moisture conditioning is therefore real work: adding water and mixing it through, or drying material by turning and aerating it, until it is within range. On a site with a seasonal high water table and long dry spells, both directions come up.

This is also why weather stops the job. After significant rain, clay this heavy is not workable, and a programme that assumes continuous dry working will slip. This region's rainfall arrives as a small number of intense events rather than as steady drizzle, which makes the stoppages sharp rather than gradual.

## Why does lift thickness matter as much as the roller?

Because compactive energy does not reach all the way down through a thick layer.

A roller applies energy at the surface, and that energy attenuates with depth. Place material too thick and the top of the lift reaches specification while the bottom stays loose. The pad tests acceptably if the test is taken near the surface, and it is defective.

Federal guidance for fill supporting buildings caps lifts at 12 inches. In Gulf Coast geotechnical practice, select fill is typically placed in loose lifts of eight inches or less, which is a description of engineering practice rather than a code requirement.

That distinction between loose and compacted thickness matters when reading a specification. A loose lift compacts down, so eight inches loose is not eight inches in the finished pad.

The practical consequence is that a specification stating a percentage and saying nothing about lifts has left the most common shortcut available. Placing thicker lifts is faster and cheaper, it produces a pad that can pass a surface test, and it is invisible.

Testing through the depth rather than only at the top is how it gets caught, which brings the testing regime back to the centre.

## What does density testing actually involve?

Field measurement of what was achieved, performed by somebody who is not the contractor placing the material.

That independence is the point. Testing by the party being tested is an assurance, not evidence, and the value of the result to a lender, insurer or future buyer depends entirely on it having been produced independently.

Testing frequency is set by the specification, usually as a number of tests per volume or area per lift. The results record location, depth, moisture content and the density achieved as a percentage of the laboratory maximum.

Failed tests are ordinary rather than shameful. A lift that fails is reworked and retested, and the record shows both. A test report with no failures across an entire large pad is less credible than one showing problems found and corrected.

The output is a document. Test results, tied to locations and elevations, are what turns a specification into an as-built record. Without them the pad may well be perfect and there is no way to demonstrate it.

That documentation is what gets requested during financing, insurance review, or a sale, and it cannot be reconstructed afterwards. Somebody would have to core the pad.

## What is being compacted, and does the material matter?

Enormously, and it is decided before any equipment arrives.

Texas engineering practice states plainly that foundations shall not be supported by non-engineered fill. Texas Section ASCE defines expansive clay as a weighted plasticity index above 15 with potential volume change over one percent, with potential vertical rise calculated over a 15-foot column.

Select fill has no statewide codified definition in Texas. In Gulf Coast practice it typically means a low-plasticity sandy clay or an imported caliche blend with plasticity roughly in the 8 to 18 range. That is convention, attributable to engineering practice rather than to code.

Critically, there is no code-mandated select fill cap depth in Texas. It is engineer-specified per project. Any contractor quoting a standard depth without an engineer's number is quoting a habit, and it is a large cost variable, so two bids assuming different depths are not comparable.

Caliche appears frequently as an imported material here. It is secondary calcium carbonate, formed by leaching and reprecipitation, quarried and crushed locally as a base material to improve drainage and traction. Base material is specified by plasticity and durability limits by grade rather than by name, and caliche is normally supplied against the lower of the flexible base grades.

Where on-site material is suitable, reusing it beats importing, and part of the assessment is establishing what can be reused.

## Where does compaction go wrong?

Five ways, and each one is invisible once the next layer is placed.

Lifts too thick, producing a pad dense at the surface and loose beneath.

Moisture out of range, where material is placed wet or dry of optimum and additional passes cannot compensate.

Testing too sparse, or taken only at convenient locations and depths, so the record does not represent the pad.

The wrong material, densified competently. A dense expansive clay still swells, and no amount of compaction changes that.

And compaction against structures or in confined areas, where a large roller cannot reach and smaller equipment is needed. Trench backfill beside a foundation, around utilities, and at the edges of a pad are classic locations for settlement later, because they are awkward and get less attention than open ground.

That last one is worth watching as an owner. Settlement that shows up as a dip along a utility trench line or against a wall is usually a backfill compaction failure rather than a pad failure.

## How does this relate to the pad and the drainage?

They are three parts of one system, and a failure in any of them presents as a failure in the others.

The pad establishes elevation and the platform. Compaction determines whether that platform stays where it was built. Drainage determines whether the material stays at the moisture content it was compacted at.

That third link is the one most often missed. Expansive clay compacted correctly at optimum moisture will still move if drainage allows it to be repeatedly saturated and dried at one edge of a structure. The compaction was not wrong; the environment changed.

Which is why positive fall away from a structure, roof drainage carried clear, and a site that sheds water are part of the foundation's working conditions rather than landscaping. The [drainage and site shaping](/services/drainage-and-site-shaping/) page covers that side.

Equally, a pad built to the right elevation on properly compacted engineered fill is the precondition for everything above it. The [building pads](/services/building-pads/) page covers elevation, flood requirements and the fill specification itself.

Compaction is the step in the middle, and it is the one with a number attached that can be verified.

## What does the work run like on site?

Sequential and unglamorous, with the pace set by moisture rather than by equipment.

Stripping removes unsuitable surface material — organics, soft ground, anything that will not perform.

The subgrade is then prepared and, where the specification requires it, proof-rolled to identify soft areas before anything is placed on top of them. Finding a soft spot at subgrade is cheap; finding it after three lifts is not.

Material is brought in or won from the site, spread to the specified loose lift thickness, and conditioned to moisture. Then rolled with equipment appropriate to the material — different soils respond to different compaction methods — until the pass count and the observed behaviour suggest specification has been reached.

The independent laboratory tests. Passing lifts are covered by the next; failing lifts are reworked and retested.

That cycle repeats to final elevation, after which the surface is finished to grade and the results are compiled into a record.

Confined areas, trench backfill and edges get the same treatment with smaller equipment and, usually, thinner lifts.

## What decides the cost of the compaction package?

Volume, material, moisture and testing.

Volume is set by the pad design and elevation requirement, which come from flood and ordinance determinations rather than from choice.

Material is the largest variable: whether on-site soil can be reused, what has to be imported, what specification the engineer requires, and how far the material has to travel. Haul distance is consistently underestimated because it is billable movement that produces nothing visible on your site.

Moisture conditioning is real cost, in water, in equipment time, and in weather delay. On a wet season or a wet site it is significant.

Testing frequency, set by the specification, is a line item and worth seeing itemised rather than absorbed.

And access, which decides equipment size and therefore lift thickness and pass count. Confined sites take longer for the same volume.

Being the lowest bid is not the aim here, and compaction is a clear illustration of why: the cheap version is thicker lifts and less testing, and neither is visible until it matters.

## What does a compaction specification look like in full?

Six elements, and a document missing any of them leaves room for interpretation.

The material: what may be placed, defined by its properties rather than by a name. Plasticity limits, gradation where relevant, and what is excluded.

The reference test: standard or modified, named explicitly, with the laboratory result for the actual material being used rather than a generic assumption.

The target density, as a percentage of that reference.

The moisture range, usually expressed relative to optimum, since a density achieved outside the workable moisture range is not durable even when it tests.

The lift thickness, stated as loose or compacted so there is no ambiguity, and the compaction method where the material warrants it.

The testing regime: frequency, who performs it, and what happens when a test fails.

An owner does not need to understand the engineering behind those to check they are present. Their presence is what makes a bid comparable to another bid, and their absence is why two quotes for the same pad can differ by a great deal without either being dishonest.

Where an engineer has produced a geotechnical report, all six come from that document. Where none exists on a structural project, that gap is itself the first thing to resolve.

## Why does the trench backfill matter more than people think?

Because it is the compaction most likely to be rushed and the settlement most likely to be visible.

Trenches for utilities, drainage lines and service runs are narrow, awkward, and frequently backfilled at the end of a day by whoever is available. The equipment that compacts open ground cannot work in them, so it takes smaller plate or rammer compaction in thinner lifts, which is slower per cubic yard than anything else on the site.

The consequences show up as a line. A visible depression tracking exactly along a utility run, months or years later, is almost always backfill that was placed too thick or too fast. Where that trench runs beneath a driveway, a slab edge or a road, the settlement takes the surface with it.

Near structures it is worse than cosmetic. Poorly compacted backfill against a foundation is a preferential path for water, which on expansive clay is the condition most likely to produce differential movement.

The fix is not complicated: thinner lifts, appropriate equipment, and testing that includes the trenches rather than only the open pad. It costs time, which is exactly why it gets skipped.

If you are watching one thing on a site as an owner, watch how the trenches get closed.

## What does the test record actually prove later?

That the ground under a structure was built to a standard, on a date, verified by somebody with no stake in the answer.

That sounds procedural until the moment somebody asks. A lender financing a commercial property, an insurer assessing a structure, a buyer's engineer during due diligence, or an owner arguing about settlement several years on — all of them want the same thing, and none of them can obtain it retroactively without coring the pad.

The record is also what distinguishes a defensible position from an assertion if something does go wrong. A pad built correctly with no testing and a pad built poorly with no testing produce identical documentation, which is to say none, and the party without evidence tends to lose that argument.

For a contractor it works the same way in reverse. Test results protect the party that placed the fill as much as the party that paid for it, because they establish what was achieved at the time rather than leaving it to be reconstructed from memory years later.

Keep the results with the property records rather than in a project file. Like a windstorm certificate, they outlive the contractor relationship and they travel with the building.

The cost of testing is small and known in advance. The cost of not having it is unknown and arrives at the worst possible moment, which is roughly the shape of every documentation decision on this site.

## When is heavy compaction not the answer?

When the material is wrong, when the loads are light, or when the ground is already suitable.

Densifying an unsuitable expansive clay does not make it suitable, and specifying more compaction to compensate for the wrong material is a common and expensive misunderstanding.

Where the native ground is a well-drained, non-vertic, low shrink-swell soil — the Laredo, Willacy and Hidalgo series among them — the specification may be far simpler than a difficult-site assumption suggests. Establishing that is worth doing, because it saves real money.

Where loads are genuinely light and no structure is involved, a compaction regime designed for a building is over-specification.

And where the determination has not been made yet. Compaction requirements come from an engineer looking at the soils and the structure, and specifying more compaction to compensate for an unanswered question is not a substitute for answering it. We build to that specification and verify it; we do not set it. Where a project needs a geotechnical investigation before anyone can say what the fill should be, saying so early is cheaper for you than proceeding on an assumption and testing against nothing.
