A building pad looks like the simplest thing on a construction site. Dirt, moved and flattened. It is in fact the element most likely to produce a problem that cannot be fixed later, because everything above it is built on the assumption that it will not move.

On this ground that assumption needs earning. Much of the Lower Rio Grande Valley sits on clays that change volume with moisture, and a pad placed without a specification will look identical on the day it is finished to one that was engineered. The difference shows up in year three, in a wall.

## What is a building pad actually for?

To give a foundation a predictable surface to bear on, at a height that keeps the structure out of water.

Two jobs, and they are frequently confused. The elevation job is about flooding: getting the finished floor above the level water is expected to reach. The bearing job is about structure: making sure what sits under the slab behaves consistently across the whole footprint.

A pad can succeed at one and fail the other. Plenty of buildings in this region sit comfortably above any flood and still crack, because the material beneath them swells and shrinks unevenly with the seasons. Equally, a beautifully compacted pad at the wrong elevation is a flood claim waiting to happen.

The third job, which nobody mentions until it goes wrong, is drainage. A pad is a raised platform in a flat landscape. Where the water goes once it runs off is part of the design, and it belongs to the same conversation.

Because the pad is buried the moment the slab goes on, it is also the part of a project where cutting corners is least visible and most consequential. Nothing about a finished house tells you how its pad was built.

## What does the ground here do?

It moves, and how much depends on which soil series you are on.

Much of the region sits on Vertisols, clays that swell when wet and shrink when dry. Harlingen clay is a very-fine smectitic Sodic Haplusterts, moderately well drained, with very slow permeability and cracks that stay open 150 to 210 cumulative days in most years. Benito clay is the same great group but poorly drained, with runoff from very slow to ponded, slickensides between 8 and 63 inches, cracks up to three inches wide reaching 20 inches deep, and a seasonal high water table 4 to 6 feet down from September through May.

Shrink-swell potential shows in the COLE values. Chargo silty clay runs 0.09 to 0.14, Olmito silty clay 0.07 to 0.15, Raymondville clay loam 0.07 to 0.17. All of those are very high shrink-swell soils.

Near the bay the chemistry takes over from the drainage. Lomalta clay is a Typic Natraquerts, poorly drained and semi-marshy, sitting slightly above sea level, with electrical conductivity from 12 to 50 dS/m and exchangeable sodium above 15 percent in every horizon.

Not all of it is difficult. Laredo silty clay loam, Willacy fine sandy loam and Hidalgo sandy clay loam are well drained, moderately permeable, non-vertic and low shrink-swell. Those are the straightforward sites, and knowing whether you are on one is worth establishing before budgeting for the hard version.

Two naming traps travel with any soils report here. Point Isabel is a clay despite what the coastal name suggests, while South Padre Island genuinely is sand. And the Rio Grande series is coarse-silty, so its phases are silt loam and very fine sandy loam. A report citing Rio Grande silty clay loam is describing something that does not exist.

## How is pad height decided?

By derivation, not by preference, and the number comes from outside the construction team.

In a mapped Special Flood Hazard Area the lowest floor must sit at or above base flood elevation, plus whatever freeboard the community has adopted on top. Base flood elevation is a surveyed height on a datum rather than a depth above your yard, so comparing it to your lot needs a survey on the same reference.

Local ordinance then adds its own requirement, and it frequently references something visible rather than a datum. Harlingen publishes a citable example: foundations in Zone X no less than 18 inches above top of curb, 24 inches inside a Special Flood Hazard Area, and lots within 100 feet of a drainage inlet with finished floors six inches above neighbouring lots.

Notice that the Harlingen rule applies even in Zone X, outside the mapped high-risk area entirely. Somebody checking only the flood map would satisfy the federal minimum and miss the local requirement.

We do not publish freeboard figures for South Padre Island or Cameron County, because we could not confirm current values from a primary source. Your floodplain administrator holds them, and the [flood elevation](/compliance/fema-flood-elevation-and-pad-height/) page sets out how the whole determination works.

The practical result is that pad volume, which is usually the largest single cost on the job, is fixed by numbers somebody else supplies. Get them before anyone quotes.

## Where can a pad not be used at all?

In coastal high-hazard flood zones, and this catches out anyone working from mainland assumptions.

In V and VE zones, participating communities must prohibit the use of fill for structural support of buildings. Elevation comes from piles or columns, the lowest horizontal structural member sits at or above base flood elevation, and anything enclosed below has to be breakaway construction.

The reason is wave action. A fill pad in that environment is not a foundation, it is material waiting to be scoured out from beneath a building.

South Padre Island is where this applies most directly in Cameron County. A site-prep bid there that prices imported material and compaction to raise a pad is answering a question that does not apply to the site. The earthwork on such a project concerns access, drainage and preparing for a pile-supported structure instead.

On the mainland, A and AE zones do allow elevation on fill, subject to the fill being engineered and to whatever the community requires about how far it extends and how it is protected. The method is available; it comes with an engineer attached.

## What does an engineered pad actually involve?

A specification, controlled placement, and testing that produces evidence.

The governing principle in Texas practice is blunt and settles most site arguments: foundations shall not be supported by non-engineered fill. Texas Section ASCE defines expansive clay as a weighted plasticity index above 15 with potential volume change over one percent, with potential vertical rise calculated over a 15-foot column.

Placement happens in lifts. Federal guidance on structures built on fill caps lifts at 12 inches and treats 95 percent standard compaction as roughly equivalent to 90 percent modified. Lift thickness matters as much as the roller passes, because material placed too deep does not densify through its full depth however many times the equipment goes over the surface.

Moisture conditioning is the step that separates competent pad work from dirt moving. Clay compacts properly only within a moisture range, and material too wet or too dry will not reach density regardless of effort. On a site with a seasonal high water table and long dry spells, managing that is real work.

Then testing, which is what converts a specification into a record. Density testing during placement documents that the pad was built as designed, and that record is what gets requested when the property is financed, insured or sold.

## What does the compaction percentage mean?

Density against a laboratory maximum for that specific material, which is why the number alone tells you little.

Ninety-five percent standard Proctor means 95 percent of the maximum dry density established by ASTM D698 for the soil actually being placed. Change the soil and the same percentage describes a different density. That is why a specification that states a percentage without identifying the test and the material is incomplete.

The modified test, ASTM D1557, uses greater compactive effort and produces a higher maximum, so percentages against it are not interchangeable with the standard test. FEMA's technical guidance treats 95 percent standard as approximately 90 percent modified for fill supporting buildings, which is the cleanest available reference for the equivalence.

What we will not do is publish compaction energies, Valley-specific plasticity ranges or potential vertical rise figures. Those vary by material and by project and are the engineer's to determine.

For an owner reading a bid, the useful questions are which standard, what percentage, what lift thickness, and who performs the testing. A bid silent on all four is not cheaper. It is smaller.

## What is select fill, and how deep should it be?

It is a description of practice rather than a code term, and there is no depth to look up.

Select fill has no statewide codified definition in Texas. In Gulf Coast geotechnical practice it typically means a low-plasticity sandy clay or an imported caliche blend, with plasticity roughly in the 8 to 18 range, placed in loose lifts of eight inches or less. That is engineering convention, attributable to practice rather than to code.

More importantly, there is no code-mandated select fill cap depth in Texas. It is engineer-specified per project, based on the soils, the structure and the loads. Any contractor quoting a standard depth without an engineer's number is quoting a habit.

That is genuinely useful to know when comparing bids, because select fill depth is a large cost variable. Two quotes assuming different depths are not comparable, and neither is necessarily wrong until an engineer says what the project needs.

Where on-site material is suitable, cut and fill is usually cheaper than importing, and part of the assessment is establishing what can be reused. Haul distance for imported material is the cost driver owners most often underestimate.

## How does the job run, start to finish?

In an order where most of the expensive decisions happen before any equipment arrives.

Determinations first: flood zone, required elevation, jurisdiction, and whether a drainage district easement touches the tract. Those come from the authorities and a surveyor, and they set the target the pad is built to.

Geotechnical investigation where the project needs one, producing the fill specification, the depth and the compaction requirement.

Approvals next, which can include a building permit, a floodplain development permit, drainage district approval for what the site discharges, and stormwater authorisation on larger disturbances. Those run in parallel and each has its own lead time. The [drainage districts](/compliance/drainage-district-and-irrigation-canal-crossings/) and [stormwater](/compliance/stormwater-and-erosion-control/) pages cover two of them.

Erosion controls installed before the ground is opened, not after the first storm.

Clearing and stripping of unsuitable surface material. Then placement: material brought to moisture, spread in specified lifts, compacted, tested, and the next lift placed only when the one below passes.

Final grading shapes the pad and the surrounding ground so water leaves the way the design intends. Then documentation: test results, elevations, and an as-built record.

## What drives the price?

Volume, distance, specification and access, in roughly that order.

Volume comes from the elevation difference across the whole footprint plus any over-build, and it is set by the flood and ordinance requirements rather than by choice.

Haul distance decides what that volume costs to deliver, because it is billable equipment movement rather than visible work on your site. It is the single most underestimated line.

Specification changes everything: what material is acceptable, what depth of select fill, what compaction standard, how much testing. An engineer's requirement can double a pad's cost relative to an unengineered assumption, which is precisely why bids diverge so widely.

Access determines method. Infill lots, occupied commercial sites and constrained approaches mean smaller equipment and more passes, which is a schedule consequence as much as a cost one.

Then site conditions: what has to be stripped, whether the existing material can be reused, and how wet the ground is when the work happens. Clay this heavy does not work in the days after real rain, and a programme assuming continuous dry working will slip.

## What goes wrong, and how is it prevented?

Four failure modes, and none of them is exotic.

Non-engineered fill under a structure, meaning material placed and graded without a specification, testing or an engineer. It looks finished and it is the single most common serious defect in residential pad work.

Lifts too thick, so the pad tests acceptably at the surface and is loose below. Prevented by specifying lift thickness and testing through the depth rather than only on top.

Moisture out of range, where material is placed too wet or too dry and never reaches density. Prevented by conditioning the material rather than by adding roller passes.

Drainage ignored, where the pad is built correctly and the surrounding ground is left to send water back against the structure or onto a neighbour. Prevented by treating final grading as part of the pad rather than as clean-up.

The fifth, which is administrative rather than technical: no test records. A pad with no evidence is indistinguishable, on paper, from one that was never engineered.

## What role does caliche play?

It is the region's workhorse imported material, and it is a base course by nature rather than a structural fill by default.

Caliche is secondary calcium carbonate, formed by carbonate leaching from upper soil and reprecipitating lower down, developing through six recognised morphologic stages. In the Valley it is commercially quarried and crushed as a base material to improve drainage and traction on unpaved roads, and it is mapped locally as a distinct geologic unit.

That origin explains both its usefulness and its limits. It drains, it compacts well, and it is available. It is also variable, because a quarried natural material differs from pit to pit and within a pit.

Which is why base material is specified by property rather than by name. The TxDOT flexible base specification sets limits on liquid limit, plasticity index and wet ball mill loss by grade, with the higher grade held to the tighter plasticity limit, and requires compaction to full specified density. Caliche is normally supplied against the lower of those grades.

In a pad context, caliche most often appears as a component of an imported select fill blend or as the base course beneath driveways, approaches and laydown areas. Where it is being used structurally, the engineer's specification governs, not its reputation.

We do not publish which pits are active or what any specific pit's material tests at. Both change, and a stale answer on a page carrying a review date is worse than none.

## Why does the ground around the pad matter as much as the pad?

Because a pad is a raised platform in a flat landscape, and everything you displace has to go somewhere.

Raising a lot changes the drainage of the ground around it. Water that used to spread across the site now runs off a platform, concentrates at its edges, and leaves in whatever direction the finished grading sends it. Get that wrong and you have moved a problem onto a neighbour, into a district channel that has rules about it, or back against your own foundation.

The last of those is the quiet failure. A pad built to the right elevation, with water allowed to pond against its edge, subjects expansive clay to exactly the repeated wetting and drying that produces differential movement. The structure is high enough and still moves.

Local ordinance frequently addresses this directly, which is why rules like Harlingen's requirement for finished floors six inches above neighbouring lots within 100 feet of a drainage inlet exist at all. They are managing the interaction between adjacent properties rather than the elevation of any one of them.

Where a drainage district governs the receiving channel, the allowable discharge rate and the permitted increase decide whether the site needs detention, which changes the layout rather than just the grading.

So final grading is part of the pad, not clean-up after it. A bid that prices placement and compaction but treats the surrounding shaping as an afterthought has priced two thirds of the job.

## What will you be asked to provide?

Six things, and most of them exist already somewhere.

The address, and whether it sits inside a city or in unincorporated county.

The flood zone and required elevation if you already have them, or permission for us to establish who holds them.

Any survey of the property, which is the document that makes volume calculable.

Any geotechnical report, whether from this project or a previous one on the tract.

What is being built, since the structure decides the loads and therefore the specification.

And where water stands after a real rain, which you know better than anyone and which tells us more about the site than a dry-day walk ever will. Photographs taken at the time are better still, because standing water is long gone by the day anyone arrives to look at it.

## When is a pad not the right answer?

More often than it might seem from a page about building pads.

In a coastal high-hazard zone, where fill cannot support a structure at all.

Where the problem is drainage rather than elevation. Water standing in the wrong part of a lot is frequently a grading and shaping question, and building a pad to solve it is an expensive way to relocate the problem.

Where the ground is good and the structure is light, in which case the specification may be far simpler than a difficult-site assumption would suggest. That is worth establishing rather than assuming, because it saves real money.

And where the determination belongs to an engineer we have not yet heard from. Foundation design, fill depth and bearing capacity are engineering decisions. We build to them; we do not make them.
