Replacing a roof is three jobs stacked on each other. The first is demolition. The second is carpentry, because the deck under a twenty-year-old roof is rarely the deck the last crew described. The third is compliance, and on this coast that is not a formality: in Cameron and Willacy counties, a roof that was never inspected is a roof the Texas Windstorm Insurance Association has no evidence about.

What follows is that sequence in order, including where the money goes and where the honest answer is to fix what you have instead.

## What does a full tear-off replacement actually include?

Everything above the framing, plus the paperwork that proves it went on correctly. A job that stops short of the following is a partial one wearing a full one's name.

**Tear-off and disposal.** Every layer down to bare sheathing, hauled off, with magnet passes over the drive and yard. A two-layer roof is twice the debris weight, which is why the first question on any bid is how many layers are up there.

**Deck inspection and repair.** With the sheathing exposed, the crew walks every square foot, marks soft spots, and pulls what has delaminated or rotted. Panels get re-fastened to the framing on a specified pattern, not just where the old nails happened to land. Spongy panels over a bathroom vent, a rafter tail eaten out at the eave: all of it is [roof decking and structural work](/services/) that has to be finished before anything watertight goes down.

**Dry-in.** Underlayment across the field, self-adhered membrane in the valleys and around penetrations, drip edge at eaves and rakes. For asphalt shingles the code floor is a 2:12 slope, and between 2:12 and 4:12 the underlayment is doubled. Below 2:12, shingles are the wrong product and no amount of extra felt fixes that.

**Flashing, replaced rather than reused.** Step flashing at walls, counterflashing into masonry, apron and headwall flashing, pipe boots, curb flashing at skylights and equipment. Old flashing bent back and re-bedded in sealant is the single most common shortcut on a reroof, and it is why so many "new" roofs leak at exactly the same spot they leaked before.

**The covering, installed to the printed instructions.** For strip shingles that means six nails per shingle here, not four. Code requires a minimum of four fasteners per strip shingle and six in high-wind areas of 110 mph and above. TDI's lowest Cameron County wind zone carries a legacy nominal design speed of 110 mph, which puts the six-nail pattern at the floor for the entire county, from the beachfront to La Feria. Nails must catch both the overlay and the underlay portion of the course, sit no closer than two inches to a joint or cutout in the shingle below, and be 11 or 12 gauge with a head of at least 3/8 inch and at least 3/4 inch of penetration.

**Ventilation and the attic assembly.** Intake at the soffit balanced against exhaust at the ridge, and a decision about whether the attic stays vented at all. In this climate zone the moisture drive is inward, which is the opposite of what most national roofing content assumes. Class I vapour retarders of 0.1 perm or less are prohibited on the interior side of air-permeable insulation in zones 1, 2 and 3A. If the attic is being converted to unvented, it needs a vapour diffusion port of at least 1:600 of the ceiling area, within twelve inches of the ridge, at 20 perms or better, on a slope of 3:12 or steeper. Rigid board above the deck needs a minimum of R-5 for condensation control.

**Documentation.** Product data sheets, the wind and impact classifications for what was actually installed, photographs of the deck before it was covered, and the windstorm paperwork described further down. On a low-slope roof over cooled conditioned space in Climate Zone 2A, the reflectance requirement belongs in that file: a three-year-aged solar reflectance of at least 0.55 with thermal emittance of at least 0.75, or an aged SRI of 64 or better. Exceptions exist and the applicable code edition governs, but a dark membrane over an air-conditioned space here should be a documented decision rather than an accident of what was on the truck.

## How does a reroof run, hour by hour, from dumpster to close-out?

Most single-family replacements run two to four days, and the order almost never changes.

### Before anyone gets on the roof

Material is staged, the dumpster is placed, and the windstorm application is filed. That last one cannot be done later: the WPI-1 goes to TDI before work begins. Plywood goes over the condenser, tarps over the beds, and someone walks the property with you to agree where trucks park. On a caliche or sand drive out toward Boca Chica or the island, dumpster placement is a real conversation; a loaded roll-off sinks into soft ground and leaves ruts you will be looking at for years.

### The tear-off

Crews open only as much roof as they can dry in the same day. That is a weather rule, not a productivity rule. The Valley averages fewer than thirty thunderstorm days a year, but the rain that does arrive comes fast and heavy; Santa Rosa recorded over fifteen inches during Hanna. An open deck at four in the afternoon is a decision someone made at nine in the morning.

### The pause at the deck

This is where the scope can change. With the sheathing bare, the actual condition is visible for the first time: soft panels get cut out, under-fastened decking gets re-nailed. If a bid did not contemplate deck repair at all, either the bidder never looked or they intend to have that conversation when you have no roof and no leverage. Ask before the tear-off how deck replacement is priced and documented.

### Dry-in

Underlayment, valley membrane, drip edge, and the building is weather-tight again. On a low-slope job the equivalent is a temporary water cut-off at the end of every shift, tied into the existing membrane so a night storm does not run into the tenant's ceiling.

### The covering, then the details

Field installation goes fast. Flashings, penetrations, ridge and terminations go slowly, which is the correct ratio: most of the labour on a good roof is spent on ten percent of its area.

### Inspection and close-out

The windstorm inspection has to happen while the work can still be seen. Photographs, a final magnet sweep, a walk of the perimeter and gutters, and the documentation package. On an occupied [commercial building](/commercial/), close-out also means confirming that every roof-mounted unit is back on its curb, every gas line and conduit is re-supported, and nothing was left on the deck for a maintenance tech to find in October.

## What actually decides what a coastal replacement costs?

Roof size is the smallest interesting variable. The drivers below move a number far more than square footage does.

**Layers, complexity and access.** Two or three layers of shingle mean double or triple debris weight and multiple hauls. Hips, valleys, dormers, chimneys, skylights, solar racking, and every penetration that has to be flashed rather than shingled past. A simple gable is a different job from a cut-up hip roof of identical area. Then there is getting to it: a single-story ranch in Los Fresnos with a gate on both sides is not a mid-rise on the island where material moves by boom lift, across a causeway, on a delivery window.

**Slope.** Steeper roofs need staging, harnesses and slower production. Low-slope roofs need different systems entirely, and the minimum slopes are not negotiable: 1/4:12 for standing seam, built-up, modified bitumen and single-ply membranes; 1/2:12 for lapped, non-soldered metal seams with applied sealant; 3:12 for the same detail without sealant.

**Deck condition.** The largest unknown on the job. Sheathing replacement is normally carried as a unit price rather than a lump sum, because nobody can see through an old roof.

**System selection.** Architectural shingle, standing seam, exposed-fastener R-panel, TPO, modified bitumen and coatings all price differently and fail differently. Cheaper up front is sometimes correct: R-panel over a shop building in Bayview is a sensible choice. R-panel on an oceanfront house is not.

**Coastal substrate and fastener upgrades.** Inside 1,500 feet of salt water the material list changes and so does the cost. Aluminium panel runs roughly 15 to 25 percent above steel before anything else is counted, and aluminium moves about twice as much as steel with temperature, so runs beyond about fifteen to twenty feet need expansion clips rather than fixed ones.

**Wind and impact class.** ASTM D3161 is the fan-induced test: Class A is 60 mph, Class D is 90, Class F is 110. ASTM D7158 compares uplift force to resistance: Class D up to 115 mph, Class G up to 150, Class H up to 190. Both standards state plainly that their results do not correlate directly to wind speeds in service. They are a comparison between products, not a promise about your roof.

**The certification path.** A replacement run in the normal sequence is inspected at no charge by a TDI inspector. A replacement that gets certified after the fact requires a Texas-licensed professional engineer, which is a cost the owner absorbs and a delay that can hold up a closing.

**Occupancy and schedule.** Night work, phased tear-offs on an operating building, fire watch on torch-applied work, temporary protection over a sales floor. All real, all billable, all cheaper to plan than to improvise.

Nothing on this page is a price. Anyone giving you a per-square number before they have seen your deck is quoting a different roof.

## What goes wrong on a coastal reroof, and how is it kept from happening?

The failure modes here are specific and repetitive. Every one of them is avoidable at the specification stage and expensive afterward.

**Wrong metal for the exposure.** The most misunderstood mistake in coastal roofing. Galvalume is not the coastal substrate; it is the mainland substrate. Its own manufacturers exclude it from substrate warranty coverage within 1,500 feet of salt water, and the industry position is blunt: neither Galvalume nor galvanized will stand up to a coastal environment. Aluminium is the oceanfront and bayfront material, and it carries coastal paint warranties steel does not. Note the unit. The exclusion is 1,500 feet, not miles, and nearly every "within X miles" claim online is a corruption of the actual warranty language.

**Wrong fasteners.** Match the fastener to the substrate. For aluminium panels on this coast that means 300-series, typically 304, stainless. Type 410 stainless is explicitly not rated for coastal service. Electroplated zinc screws do not belong outdoors at all, and galvanized fasteners are not recommended in contact with aluminium cladding. Organic-coated steel screws should show no red rust after 500 hours of ASTM B117 salt spray. One counter-example worth knowing, because "always stainless" is not universally true: DECRA discourages stainless fasteners with its own steel systems. Read the manufacturer's instructions rather than a rule of thumb.

**Designing for distance instead of for wash-off.** Airborne chlorides are hygroscopic. They hold a film of electrolyte on the metal long after the rain stops, and they break down the passive oxide layer on both zinc and aluminium. Deposition and rinsing therefore matter more than a number on a map. Sheltered undersides, eave returns, the back of a fascia and anything under a soffit corrode faster than the rain-washed field of the same roof. Fresh-water rinsing of sheltered metal is a genuine maintenance item on a bay-front building.

**Ignoring thermal movement.** Standing seam floats on clips designed to let the panel move. Exposed-fastener panels are pinned through the face, which limits movement and works the fasteners loose over time. That is why exposed-fastener roofs need looking at up to twice a year and standing seam does not.

**Nailing errors.** High nails, overdriven nails that cut the mat, and four nails where six were required. The documented failure mode across Beulah, Dolly and Hanna is not engineered buildings collapsing; it is roof coverings coming off lower-quality construction. The final report on Dolly, a Category 1 at mainland landfall, describes mainly moderate structural damage on South Padre Island, primarily to roofs. Hanna's notes hundreds of poorly built structures with roof damage. The wind finds the fastening pattern.

**Reused flashing, with sealant asked to do its job.** Sealant is a maintenance item with a service life. Flashing is permanent. Substitute one for the other and the roof has a scheduled leak.

**Ponding, and the myths about it.** Manufacturer warranty language commonly defines ponding as water that has not drained or dissipated within 48 hours after rain stops. That is a warranty definition, not a code definition. "Ponding voids everything" is too broad: GAF's EverGuard Diamond Pledge NDL Guarantee, by its own terms, covers leaks in ponding areas on TPO and PVC, while on Galvalume ponding is an absolute exclusion. What matters is the specific document for the specific system, which is why the low-slope conversation starts with drainage design and tapered insulation rather than membrane brand.

**Assuming UV is what kills a membrane.** For TPO it is heat. Research published through NRCA puts utility-grade material at roughly fifteen years at a sustained 160°F, standard grades near twenty-five years at 180°F, and the most heat-stable formulations beyond thirty-five years at around 200°F, with UV ageing not appearing to be the issue. Heat performance differs materially between manufacturers, so the data sheet matters more than the category name.

**Believing Class 4 means hail-proof.** UL 2218 Class 4 is a two-inch steel ball dropped from twenty feet, twice at the same spot, on new material, judged by whether the underside fractured; cosmetic damage on the top surface is explicitly not the criterion. GAF's own disclaimers state its impact-resistant shingles are not warranted to withstand hail damage, and insurance credits vary by state and carrier. Hail here is episodic: the 29 March 2012 mid-Valley storm ran to roughly $600 million insured. On the coast, wind is the everyday driver.

## How does windstorm certification actually work on a replacement?

It works in one direction only, and the order is the whole story. This is what a mainland roofer gets wrong most often, and the consequences land on the owner.

**Who is in the program.** The designated catastrophe area covers fourteen first-tier coastal counties. Cameron and Willacy are in it. Hidalgo County is not; it is second-tier, which is not the same thing and does not carry the same requirement. A reroof in San Benito is in the program. A reroof in Weslaco is not. Details of which rules bite where are collected on the [compliance pages](/compliance/), and the county line matters more than any distance from the water.

**The WPI-1 comes first.** The application is filed with the Texas Department of Insurance before work begins. Not the day after the tear-off, not when the last ridge cap goes on. Before.

**TDI issues the certificate, and only on someone else's certification.** The WPI-8 is a Certificate of Compliance issued by TDI. It rests on certification by a TDI-employed inspector, a TDI appointed qualified inspector, or a Texas-licensed professional engineer. A roofing contractor is none of those three. No contractor can certify their own work, and any pitch that includes "we'll handle the certificate ourselves" is describing a document that does not exist. What a contractor can do is build to the certifiable specification, file the application on time, and be there when the inspector arrives. TDI inspectors charge no fee for the inspection, and once TDI has issued a certificate, it may not rescind it.

**The small-repair exemption, quoted correctly.** Roof repairs under 100 square feet that do not involve decking or framing members do not require inspection. That is 100 square feet, roughly a ten-by-ten patch. It is widely misquoted online as "100 squares," which would be 10,000 square feet, and sometimes as a dollar threshold. A full replacement never qualifies.

**What happens if the roof is already closed in.** TDI and appointed qualified inspectors cannot inspect work they can no longer see. Once the assembly is closed in, the only remaining route is a Texas-licensed professional engineer, who evaluates and submits a WPI-2E; TDI then issues a WPI-8-E. It is slower and more expensive than the ordinary path, and it is entirely avoidable by making one phone call before the tear-off rather than after. This is the strongest practical argument for scheduling a coastal reroof properly.

**A form you should never hear mentioned.** The WPI-3 has been dead since 31 May 2020, when TWIA stopped accepting it. Texas roofing sites still cite it. If a contractor or an agent offers a WPI-3 as your path to coverage, that advice is at least six years out of date, and you should treat everything else they told you with the same suspicion.

**What the certificate is worth.** TWIA's own position is that without a certificate it lacks evidence the structure conforms to applicable building codes, and the structure may be considered uninsurable and ineligible for coverage. That is a hedge and should be quoted as one; nobody can tell you your policy will be cancelled. The certificate is the evidence, and the evidence is easy to create during construction and awkward to create afterward.

**Two compliance tracks, not one.** The city building permit and the TDI windstorm certificate are separate. Port Isabel's permits run on the 2018 I-codes. Work begun on or after 1 April 2026 is certified for windstorm purposes to the 2024 IRC and IBC; work begun between 1 September 2020 and that date was certified to the 2018 codes. A project can satisfy the city inspector and still fail the windstorm certification, because they are being measured against different editions by different people for different purposes. Almost nobody explains this before it happens.

**The zones, and what they are not.** Cameron County has three. Seaward is South Padre Island only. Inland I covers Brownsville, Port Isabel, Los Fresnos, Laguna Vista, Laguna Heights, Rio Hondo, Bayview, Arroyo City, Boca Chica, Indian Lake, Lozano, Southmost and Port Brownsville. Inland II covers Harlingen, San Benito, La Feria, Combes, Primera, Palm Valley, Rancho Viejo, Olmito, Santa Rosa, Los Indios, Santa Maria and Stuart Place. Port Isabel is Inland I despite fronting the bay, because the zone boundary is U.S. 77 rather than a distance from water, and Brownsville is assigned Inland I by special rule regardless of the line. The zone map for the county is the authority, and every market page under [locations](/locations/) states the zone for that town rather than guessing from a map pin.

**On design wind speed, a deliberate silence.** The familiar figures of 130, 120 and 110 mph for Seaward, Inland I and Inland II are legacy nominal speeds from the 2006 code cycle. The same buildings under ASCE 7-10's ultimate convention correspond to 168, 155 and 143 mph. Those are two units for one thing, not two levels of safety, and a bare number pulled out of either set is meaningless. For a structure permitted today the design wind speed is determined per structure under ASCE 7 by the engineer of record, and no number on this site substitutes for that.

**Willacy County, honestly.** Willacy is inside the designated catastrophe area; that much is settled, and it is why Raymondville, Lyford, Sebastian, San Perlita and Port Mansfield are all in the program. The specific zone assignment for those communities is not published here, because the county's zone map has not been verified directly. A wrong zone on a roof specification is worse than an admitted gap.

## What will you be asked to provide before the crew arrives?

Less than you would think, but the few that matter, matter early.

**The address and the jurisdiction.** City limits versus unincorporated county changes who permits the work, and the county decides whether the windstorm track applies at all.

**A decision on the certificate.** If you want the WPI-8, say so before work starts. Owners planning to sell within a few years should assume a buyer's lender or insurer will ask.

**Owner and property information for the application.** Name, mailing address, property description and the scope of work. The application is about the structure, not about the contractor.

**Prior certificates.** If a previous replacement, addition or window job was certified, the earlier WPI-8 documentation is worth digging out. A complete file is one of the more useful things a coastal owner can keep.

**Access.** Gate codes, dogs, side-yard clearance, the electrical panel and water spigot, and where a roll-off can sit without cracking a drive.

**Association or park approvals.** On the island, in Long Island Village and in the golf-course communities, colour, profile and sometimes material are governed by the association, whose approval calendar runs on its own schedule.

**Insurance documentation, if this is a claim.** The adjuster's scope, the declarations page and the claim number. A roofer can meet an adjuster on the roof and document conditions. A roofer is not an adjuster, cannot negotiate your claim, and cannot tell you what your policy covers. If someone offers to handle your claim, ask what licence they hold to do it.

**For commercial buildings:** a roof plan if one exists, the deck type, an equipment and penetration list, tenant hours, after-hours access, and the fire-watch policy if torch or hot work is involved. Phasing a roof over an operating building is a scheduling problem as much as a roofing one.

**One thing you will not be asked for:** a state roofing licence number, because Texas does not license roofing contractors. TDLR's regulated programs do not include roofing. That surprises most owners, and it puts the burden of vetting insurance and work history on you. Local registration is separate and set city by city. ## When is a repair the better answer than a replacement?

Often enough that a roofer who never recommends one is selling, not diagnosing. The test is not age. It is whether the failure is local or general.

**Repair is usually the right call when:**

The problem is flashing. Most roofs leak where the roof stops rather than in the field: a wall termination, a chimney, a pipe boot cracked by twenty years of sun, a skylight curb. A sound field with a failed detail is a repair, and replacing the whole roof will not fix a badly built chimney saddle anyway.

Damage is confined to one slope or one storm. Wind that stripped shingles off a single elevation of an otherwise tight roof is a repair, and if the product is still available the match will be acceptable.

The roof is young and the deck is dry. Early granule loss is normal shedding, not failure.

The water is not coming from the roof. Wind-driven rain through a window head, a failed wall penetration, a condensate line backed up in the attic, a bath fan dumping moist air into the attic in a climate where the vapour drive is already inward. Ceiling stains are frequently blamed on roofs that are not leaking.

The repair stays under 100 square feet and does not touch decking or framing. In that case, in Cameron or Willacy County, the small-repair exemption applies and the inspection requirement does not.

**Replacement is the honest answer when:**

The mat is showing across multiple slopes, shingles are brittle enough to crack when lifted, and the sealant strips have released generally rather than locally. At that point every repair breaks two shingles to fix one.

There are multiple layers. A second layer of shingles hides the deck, adds weight, and makes any certification conversation harder than it needs to be.

The deck is compromised over more than isolated bays. Widespread soft sheathing is a structural repair with a roof on top of it, and doing that under an old covering costs more than doing it during a tear-off.

The metal is corroding at the fasteners across the field rather than at one flashing. That usually means the fastener specification was wrong for the exposure from the start, and re-screwing it buys a year or two.

A low-slope membrane is at the end of its service life with wet insulation underneath. Once the insulation is saturated, patching the membrane traps the water instead of removing it. Core cuts settle that argument quickly.

Repairs have become a subscription. Three service calls in two years on the same roof is a spending pattern, not a maintenance plan.

**Two honest edge cases.** If you are selling within a year and the roof is sound but dated, a documented repair and a clean file may serve you better than a replacement you will not enjoy. And in Weslaco, Mercedes, Donna or Progreso the windstorm certificate is not part of the decision at all, which changes the arithmetic; there the driver is hail and straight-line wind, and impact classification matters more than certification.
