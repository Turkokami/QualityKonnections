Storm work is where roofing is done best and worst. The damage is real, the urgency is real, and the combination produces both excellent emergency work and the least honest selling in the trade.

This page is written to be useful in the days when you are least able to evaluate anything: what to do first, how damage is honestly assessed, and which single step is most often skipped under pressure and most expensive to recover.

## What should you do in the first hours?

Protect, document, then decide. In that order, and the order matters.

Protection first, because interior damage compounds faster than roof damage. Water reaching insulation, ceilings, contents and electrical systems turns a roof problem into a building problem within a day. Stop water entering, get standing water out, and move what can be moved.

Documentation second, and it has to happen before anything is cleaned up or repaired. Dated photographs of the exterior from several angles, the interior damage, any affected contents or equipment, and the roof surface only where it can be viewed safely. Photograph what came off and where it landed. Keep the record of any temporary work and when it was done.

Decisions third. Scope, contractor, claim. None of those is improved by being made in the first hour, and all of them are made worse by being made under pressure from somebody standing in your driveway.

One thing not to do: walk a wet, damaged or aged roof yourself. Assessment from the ground with binoculars tells you whether to call somebody, and that is all you need to establish yourself. The rest of it is somebody else's job, and somebody else's risk to take.

## What does temporary protection actually involve?

Buying time without creating a second problem.

On a sloped roof that usually means covering the breach with a properly secured tarp, fastened so it does not become a sail in the next gust and does not itself create new penetrations in sound areas. A tarp that blows off overnight has achieved nothing.

On low-slope commercial roofing, the objective is closing open seams and getting water off the surface, because standing water finds any opening. Drains and scuppers frequently need clearing at the same time, since debris is what caused the ponding.

Temporary protection is not a repair, and it should not be sold as one. It is a holding position while the permanent scope is established, the claim is worked, and materials are obtained. On a coast where storms arrive at the start of a season rather than the end, that holding position sometimes has to last.

It also has to be documented, because temporary work performed and paid for is part of the loss record. Photograph it, keep the paperwork.

What temporary work should not do is destroy evidence of the original damage before it has been recorded. Which is why documentation belongs alongside protection rather than after it.

## How is damage assessed honestly?

By establishing where water is actually getting in and how much of the assembly is compromised, rather than by counting visible marks.

Interior staining is a poor locator. Water travels along deck flutes, purlins and insulation joints before it appears, so the mark on the ceiling is frequently nowhere near the breach. Chasing stains produces repairs that each fix something real and none of which fix the cause.

A useful assessment establishes several things. Whether the covering has failed generally or in a specific area. Whether the underlayment beneath is intact. Whether insulation is wet, and across how much area. Whether the deck has been compromised. Whether the details — edges, flashings, curbs, penetrations — are the origin, which they usually are.

That evidence produces the honest recommendation, and sometimes the honest recommendation is a repair. A localised failure on an otherwise sound assembly with dry material beneath is a repair. Selling a replacement into that situation is the behaviour that has damaged this trade's reputation.

The reverse is equally true and less often said. Widespread wet insulation is not a repair regardless of how modest the visible damage looks, because the thermal value is already gone and the fastener holding capacity the wind rating depends on is going with it.

## What do storms here actually break?

Roof coverings on weaker construction. Consistently, across every major event on record.

Beulah came ashore in 1967 as a Category 3 between the mouth of the Rio Grande and Brownsville, cutting 31 new channels across South Padre Island and spawning roughly 115 tornadoes. Allen in 1980 made a Category 3 landfall near Port Mansfield with a 12-foot surge, 68 new cuts on Padre Island, and four feet of water in Brownsville buildings.

Dolly in 2008 was a Category 1 at mainland landfall, about 70 knots, with roughly $1.05 billion in damage. The final report described mainly moderate structural damage, primarily to roofs, on South Padre Island. Brownsville recorded 13.20 inches of rain. The commonly repeated Category 2 figure was the operational estimate rather than the final analysis.

Hanna in 2020 was a Category 1 at Padre Island landfall, about 80 knots, roughly $1.2 billion in damage, with a peak gust of 103 miles per hour at Rincon del San Jose, 15.49 inches at Santa Rosa, more than 250,000 customers without power, and hundreds of poorly built structures suffering roof damage.

The pattern across all four is the same, and it is the honest argument for spending on the assembly rather than the appearance. Coverings, cladding and light-frame roofs failed. Engineered buildings did not collapse.

## Is the everyday threat really hurricanes?

No, and planning around them alone misses most of the risk.

The Valley averages fewer than 30 thunderstorm days a year, with severe conditions on under a third of those. Between 1996 and 2021 the region recorded 540 hail and wind events, roughly 21 a year, peaking in May.

Downbursts are the primary wind threat here and occur far more frequently than tornado damage. That is a meaningful distinction, because a downburst produces straight-line damage in a concentrated area and is often mistaken for a small tornado by the people it hits.

Hail is real but distributed differently. The 29 March 2012 mid-Valley storm hit McAllen, Edinburg, Mission and Pharr with baseball-sized hail, damaged more than a thousand structures and ran to roughly $600 million insured. Regional wind and hail losses since 2012 exceed $750 million.

The honest framing for a coastal property is that when hail comes it comes hard, but wind is the everyday driver. Which is why fastening, edge detail and substrate matter more here than impact ratings do.

## How do you avoid being taken advantage of?

By looking for permanence rather than polish, and by refusing to be hurried.

Storm events draw crews from well outside this region. Some are competent and some are not, and the difference is invisible from a truck. The reliable signals are dull: how long the entity has existed, whether the address resolves to a real place, whether references can be located in this county from before the storm.

Pressure is the clearest warning. Urgency about signing today, a discount expiring this afternoon, or a request for substantial payment before anything is delivered. Legitimate contractors are busy after storms and do not need a decision within the hour.

Be especially careful with claim entanglement. A contractor offering to handle your claim, negotiate with your carrier, or cover your deductible is proposing arrangements ranging from unwise to unlawful. Keep the roofing contract and the insurance claim as two separate relationships.

Be sceptical of catastrophic damage documented only by photographs you cannot place. Ask to see it yourself where it is safe, or ask for images that clearly show location and context rather than close-ups that could be any roof.

And apply the certification test, below. It separates people who have completed coastal work properly from people who have not.

## Why does the windstorm filing matter most right now?

Because urgency is exactly the condition under which it gets skipped, and skipping it is the most expensive mistake available.

If the damage is severe enough that the work becomes a full replacement, the windstorm application has to reach the department before the tear-off starts. Not during, not at completion. Before.

Get that order right and the inspection is routine. Get it wrong and the only remaining path runs through a Texas-licensed professional engineer working after the fact, establishing what is beneath a finished surface through documentation, selective opening, or both. You pay for the investigation, any remediation it finds, and the certification.

Small repairs are different. Work under 100 square feet that does not touch decking or framing members sits within the inspection exemption. A repair that grows into decking work has changed category, and the correct response is to stop and re-scope rather than to press on.

The full mechanics are on the [windstorm certification](/compliance/windstorm-certification-wpi-8/) page, and the insurance consequences of a documentation gap are covered under [TWIA coverage requirements](/compliance/twia-coverage-requirements/).

Ask any storm contractor when the filing goes in. A vague answer tells you how many coastal replacements they have completed properly.

## What will your insurer want?

Evidence, most of which has to exist before you needed it.

The strongest items are the ones that cost nothing in advance: dated photographs of the undamaged building, records of previous roof work including what system was installed, any windstorm certificate, and any inspection or condition report predating the event.

After the event: dated photographs of the damage before anything is disturbed, the record of temporary protection and when it was performed, receipts for emergency work, and details of anyone who has been on the property.

Understand your own policy before you need it, particularly the wind and hail deductible, which is frequently structured differently from other perils and is not a small difference on a coastal property. That is a conversation with your agent.

What we do and do not do is worth stating plainly. We document conditions and our work thoroughly, and we will give you everything we produce. We do not adjust claims, determine coverage, negotiate with carriers on your behalf, or absorb deductibles.

## What does a permanent repair actually look like?

Matching the fix to the failure, which usually means details rather than field area.

Where the covering has failed locally on a sound assembly, the repair replaces the affected material and, critically, addresses whatever allowed it to fail. Shingles that lifted at an edge are telling you something about the edge detail or the fastening pattern, and replacing them without addressing that buys a repeat.

Where flashing, curbs or penetrations are the origin, the repair is at those details. This is the most common case and the one most often patched superficially.

Where decking is compromised, it is replaced before anything goes over it, because fastener holding values assume sound material and the wind rating depends on them.

On metal, storm work frequently means backed-out fasteners, damaged closures and lifted trim rather than panel failure. The substrate and fastener questions on the [metal roofing](/services/metal-roofing/) page apply to the repair as much as to a new roof.

And where the assessment says replacement, the [roof replacement](/services/roof-replacement/) page covers how that runs from filing to close-out.

## Should the rebuild be different from what was there?

Often, yes, and the roof being open is the cheapest moment to improve it.

Fastening is the first place to spend. Code requires at least four fasteners per strip shingle and six in high-wind zones at and above a defined threshold, and since the lowest zone anywhere in Cameron County already sits at that threshold, the six-fastener pattern governs county-wide. Perimeters and corners carry higher uplift than the field and deserve tighter patterns.

Edge metal and detailing deserve attention because that is where failures originate. Spending there returns more than upgrading the field material.

On impact resistance, be realistic. UL 2218 Class 4 means a two-inch steel ball dropped from 20 feet, twice on the same spot, with failure judged on the underside rather than on cosmetic surface damage, tested on new material. Manufacturers state plainly that impact-resistant shingles are not warranted to withstand hail damage, and insurance credits vary by state and company. Many insurers do offer one. Ask yours.

Wind ratings deserve the same scepticism. ASTM D3161 runs to Class F at 110 mph, ASTM D7158 to Class H at up to 190, and both standards state that results do not directly correlate to wind speeds experienced in service.

## What drives the cost of storm work?

Scope uncertainty, mostly, which is why the assessment is worth doing properly.

The knowns are straightforward: area affected, materials, access, and the details requiring rework. The unknown is what is underneath, and on storm work that unknown is larger than on planned work because water has usually been where it should not.

Deck condition is the main variable, and the right way to handle it is a stated unit rate agreed before anything is opened, so a worse-than-expected deck becomes arithmetic rather than a negotiation at a bad moment.

Emergency and temporary work is its own line and should be identified separately, since it is frequently recoverable in a claim when documented.

Timing affects price after a widespread event, because demand for materials and crews rises together. That is real, and it is also the cover under which pressure selling happens.

We are not the cheapest and do not try to be. On storm work specifically, the commitment that matters is that the scope reflects what the roof needs rather than what the moment allows.

## How long will it take, and why?

Longer than an ordinary job after a widespread event, and the reasons are worth understanding rather than resenting.

Immediately after a significant storm, every trade in the region is working the same week. Assessment appointments queue, materials tighten, and the crews available first are frequently the ones who arrived from elsewhere. That is the moment when patience is most valuable and hardest.

The permanent work then waits on several things in sequence: the assessment, the claim if there is one, the windstorm filing where a replacement is involved, materials, and any permit the jurisdiction requires. Several of those run in parallel if they are started together and in series if they are discovered one at a time.

Material lead time is the variable most often underestimated on metal and on anything specified rather than stocked. A panel profile in a coastal substrate is not something a supplier has sitting in the yard in quantity after a regional event.

What this means practically is that temporary protection has to be good enough to last, and that the sequencing conversation should happen at the first visit rather than the third. An honest contractor will tell you at the outset roughly where you sit in that queue.

It is also why a contractor promising to start tomorrow, in the week after a widespread event, is worth a question about what they are not doing for somebody else.

## What if the damage is to a commercial building?

The same principles apply and the documentation burden is heavier.

Business interruption changes the calculation entirely. A roof over an operating facility has to be secured and repaired around shifts, deliveries, tenants or production, so phasing is part of the scope from the first conversation rather than a scheduling detail.

The evidence requirements are greater because more parties ask for them: an insurer, possibly a lender, a corporate parent, and eventually a buyer. That means the system specified, the materials actually installed, manufacturer documentation, inspection records and the certification status all need to survive as a package.

Equipment on the roof complicates both the damage and the repair. Curbs, supports and penetrations are common failure origins, and units themselves may have been affected in ways that are not a roofer's call.

Low-slope specifics matter after a storm too. Ponding is defined in manufacturer warranty language as water that has not drained within 48 hours after precipitation ends, and a roof that has stopped draining has a different problem from one with a puncture. On Galvalume metal, ponding is an absolute exclusion.

## When should you not replace the roof?

When the evidence does not support it, and somebody should be willing to tell you so.

A localised failure on a sound assembly with dry material beneath is a repair. Cosmetic marks on a functioning roof are not damage requiring replacement, whatever a canvasser suggests. Age alone is not storm damage, and presenting it as such to a carrier is not a service anyone should accept.

Equally, do not defer work that is genuinely compounding. Active water entry does not wait for a better budget year, because in this climate vapour drive runs inward and a wet assembly does not dry the way northern intuition expects. From there it reaches the deck, and the deck is the expensive part.

The distinction between those two situations is what an honest assessment produces, and it is most of the value in calling somebody early.

There is a third case worth naming, because it is common and rarely discussed. A roof that was already near the end of its service life, and has now taken damage, sits in an awkward middle. The damage may be genuinely repairable while the roof itself has a year or two left. Repairing it is not wrong, and neither is replacing it, but the decision belongs to you with the facts in front of you rather than to whoever is holding the ladder. What you should expect from a contractor is both options priced, with the condition of the underlying assembly described plainly, so the choice is yours to make.
