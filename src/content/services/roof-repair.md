Most roof repairs fail for the same reason: somebody fixed the place where the water appeared rather than the place where it got in. That produces a repair that is genuinely competent, genuinely paid for, and completely ineffective.

Repair work is therefore a diagnostic trade before it is a construction one. The fixing part is usually straightforward. Knowing what to fix is the part worth paying for.

## Why is a leak so hard to locate?

Because water does not fall straight down once it is inside a roof assembly.

It travels. Along the underside of decking, down the flutes of a metal deck, across the tops of rafters or purlins, along insulation joints, following whatever path is available until something stops it or it finds a gap. The point at which it finally drops through and marks a ceiling can be a considerable distance from the breach.

That distance is why patching directly above a stain so often achieves nothing, and why the same ceiling can be patched three times by three contractors who each fixed something real.

Wind complicates it further. On this coast a great deal of water entry is driven rather than gravitational, pushed sideways under a lap or through a detail by pressure rather than falling onto it. That is why a roof can be watertight in an ordinary downpour and leak in a squall, and it points the investigation toward details rather than toward holes.

The practical consequence is that a proper repair starts with an investigation of the assembly, not with a bucket of sealant and a ladder positioned under the stain.

## How is a leak actually traced?

By working from the evidence rather than from the complaint.

The first step is establishing conditions. When does it leak — every rain, or only wind-driven rain, or only after prolonged rain? Does it start immediately or hours later? Has anything changed on the roof recently, like an equipment installation or a previous repair?

Then the roof itself gets examined at the places failures actually begin: penetrations, flashings, curbs, valleys, transitions between planes, edges and terminations. On metal, fasteners, closures and lap sealant. On low-slope, seams, drains and anything that has been cut into since installation.

Where the source is not evident, the assembly may need opening to establish what is wet and how far the moisture extends. That is not a failure of diagnosis; it is diagnosis. Knowing whether insulation is saturated across a wide area or damp in one place changes the recommendation entirely.

Interior evidence helps when read properly. The pattern of staining, whether it recurs in the same place, and whether it corresponds to a structural member above all narrow the search.

What does not work is guessing, sealing broadly and hoping. That approach produces roofs covered in patches, each marking a place somebody was wrong.

## Where do roofs actually fail?

At details, overwhelmingly. Not in the middle of a field of sound material.

Flashings are the most common origin: where the roof meets a wall, a chimney, a parapet or a change of plane. These are the junctions where two materials and two movements meet, and they are where installation quality shows.

Penetrations follow: vents, pipes, conduits, equipment supports, anything passing through the surface. Each one is a hole that has been deliberately made and then sealed, and sealant is the component with the shortest life on any roof.

Edges and terminations matter more here than in calmer climates, because uplift concentrates at perimeters and corners. Edge metal that has lifted is both a leak and a warning about wind performance.

Valleys concentrate water volume, so a marginal detail there fails before the same detail elsewhere.

On metal specifically, backed-out fasteners are the classic finding, particularly on exposed-fastener systems where restrained thermal movement works the screws loose over years. On low-slope, drains and the areas around them are the usual suspects, along with anything installed after the original roof went on.

The pattern is consistent: the roof fails where somebody had to make a decision, not where somebody simply laid material.

## When is a repair the right answer?

When the failure is localised and what sits beneath it is sound and dry.

That is the honest test, and it is a genuinely common situation. A lifted flashing, a failed penetration seal, storm-displaced shingles over an intact underlayment, a punctured membrane on a dry assembly — all of these are repairs, and treating them as replacements is the behaviour that has cost this trade its reputation.

A repair is also the right answer when the roof has real service life left and the budget is better spent elsewhere this year. Roofs do not have to be replaced the moment they develop a problem.

And it is right when the objective is buying defined time. An owner planning to replace in two years, who needs the building dry until then, is well served by a repair that is scoped and priced as exactly that rather than sold as a permanent fix.

What makes any of those decisions sound is knowing the condition of the assembly below the visible failure. Which is the whole argument for investigating before quoting.

## When is a repair not the right answer?

When the assembly beneath has already failed, however small the visible damage looks.

Widespread wet insulation is the clearest case. Once moisture is through a large area, the thermal value is gone and does not come back, and in this climate it does not dry out — vapour drive here runs inward for most of the year, so the assembly cannot shed moisture in the direction the weather is pushing. From there the deck begins to degrade, and the fastener holding values that the wind rating depends on degrade with it.

Repairing the surface over that condition seals the problem in. It is money spent to make a failure invisible for a season.

The second case is a roof at the end of its service life with failures appearing in multiple unrelated places. That pattern is the assembly telling you something, and continuing to patch it is a slow, expensive way of arriving at the same replacement.

The third is where the original installation was wrong in a way a repair cannot correct — the wrong substrate for a coastal exposure, or fastening that never met the pattern required here.

An honest contractor should be willing to say all of this, including when it means a larger job. And equally willing to say the opposite when the evidence supports a repair.

## What does the windstorm rule mean for repairs?

That most genuine repairs sit outside the inspection requirement, and that the exemption is narrower than it sounds.

The exemption covers repairs under 100 square feet, provided the work leaves decking and framing members untouched. A full replacement is outside it.

The unit is square feet, not roofing squares. A roofing square is 100 square feet, so the commonly repeated misreading describes something a hundred times larger than the actual threshold. That error always runs in the direction that lets somebody skip a step.

The second half of the test carries as much weight as the area. Work that touches decking or framing is outside the exemption even below the threshold, which matters because a repair frequently discovers a compromised deck once it is opened. At that point the job has changed category, and the correct response is to stop and re-scope rather than to press on and finish quickly.

Where a repair is genuinely borderline, the conservative reading is the cheap one. Treating it as requiring inspection costs an inspection. Treating it as exempt when it was not can cost an engineer, because after close-in only a licensed professional engineer can certify.

The full mechanics are on the [windstorm certification](/compliance/windstorm-certification-wpi-8/) page.

## What does the coast do to repair work?

It makes materials and fasteners matter on jobs small enough that people stop thinking about them.

A repair introduces new material into an existing assembly, and the same corrosion rules apply to a handful of fasteners as to a whole roof. Within 1,500 feet of saltwater, steel-based metal products carry no substrate warranty, and fastener grade has to match the substrate rather than whatever is in the van.

Galvanized fasteners against aluminium cladding are not recommended, and the martensitic stainless commonly stocked is not coastal-rated. Pairing dissimilar metals during a small repair is an easy way to start galvanic corrosion at exactly the point that was just fixed.

Airborne chlorides are hygroscopic and hold moisture against surfaces long after rain stops, so corrosion tracks deposition and wash-off rather than distance alone. Sheltered areas that rain never scours degrade faster than exposed ones — which is where a lot of repairs are needed and where new metal will sit unwashed.

Sealants have a harder life here too, under sustained heat and UV, which is part of why detail repairs recur.

None of that makes repairs inadvisable. It means a repair specified casually will not last as long as one specified properly, and the difference in material cost on a small job is trivial.

## What does a repair actually involve?

Diagnosis, then correction of the cause, then verification.

Diagnosis establishes the entry point and the extent of any damage beneath, as described above. This is the part that determines whether everything after it works.

Correction addresses the cause rather than the symptom. If shingles lifted at an edge, the repair replaces them and addresses whatever allowed them to lift — fastening pattern, edge detail, or an underlying condition. If a flashing failed, the repair remakes the flashing rather than sealing over it.

Where damaged decking is found, it is replaced before anything goes back over it, because fastener holding values assume sound material.

Materials are matched as closely as available, with an honest statement up front about what the match will look like. Weathering changes colour, and some profiles are discontinued. An owner told this in advance is rarely unhappy; one told afterwards usually is.

Verification means knowing the repair worked, which sometimes means waiting for weather and sometimes means testing. A repair signed off without either is a hope rather than a result.

Documentation closes it out: what was found, what was done, and photographs. That record matters later, particularly if a claim or a sale is in the future.

## What drives the price of a repair?

Access, diagnosis, extent and detail complexity — rarely the material.

Access frequently dominates on small jobs. A two-storey roof, a steep pitch, restricted approach or an occupied commercial building all change the method before any work happens.

Diagnosis is real work and it is legitimate to pay for it. A contractor who quotes a repair without investigating is either guessing or has already decided to sell you something larger.

Extent is what investigation establishes, and it is why a repair price sometimes cannot be given definitively until the assembly is opened. The honest way to handle that is a stated rate for what may be found, agreed in advance.

Detail complexity matters more than area. Remaking a chimney flashing properly is more work than replacing a considerably larger area of field material.

And material specification, which on the coast is not the place to economise even on a small job.

What we will not do is quote a repair sight-unseen. A number given without seeing the roof is a guess with room built into it.

## Does a repair affect a warranty or a certificate?

It can affect both, and the answer is worth checking before authorising work rather than after.

On the manufacturer side, most system warranties carry conditions about who may work on the roof and what materials may be used. A repair performed with incompatible material, or by someone outside an authorised programme, can compromise coverage that was otherwise still running. If your roof is within a warranty period, read the document or ask whoever holds it before anyone opens anything.

On the workmanship side, a contractor's own warranty on a previous installation may be affected by another party working on the same roof. That is not obstruction; it is that they can no longer be responsible for an assembly somebody else has altered.

On certification, a repair within the exemption does not disturb an existing windstorm certificate. The certificate describes the work it covered, and a small repair leaving decking and framing untouched does not change that. Where a repair grows into replacement territory, the certification question opens up, which is covered above.

There is also a documentation point that costs nothing. Keep the record of every repair — what was found, what was done, dated photographs. That record supports a future claim, answers a buyer's question, and tells the next contractor where somebody has already been. Roofs that have been repaired repeatedly by different people, with no record kept, are considerably harder and more expensive to diagnose.

## How do you tell a good repair from a cheap one?

By what it addresses and what it leaves behind.

A good repair identifies and corrects the cause. A cheap one covers the symptom, usually with sealant, in the place where the water appeared. Sealant has a legitimate role in roofing as a component within a detail; it is not a repair method in its own right, and a roof with visible smears of it across the surface is a roof that has been guessed at repeatedly.

A good repair reinstates the detail as it should have been built. Flashing remade, penetration properly reflashed, fastening pattern corrected. A cheap one adds a layer over the failure and relies on adhesion to material that is already compromised.

A good repair uses material suited to the exposure, including fastener grade near saltwater. A cheap one uses what was in the van.

A good repair reports what it found underneath, including bad news. A cheap one does not look.

The tell for an owner is usually the explanation. A contractor who can describe why the failure happened, and what the repair changes about that, has diagnosed something. One who describes only what they will apply has not, and the difference between those two answers is worth more than any price comparison.

## What should you do before we arrive?

Four things, none of which require going on the roof.

Note the conditions. Which rain produces the leak, how soon after it starts, and whether it has changed over time. That information narrows the search more than anything visible from the driveway.

Photograph the interior damage, dated, before cleaning up. If it recurs, photograph it each time — the pattern is diagnostic.

Find any documentation on the roof: when it was installed, by whom, what system, any previous repairs, and any windstorm certificate. Previous repair history is especially useful, because it tells us where somebody has already looked.

Protect the interior. Move what can be moved, contain the water, and if the ceiling is holding water, be aware it may release suddenly.

Then stay off the roof. Assessment from the ground is all you need to establish, and a wet or damaged roof is not a safe place to satisfy curiosity.

## What about repairs on a roof somebody else installed?

Routine, and there are two things worth knowing before you call.

The first is that we will tell you what we find, including if the original installation is the reason for the failure. That is sometimes an uncomfortable conversation, particularly if the roof is recent, but you are better served knowing that the substrate was wrong for the exposure, or the fastening pattern never met what this county requires, than being handed a patch and an invoice.

The second is that if the roof is still within a workmanship warranty from whoever installed it, your first call should be to them rather than to us. That is not us declining work. It is that you have a contractual right worth using, and bringing in another contractor can compromise it. We will say so if that appears to be the situation.

Where the original installer is no longer trading, which is common enough in this market, that route closes and the repair becomes an ordinary job. Bring whatever documentation exists, because it tells us what system we are working with and what materials will be compatible.

On manufacturer warranties the same logic applies in reverse: some require work to be performed within an authorised programme, and doing otherwise can end coverage that still had years to run. Worth ten minutes with the paperwork before authorising anything.

## Does a small repair need a permit?

Sometimes, and it varies more across this county than most owners expect.

Jurisdictions differ on what triggers a permit for roofing work, and the answer for a repair is frequently different from the answer for a replacement. A city may require nothing for a minor repair and a full application for a re-roof, while the unincorporated county applies its own rules entirely.

Because the boundaries here are irregular, particularly on the fringes of Brownsville and Harlingen, the first thing to establish is whether the property sits inside a city at all. A mailing address does not settle it. That question decides which building department applies, and it is covered on the [Cameron County permits](/compliance/cameron-county-permits/) page.

For a genuine small repair the practical answer is usually straightforward, and we confirm it rather than assuming. Where a repair grows — a soft deck discovered under a patch, an area larger than it first appeared — both the permit position and the windstorm position can change at the same moment, which is another reason a repair that expands is a repair worth pausing on.

## When should you call rather than wait?

When water is actively entering, and when the weather is about to make it worse.

Active entry compounds. Every hour of water reaching insulation, ceilings, contents and electrical systems converts a roof problem into a building problem, and the building problem is the expensive one.

Anything storm-related also warrants a call rather than a form, because it needs triage rather than a queue position. The [storm and wind damage](/services/storm-and-wind-damage-repairs/) page covers what to do in the first hours.

Less urgent, but worth not deferring: a leak that has appeared for the first time, any sagging or soft spot in a ceiling, and visible daylight through a roof from inside.

Genuinely fine to schedule: a known minor issue in dry weather, routine maintenance, and anything you have been living with for a year that is not getting worse. The distinction that matters is not how large the problem looks but whether it is changing, because a stable fault waits comfortably and a worsening one does not.
