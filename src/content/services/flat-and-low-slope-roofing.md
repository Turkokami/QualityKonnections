Low-slope roofing is judged on things nobody looks at. Nobody stands in a car park admiring a membrane. What an owner experiences is either nothing at all for twenty years, or a leak above a server room on a Tuesday.

That makes it a specification business rather than a selling business, and it makes the differences between systems worth understanding properly, because almost everything repeated about them in this trade is either oversimplified or backwards.

## What counts as low-slope, and why does it matter?

Anything below the minimum where shingles become viable, which is the point at which the whole approach to keeping water out changes.

Steep-slope roofing sheds water. Gravity does the work, materials overlap like scales, and small imperfections are forgiven because water does not linger. Asphalt shingles need at least two inches of rise per foot, with double underlayment required between two and four.

Low-slope roofing contains water instead. The membrane is a continuous barrier rather than a set of overlapping pieces, and every seam, penetration and edge is a potential entry point that gravity will not rescue.

The code minimums establish where each system can go. Built-up roofing, modified bitumen, and thermoset and thermoplastic single-ply systems all run down to a quarter of an inch per foot, which is two percent. Standing seam metal reaches the same quarter-inch. Lapped-seam metal panels without sealant need three inches per foot.

Below a system's stated minimum you are outside its own installation instructions. That is not a matter of degree; it is the point where the manufacturer's obligations stop and yours begin.

The practical consequence for an owner is that slope is the first question, not the last. It filters the options before any preference about material enters the conversation, and a bid proposing a system below its minimum has already answered the wrong question.

## What is actually limiting TPO?

Heat, not ultraviolet exposure. This is the single most repeated error in commercial roofing and it is worth correcting carefully.

Research published through the roofing association found that UV ageing does not appear to be an issue for TPO membranes. What governs service life is sustained temperature. That same work put utility-grade material at roughly 15 years at 160 degrees Fahrenheit sustained, standard grades near 25 years at 180 degrees, and the most heat-stable formulations beyond 35 years at around 200 degrees.

Read those numbers as a spread rather than a promise. The important finding is not any single figure but the size of the gap between grades, because it means the category name tells you very little.

TPO is explicitly a varied product. Heat-ageing performance differs materially between manufacturers, and two membranes both correctly described as TPO can have substantially different expected lives on the same roof. A specification naming the category without naming the product has not specified anything.

For an owner on this coast the practical translation is straightforward. Ask which manufacturer and which grade, ask what the membrane thickness is, and understand that a cheaper TPO bid is frequently cheaper because it is a lower-grade membrane rather than because the contractor is more efficient.

Roof surface temperature is also a design input. Colour, insulation and ventilation all affect what the membrane actually experiences, which is why the same product performs differently on two buildings in the same town.

## What is modified bitumen good at?

Redundancy, toughness and repairability, which are the grounds it should be argued on.

Modified bitumen is a multi-ply system, and that redundancy is its central virtue. A single breach in one ply does not put water into the building the way a breach in a single-ply membrane can. On a roof carrying regular foot traffic, equipment servicing or the general abuse of a working building, that matters.

It also tolerates puncture better than thin single-ply, and it repairs readily with materials that bond to what is already there. On a large roof expected to be patched over its life rather than replaced wholesale, ease of repair is an underrated economic property.

What we will not publish is a service life figure. No association-published number exists for modified bitumen, and every twenty-to-thirty-year range circulating online traces back to contractor marketing rather than to research. Where a specific membrane carries a manufacturer warranty term, that term is the honest number to quote, and it belongs to that product rather than to the category.

That refusal costs us a comparison table that competitors publish freely. It also means nothing on this page will be contradicted by the first person who checks.

Where modified bitumen loses is on reflectivity, weight and installation conditions. Those are real trade-offs and worth weighing against the redundancy rather than pretending they do not exist.

## What does ponding actually do?

Less than the blanket claim suggests, and more than an optimistic contractor admits.

Start with the definition, which comes from manufacturer warranty language rather than from an industry body: water that does not drain or dissipate from the roof surface within 48 hours after precipitation ends. Attribute it correctly, because it is a warranty term rather than a code requirement.

Then the claim that ponding voids your warranty, which is not universally true. At least one major manufacturer's premium no-dollar-limit commercial guarantee expressly covers leaks in ponding areas on TPO and PVC. On Galvalume metal, by contrast, ponding is an absolute exclusion. Two systems, opposite answers, same weather.

So the accurate position is that the governing document decides, and the only way to know is to read the one attached to your roof.

What ponding certainly does, warranty aside, is accelerate everything. It concentrates dirt and biological growth, holds heat differently across the surface, adds load, and puts standing water permanently over whichever seam happens to sit in the low spot. A roof that has stopped draining has a different and usually worse problem than one with a puncture.

Which is why drainage design is part of a low-slope roof rather than an accessory to it. Tapered insulation, correctly positioned drains, adequate scuppers and overflow provision are how the 48 hours gets respected, and they belong in the bid.

## How does this climate change the assembly?

By reversing an assumption most published detailing is built on.

Cameron County sits in a hot-humid climate zone, and vapour drive here runs inward. Outside air is warm and wet, the conditioned interior is cool and dry, and moisture pushes from outside toward inside for most of the year. Cold-climate detailing assumes the opposite, and a great deal of roofing advice was written for the opposite.

The code has responded specifically. Low-permeance vapour retarders are prohibited on the interior side of air-permeable insulation in this zone and its neighbours. A detail that is mandatory in a northern state is not permitted here, because it traps moisture against the wrong surface and prevents the assembly drying in the direction the climate is pushing.

Energy provisions apply too. Low-slope roofs over cooled conditioned space are subject to reflectance and emittance minimums, with three-year-aged solar reflectance of at least 0.55 and thermal emittance of at least 0.75, or an aged solar reflectance index of at least 64. Exceptions exist and the applicable code edition is a local question, covered on the [Cameron County permits](/compliance/cameron-county-permits/) page.

Where rigid board sits above the deck, minimum thermal values exist for condensation control rather than for energy performance. That distinction matters: reducing the board to save money reintroduces the condensation the assembly was designed to avoid.

The summary for an owner is that a low-slope assembly here needs to dry inward, and any detail preventing that is a defect however standard it looks in a generic manufacturer drawing.

## What sits under the membrane?

Deck, then insulation, then cover board — and each one decides something the membrane cannot.

The deck determines fastener holding values, which is what the wind rating ultimately depends on. On a re-roof, deck condition is frequently the limiting element and it is not fully knowable until the existing system comes off.

Insulation carries the thermal performance and, on most low-slope roofs, the slope itself where tapered systems are used to build fall into a structurally flat deck. Wet insulation is the quiet killer of low-slope roofing: it loses thermal value, it does not dry in this climate, and it degrades the fastener holding capacity that the whole assembly relies on.

Cover board sits between insulation and membrane, protecting the membrane from the insulation below and providing a stable substrate for adhesion or fastening. It is one of the more common items to be quietly omitted from a cheap bid.

Attachment method — mechanically fastened, fully adhered, or ballasted — changes wind performance, cost and how the roof behaves. It should be named in a bid.

An owner reading two quotes should compare these four items before comparing membrane brands. Two bids naming the same membrane can describe materially different roofs, and the difference lives underneath.

## What do the wind ratings mean here?

Four different tests, and bids cite whichever flatters the system.

UL 580 is pass or fail against a threshold, so it tells you something cleared a bar and nothing about margin. UL 1897 loads to failure, which is more informative, but excludes the deck from the assembly — a significant omission on a re-roof where the deck is often the weak link.

FM 4471 ratings already carry a safety factor of two, so a 1-90 rating corresponds to 45 pounds per square foot allowable. Quoting the headline figure as the working load overstates the system twofold. ASTM E1592 tests the complete assembly to failure including anchors and deflection, and is generally regarded as the most reliable basis for standing seam design.

The single most useful question about any quoted rating is which standard produced it and whether the deck was included.

On this coast, wind performance on low-slope roofing concentrates at the perimeter and corners, where uplift is significantly higher than in the field. Enhanced fastening in those zones is standard practice for good reason, and a bid pricing uniform field attachment across the whole roof has priced an average rather than a design.

## Does the salt air matter on a membrane roof?

Less to the membrane than to everything else up there.

The membrane itself is comparatively indifferent to chloride. What is not indifferent is the metal: edge metal, counter-flashing, drain and scupper components, equipment curbs, fasteners and anything mechanical sitting on the roof.

Airborne chlorides are hygroscopic, holding moisture against a surface long after the weather clears, so corrosion tracks deposition and wash-off rather than distance alone. On a low-slope roof that means sheltered metal — the lee side of curbs, undersides of flashings, anywhere rain does not scour — degrades faster than exposed surfaces.

Practically, that pushes edge metal and flashing specification toward corrosion-appropriate materials near the water, and it makes rinsing sheltered components a genuine maintenance item rather than an upsell.

It also affects the equipment. Rooftop condensing units, exhaust fans and their supports corrode, and their failure frequently becomes a roofing problem when somebody cuts into the membrane to replace them.

## How does the job run, start to finish?

In a sequence where the investigation matters more than on a steep-slope job.

Assessment establishes slope and drainage, existing system and its condition, and — critically — whether the insulation below is wet and over what area. A moisture survey is worth its cost on any roof where that is uncertain, because it determines whether this is a recover, a partial tear-off or a full replacement.

Filing and permits follow, before anything is removed. Windstorm application where a full replacement applies, and permits with whichever authority has jurisdiction.

Material is ordered to the named specification: membrane manufacturer, grade and thickness, insulation type and thickness, cover board, attachment method, edge metal.

Tear-off or preparation exposes the deck, and any deck repair happens at the agreed unit rate rather than as an improvised conversation.

The system goes down in order, with drainage components set correctly, and every penetration, curb and transition detailed. Those details are where the large majority of low-slope leaks originate.

Inspection has to happen while the assembly is still observable, so it is scheduled into the sequence rather than after it. Then close-out: certificate where applicable, permit records, manufacturer documentation for what was installed, and warranty registration actually completed.

## Where does the money go on a low-slope roof?

Six things, and area is only one of them.

The system and its grade, which on TPO specifically can move the price substantially without changing the word on the bid.

Insulation thickness and whether tapered insulation is being used to build slope, which is frequently the largest single line on a low-slope job and the one most often value-engineered out.

Deck condition, unknown until opened, which is why the unit rate belongs in the contract.

Penetrations and details. A roof with twenty curbs, drains and vents is a different job from a clear expanse of the same square footage, because labour concentrates at details.

Access and logistics, particularly on occupied commercial buildings where material has to reach the roof without crossing operations.

And the compliance track: filing, inspection coordination and the specification required to pass it.

Competing on price is not what this business does, and low-slope work is where that shows most clearly. What we commit to is a scope specific enough that there is nothing left to revise once the roof is open.

## Where do low-slope roofs actually leak?

Five failure modes, and none of them is the membrane failing in the field.

Details. Curbs, drains, penetrations, edges and transitions are where low-slope roofs leak, and a bid pricing field area generously while treating details as incidental has priced the easy part.

Drainage that does not work, producing ponding that concentrates every other problem. Prevented by designing fall and drainage rather than assuming the deck provides it.

Wet insulation left in place under a new membrane or a coating, which seals the problem in. Prevented by surveying for moisture rather than guessing.

Attachment that ignores perimeter and corner uplift, which fails in the first serious wind event at exactly the place uplift concentrates.

And the assembly that cannot dry inward, because a detail imported from a colder climate trapped moisture on the wrong side.

## When is a coating the right answer, and when is it not?

Right when the existing system is sound and dry and the objective is extending service life. Wrong whenever it is being used to avoid finding out what is underneath.

A coating over a sound, dry, well-adhered membrane can genuinely add years, improve reflectivity and buy time on a capital plan. That is a legitimate product used properly.

A coating over wet insulation is a way of spending money to make a problem invisible. The water does not leave, the insulation does not recover, the deck continues to degrade, and the eventual bill is larger for the delay.

The determining question is therefore not the condition of the surface but the condition of what sits beneath it, which brings the moisture survey back to the centre of the decision.

Be cautious of coating proposals that arrive without any investigation of the assembly, and of any product presented as a universal solution regardless of the substrate.

## What maintenance does a low-slope roof actually need?

Little, done regularly, which is why it gets skipped until it is expensive.

Drains, scuppers and overflows need clearing. Blocked drainage is the most common cause of ponding, and ponding concentrates every other problem on the roof. On a building with trees nearby or a dusty industrial setting, that is a scheduled task rather than an annual thought.

Details need looking at: sealant at penetrations, condition of curbs and their flashings, edge metal, and anything that has been cut into since the roof was installed. Equipment changes are a frequent source of new penetrations made by people who are not roofers.

The surface needs walking after any significant weather, particularly on a building where a leak would be expensive. Punctures from debris are easy to find deliberately and hard to find accidentally.

And records need keeping. A roof with a maintenance history is easier to diagnose, easier to warranty and easier to sell than one without.

None of that requires a contract with anyone, though on a large commercial roof a scheduled programme usually pays for itself in avoided emergency work. What it requires is that somebody is responsible for it, which on many buildings is nobody. Naming that person, whether in-house or a contractor, is the cheapest single thing an owner can do for a low-slope roof. It costs nothing, it is usually the step that has never been taken, and it is the difference between finding a puncture in March and finding it through a ceiling tile in September.

## When is low-slope the wrong conversation entirely?

When the roof needs a repair, when the slope allows something better, or when the real problem is drainage.

A localised failure on a sound assembly is a repair, covered on the [roof repair](/services/roof-repair/) page, and routing it into a full replacement is behaviour this market already has too much of.

Where slope permits, and particularly near the coast, a metal system may serve better and longer than a membrane. The substrate and fastener reasoning on the [metal roofing](/services/metal-roofing/) page decides whether that is true for your building.

And where the complaint is water standing on the roof, the answer is sometimes drainage work rather than a new roof. Clearing drains and correcting fall can resolve what looks like a membrane problem.
