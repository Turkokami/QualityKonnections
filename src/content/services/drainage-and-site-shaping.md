Drainage is the most consequential dirt work there is and the least visible. A pad that is two inches low is an inconvenience. Water that runs the wrong way for twenty years is a foundation problem, a mould problem, and eventually somebody else's lawsuit.

On this ground it is also unusually difficult, for reasons that have nothing to do with skill. The land is flat, the clay does not absorb, and the place your water is going belongs to somebody with rules about it.

## Why is drainage harder here than elsewhere?

Three conditions compound, and each one alone would be manageable.

The ground is flat. Across much of this region there is very little natural fall to work with, which means drainage has to be built rather than found. On a sloping site gravity forgives small errors; on ground this level a quarter of an inch in the wrong place produces a puddle that lasts three days.

The soil does not absorb. Much of the region sits on clays with very slow permeability, and in the poorly drained series runoff ranges from very slow to outright ponded. Water that would infiltrate on a sandy site simply sits here. Seasonal high water tables within a few feet of the surface compound it, because there is nowhere for infiltrating water to go either.

And the receiving system is somebody else's. Most developable land here drains into a canal, drain or channel operated by a district with genuine rulemaking authority over its easements. Where your water goes, at what rate, and how it connects are not entirely your decisions.

The result is that drainage here is a designed system rather than a graded surface, and it involves at least one authority most owners have never heard of before they need one.

## What does the ground actually do with water?

Very little, which is the whole problem.

The Vertisols across much of the region shrink and swell with moisture, and when dry they crack open. Harlingen clay has cracks that stay open 150 to 210 cumulative days in most years. Those cracks take water rapidly at first, then swell shut, at which point the surface becomes effectively impermeable partway through a storm.

That behaviour is why rainfall here often produces little runoff early and a great deal of it later in the same event. A site that appeared to drain during the first inch stops draining during the second.

Benito clay is poorly drained with runoff from very slow to ponded and a seasonal high water table 4 to 6 feet down from September through May. Nearer the bay, Lomalta clay is poorly drained and semi-marshy, sitting slightly above sea level.

Underneath much of the Brownsville area the mapped geology is Holocene muddy floodplain alluvium and low-permeability Beaumont mud and clay. That is the parent material behind a lot of drainage behaviour people experience as inexplicable in their own yard.

The straightforward sites exist too. Laredo silty clay loam, Willacy fine sandy loam and Hidalgo sandy clay loam are well drained and moderately permeable. Establishing which you are on is worth doing before assuming the difficult version.

## What does a drainage design actually consist of?

Fall, conveyance, storage where required, and a legal outfall.

Fall is the shaping: getting the ground surface to move water in a chosen direction, away from structures and toward wherever it is meant to leave. On flat ground this is precision work rather than rough grading, and it is why site shaping and drainage are the same conversation.

Conveyance carries the water once it is moving — swales, ditches, pipes, culverts under drives and access points. Sizing matters, and so does maintaining fall along the whole run rather than only at the ends.

Storage is detention, needed when the volume or rate leaving the site exceeds what is permitted. It can be a pond, an underground system, or storage designed into the grading itself. It consumes land and changes layout, which is why it belongs in the design early rather than late.

The outfall is where water leaves, and it is the part with the most rules attached. An engineered outfall of specified size, at a specified elevation, with erosion protection, discharging at the permitted rate.

Everything upstream of the outfall is constrained by it. Which is why the district conversation happens before the site plan is drawn rather than after.

## Who decides how much water may leave your site?

A drainage district, in most of this region, and they set two numbers that together drive the design.

The first is an allowable run-out rate: how much water the district's system is designed to accept from an acre of land in their area. The second is the permitted net increase for development, usually differentiated by density, since lower-density residential on larger lots generates less impervious cover than dense development.

Where your projected discharge exceeds the allowance, the difference has to be held on site and released slowly. That is the detention decision, and it is arithmetic rather than opinion.

Cameron County contains drainage districts numbered 1, 3, 4, 5 and 6, alongside irrigation districts including Bayview, Brownsville, Cameron County numbers 2, 6 and 16, Harlingen, Hidalgo and Cameron Counties number 9, and La Feria. A single tract can have an irrigation lateral on one boundary and a drainage channel on another, each with its own district and its own process.

## What are the rules on connecting to a channel?

Specific, and one of them is absolute.

Open ditch connections to existing channels are prohibited outright. An unengineered open connection erodes at the confluence, undercuts the bank, deposits sediment where the district's equipment has to work, and creates a maintenance liability at the worst possible point.

What is required instead is an engineered outfall: specified size and elevation, erosion protection, and discharge at the permitted rate.

Crossings have their own geometry. Utility lines crossing a district easement must run close to perpendicular rather than obliquely, pass a specified depth below the existing flowline of the channel rather than merely below ground level, and be protected, typically by encasement.

None of that is adjustable on site. It is designed in, approved in advance, and inspected. A crossing installed to a crew's judgement and discovered later becomes a removal and reinstallation.

Historic arrangements do not establish rights. Water may have left a property the same way for fifty years, and that predates the current rules without creating permission to replicate it.

## What do the resacas change?

They add a water body that is simultaneously an amenity, a drainage facility and an actively managed asset.

The resacas are former channels of the Rio Grande, severed by flood control works including Falcon Dam, which also prevented new ones forming. Roughly 3,500 acres remain against an estimated 70,000 before agricultural expansion in the 1870s. The Corps of Engineers evaluated 66 near Brownsville, ranging from under an acre to over 50 and averaging about 18. The three that run through the urban core are the Resaca de la Guerra, the Resaca del Rancho Viejo and the Town Resaca.

They are not decorative. Brownsville's utilities board manages over 50 miles of the city's historic resaca waterways, holding something like four million cubic yards of sediment, under a 75-year maintenance plan begun in 2013. The system carries irrigation, raw water supply, stormwater drainage and recreation simultaneously.

For a property owner near one, that means alteration of the bank or adjacent ground is constrained, discharge into it is governed rather than assumed, and maintenance access has to be preserved.

## How does drainage relate to the building itself?

Directly, and this is where drainage failures become structural ones.

Water standing against a foundation on expansive clay produces exactly the repeated wetting and drying that causes differential movement. One edge of a structure sits in wet soil that swells while another sits in soil that dries and shrinks. The building responds, and the response appears as cracking.

That is why positive fall away from a structure on all sides is not a finishing touch. It is part of the foundation's working environment for the life of the building.

Roof drainage feeds into it. Downspouts discharging at the base of a wall concentrate a large catchment into a small area of soil, and on this ground that concentration matters more than it would elsewhere. Carrying that water away from the structure is cheap and frequently omitted.

Local ordinance often addresses the interaction between properties directly. Harlingen requires finished floors six inches above neighbouring lots within 100 feet of a drainage inlet, alongside its 18-inch and 24-inch above-curb foundation requirements. Those rules exist to manage what happens between adjacent properties rather than on any one of them.

Pad elevation and drainage are therefore a single design problem, covered from the other side on the [building pads](/services/building-pads/) page.

## What does the work actually involve?

Survey, design, approvals, then earthwork, in that order.

A survey establishes existing elevations across the site on a common datum, which is what makes fall calculable rather than estimated. On ground this flat, that measurement is the foundation of everything after it.

Design establishes where water will go, at what rate, through what conveyance, into which outfall, with detention if the calculations require it. On anything of scale this is engineering work.

Approvals follow: district approval for discharge and any crossing, a floodplain development permit where the site is in a mapped hazard area, city or county permitting, and stormwater authorisation on larger disturbances. Those run in parallel, and each has its own lead time.

Then the earthwork. Cut and fill to establish the designed surface, swales and ditches shaped to the specified fall, pipes and culverts set at design elevations, structures built, and erosion control maintained throughout.

Verification closes it: elevations checked against design, and the system observed in real rain where possible. A drainage system signed off in dry weather has been measured but not tested.

## What sets the cost of a drainage job?

Volume, structures, approvals and access.

Volume of material moved, which on shaping work is driven by how much the existing surface differs from the designed one across the whole site. Small changes in target elevation over a large area produce large volumes.

Structures: culverts, inlets, outfall works, pipe and detention. These are discrete costs that scale with the design rather than with the area.

Approvals, which cost time more than money but are frequently the schedule driver. District approvals run on meeting calendars, with submissions required a set period in advance.

Access and conditions. Clay this heavy does not work in the days after significant rain, and this region's rainfall arrives as a small number of intense events rather than steady drizzle, with fewer than 30 thunderstorm days a year and severe conditions on under a third of those.

And engineering, where the project needs a designed system rather than corrective shaping.

Lowest bid is not what this business competes on, and drainage is where that matters most: a cheap quote here usually means shaping without design, which relocates water rather than resolving it.

## Which drainage mistakes are the expensive ones?

Five failures, and most of them are design failures rather than construction ones.

Insufficient fall, so water moves too slowly or not at all. Prevented by surveying and designing rather than eyeballing.

Fall that reverses partway along a run, usually because the middle of a swale was never checked. Prevented by verifying elevations along the whole length, not only at the ends.

Water relocated rather than resolved — moved efficiently off one property and onto a neighbour, or into a channel at a rate the district does not permit. Prevented by knowing the allowable discharge before designing.

Outfalls that erode, because a connection was made without the specified protection.

And the sequencing failure: earthwork designed and priced before the district's requirements were known, then redrawn. That is the most common expensive mistake on commercial sites here.

## What does erosion control have to do with it?

It is the other half of moving water, and on a site of any size it is a legal obligation rather than good practice.

Construction that disturbs one or more acres generally requires authorisation under the state construction general permit, with a site-specific plan, controls installed and maintained, and inspections on a defined schedule. Smaller sites can still be caught where they form part of a larger common plan of development, which is a frequent trap on phased projects.

The reason it bites hard on drainage work specifically is that drainage work is disturbance. Cutting swales, shaping ground and building detention all open soil at precisely the moment the site's water is being redirected.

Valley clay makes it harder than the standard guidance assumes. Fine clay particles stay in suspension far longer than sand, so sediment travels well beyond the site and passes through barriers that would trap coarser material. Controls that work adequately on granular soils underperform here.

Sequencing is what actually protects a site. Controls installed before the ground is opened work; controls installed after the first storm are a response to damage already done. Progressive stabilisation of completed areas, rather than leaving everything bare until the end, reduces both exposure and the maintenance burden.

## Why does the receiving water's condition matter?

Because the systems this region drains into are already failing their standards, which raises the scrutiny on anything new.

The Arroyo Colorado carries formal impairments: dissolved oxygen on its tidal segment and bacteria on the segment above it. It is an ancient distributary of the Rio Grande running roughly 90 miles from Mission to the Laguna Madre, draining a watershed of about 706 square miles, and it functions as an integral part of a major floodway system. The Anzalduas diversion sends roughly a fifth of its 105,000 cubic feet per second down the Arroyo, some 21,000.

The Laguna Madre beyond it is a hypersaline lagoon of roughly 280,910 acres averaging about four and a half feet deep — shallow, poorly flushed and unsuited to absorbing sediment load.

For an owner this is not an abstraction. It is why districts and regulators take an interest in both the rate and the quality of what leaves a site, and why a drainage design that solves your standing water by sending it somewhere faster is not automatically an improvement.

It is also the honest answer to why the approvals exist. On this landscape, water leaving a property has a specific destination and a specific person responsible for maintaining it.

## What should you have before calling about a drainage problem?

Four things, and one of them you can only gather in the rain.

Observations during and after real rainfall. Where water arrives from, where it stands, how deep, how long it takes to clear, and whether it reaches any structure. That record is worth more than anything visible on a dry day, and photographs with dates are better than description.

Any survey of the property. Elevations on a common datum are what make fall calculable rather than estimated, and on ground this flat that measurement is the difference between a design and a guess.

Any plat or title document showing easements. Drainage and irrigation district easements frequently cross properties whose owners have no idea they are there, particularly on rural tracts that were once farmed.

And the jurisdiction question: whether the parcel sits inside a city or in unincorporated county. That determines which authority reviews the work, separately from whichever district governs the receiving channel.

Whether the problem has changed over time is useful too. Standing water that has appeared in the last two years usually has a cause — new construction upslope, a blocked culvert, a neighbour's regrading — and finding that cause is cheaper than engineering around it.

## When is the answer not drainage work?

More often than an owner assumes, and it is worth checking before moving dirt.

When the water originates elsewhere. Runoff arriving from a neighbouring property, a road, or a failed system upstream is not solved by regrading your lot, and doing so may simply move the argument. Establishing where the water is coming from before deciding what to build is the single most useful hour anyone can spend on a drainage complaint, and it is frequently the hour nobody spends. Walking the ground uphill of the problem during rain answers it faster than any amount of measurement afterwards.

When the problem is roof drainage. Gutters, downspouts and where they discharge account for a surprising share of complaints about standing water near a building, and that is a much cheaper fix.

When the water sits in a district easement. That ground is not yours to alter, and altering it can be a genuine problem.

When the issue is a blocked existing system rather than an inadequate one. Clearing a culvert, an inlet or a silted swale sometimes resolves what looks like a design failure, and it is worth ruling out before anyone proposes rebuilding the system that was working until it filled up.

And when the honest answer is that the site needs an engineer before it needs a contractor. On anything beyond corrective shaping around a single structure, the design has to come first, because moving dirt to a plan that has not been calculated is how a drainage problem becomes two drainage problems on adjoining properties. Saying that early costs a conversation. Discovering it after the earthwork costs the earthwork.
