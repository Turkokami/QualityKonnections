Almost everything expensive that goes wrong on a building here was decided by a specification the owner never read. The fastener grade. The base course thickness. Whether the fill was engineered. Which membrane, from which manufacturer, at what thickness. Those choices are invisible once the work is finished, and they determine how the building behaves for the next twenty years.

This library exists to make them visible. It is not a catalogue and it is not a sales tool. It is the reference material behind the work, written so that an owner can ask a bidder a question that has to be answered specifically.

## What is this library actually for?

Giving you the vocabulary to interrogate a bid.

The single biggest asymmetry in construction is that the buyer usually cannot evaluate the product until years after purchase. A roof that will fail in year eight looks identical on the day of completion to one that will last thirty. The only way to close that gap is to understand the specification, because the specification is the difference and it is knowable in advance.

So these entries are organised around decisions rather than products. What a system is. Where it genuinely belongs and where it does not. How it fails, and what that failure looks like from the ground. What owning it actually costs over time, including the maintenance nobody mentions at the point of sale. And the local angle, because a material that performs well in Dallas can be the wrong answer 500 miles south with salt in the air.

Where an entry cannot support a claim from a primary source, it says so. There are several such gaps across this site, deliberately left open. On a coast where a wrong number gets built into a structure, an acknowledged gap is a feature.

## How should you choose between roof systems?

Slope first, then exposure, then how the building is used, and only then preference.

Slope is a filter rather than a factor. Every system has a minimum below which its own installation instructions no longer apply, and installing below it is not a compromise, it is an unwarranted assembly. Asphalt shingles need at least two units of rise per twelve, with double underlayment required through the shallow end of their range. Lapped metal seams without sealant need three. Standing seam and the low-slope membrane families go considerably lower.

Exposure decides the material. Within 1,500 feet of saltwater the substrate question overrides nearly everything else, because the steel-based metal products carry no substrate warranty in that band. That distance is worth committing to memory precisely, since the figure drifts upward into miles in a great deal of published advice.

Use decides the details. A roof carrying mechanical equipment and regular foot traffic has different requirements from an unoccupied warehouse deck, and a system chosen without asking who walks on it will be repaired by people walking on it.

Preference comes last and legitimately exists. Appearance matters on a building people look at. It should just not be the first filter, and a contractor who leads with appearance is selling rather than specifying.

## What does a soil series actually tell you?

How the ground will move, drain and corrode — three separate questions with one answer set.

Movement is the first. The Vertisols across much of this region shrink and swell with moisture content, and the standard measure of that tendency shows up as high values on the shrink-swell soils in the area. Texas engineering practice draws the expansive line at a weighted plasticity index above fifteen with potential volume change over one percent. That is not a trivia figure. It is the threshold at which a pad becomes an engineered structure rather than a pile of compacted dirt.

Drainage is the second, and it is where names mislead. Two clays in the same taxonomic family can differ from moderately well drained to poorly drained with ponded runoff, and seasonal high water tables sit within a few feet of the surface across long stretches of the year in the wetter series. That governs when you can work, how a site behaves during construction, and whether a finished lot holds water against a slab.

Corrosion and chemistry is the third, and it is specific to the bay margin here. Sodic and saline profiles with high exchangeable sodium and wide-ranging electrical conductivity are common near the water. What we do not publish are inferences about sulphate attack on concrete or corrosion of buried metal, because those corollaries need a local geotechnical source and we do not have one. It is a real question. It is somebody's engineering determination, not our blog post.

The naming traps are worth carrying: the barrier island is genuinely sand, the similarly named mainland series near the lighthouse is a clay, and the Rio Grande series is coarse-silty, so the "silty clay loam" phase people quote does not exist.

## What does "done right" mean in a specification?

It means numbers that can be tested, tied to a standard, and produced on demand.

Compaction is the clean example. A percentage on its own is meaningless until you know which laboratory maximum it references, because the same figure describes different densities on different materials. Lift thickness belongs in the specification alongside the percentage, since material placed too deep does not densify through its full depth however many passes the roller makes. Federal guidance on fill supporting buildings caps lifts and gives an equivalence between the two common compaction standards, which is the cleanest citation available for it.

Base course is the second. Flexible base is specified by plasticity and durability limits by grade rather than by what the material is called, and quarried caliche is normally supplied against the lower grade. Caliche itself is secondary calcium carbonate, formed by leaching and reprecipitation through recognised morphologic stages, and quarried locally to improve drainage and traction on unpaved surfaces. That is its purpose. It is a base, not a structural fill by default and not a surface.

The third is what is not specified anywhere. There is no statewide codified definition of select fill in Texas, and no code-mandated depth for a select fill cap. It is engineer-specified per project. In Gulf Coast geotechnical practice the term typically refers to a low-plasticity sandy clay or an imported caliche blend placed in shallow lifts, but that is a description of practice rather than a code requirement. Any bid quoting you a standard cap depth is quoting a habit.

On roofing, the specification equivalents are substrate, fastener grade, attachment pattern, membrane thickness and edge detail. A bid that names the manufacturer but not those has named a brand rather than a system.

## Why do equipment choices show up in your schedule?

Because the machine determines how many times material gets handled, and handling is the cost.

On an earthwork site the expensive inefficiency is rarely the hourly rate of any single machine. It is material being moved twice because the sequence was wrong, or a site laid out so that haul routes cross working areas, or compaction equipment mismatched to lift thickness so that passes accomplish less than they should.

Access is the constraint people underestimate. What can physically reach the work decides the method, and on infill lots, occupied commercial sites and island properties the answer is often smaller equipment and more passes. That is a schedule consequence rather than a quality one, and it should be visible in the estimate rather than discovered.

Weather is the other scheduling input, and here it is specific. This region sees relatively few thunderstorm days in a year and severe conditions on a minority of those, with the annual peak in May and downbursts posing the primary wind threat. The practical effect is that risk arrives as a small number of sharp events rather than as constant drizzle. On earthwork that means moisture conditioning and timing around a wet clay that becomes unworkable quickly. On roofing it means open area is sized to what can be closed the same day.

## Which common claims about this work are simply wrong?

Several, and they persist because they sound plausible and nobody checks.

Ultraviolet exposure is not what limits TPO. Published membrane research found UV ageing not to be a meaningful issue for these membranes, and identified sustained heat as the governing factor instead, with a wide spread between grades and manufacturers. A specification naming the category without naming the product has not addressed the actual variable.

Galvalume is not the coastal metal. This one is inverted in a lot of local advice. The aluminium-zinc coated steel products are excellent inland and carry no substrate warranty in the near-shore band, while aluminium is the substrate that holds long-term coastal paint warranties. Aluminium brings its own trade-offs — roughly double the thermal movement of steel, easier denting, higher cost — and those are worth discussing honestly rather than hiding.

Ponding does not universally void a warranty. That claim is repeated as though it were a rule of the industry. In reality the manufacturer document governs, and at least one premium single-ply guarantee explicitly covers leaks in ponding areas, while on one metal substrate ponding is an absolute exclusion. Two systems, opposite answers, same weather.

Impact-rated shingles are not hail-proof. The test drops a two-inch steel ball from twenty feet twice onto the same spot and judges failure on the underside rather than on cosmetic surface damage, using new material. Manufacturers state directly that their impact-resistant products are not warranted against hail damage, and that insurance credits vary by state and company. A credit may well exist. Ask your insurer, not your roofer.

The windstorm repair threshold is measured in square feet, not roofing squares. Those two readings differ by a factor of one hundred, and the error always runs in the direction of skipping an inspection.

The WPI-3 has not been accepted since May 2020. It still appears on Texas roofing sites as current guidance.

And one storm record correction, because it circulates locally: Dolly's final report put it at Category 1 at mainland landfall. The Category 2 figure that gets repeated was the operational estimate, not the final analysis.

## How does a roof assembly manage moisture in this climate?

Differently from almost everywhere else in the country, and this is where imported detailing does real damage.

Cameron County sits in a hot-humid climate zone, and the defining consequence is that vapour drive runs inward. Outside air is warm and wet, the conditioned interior is cool and dry, and moisture pushes from outside toward inside for most of the year. Cold-climate detailing assumes the reverse, and a great deal of published roofing and attic advice was written for the reverse.

The code has caught up with this in a specific way that surprises people. Low-permeance vapour retarders — the class that includes polyethylene sheet — are prohibited on the interior side of air-permeable insulation in this zone and its neighbours. A detail that is mandatory in a northern state is not permitted here. Installing one traps moisture against the wrong surface, and the assembly cannot dry in the direction the climate is pushing.

Unvented attic assemblies have their own requirement set in these zones. A vapour diffusion port sized to at least one six-hundredth of the ceiling area, positioned within a foot of the ridge, built to a high permeance, on a roof of adequate slope. Where rigid board is used above the deck, a minimum thermal value is required specifically for condensation control rather than for energy performance. That distinction matters: the board is doing dew-point work, and reducing it to save money reintroduces the condensation the assembly was designed to avoid.

Prescriptive ceiling insulation in this zone runs to a substantially higher value than most existing Valley houses actually carry, which is worth knowing before a re-roof, because the roof being open is the cheapest moment in a building's life to address what sits below the deck.

The practical summary for an owner: on this coast, an assembly needs to be able to dry inward, and anything that prevents that is a defect regardless of how standard it looks in a manufacturer's generic detail sheet.

## What actually determines how long a metal roof lasts?

Movement, fasteners and washing — in roughly that order, and none of them is the paint.

Thermal movement is the structural reality behind panel design. Standing seam panels are attached with clips that allow the panel to slide as it expands and contracts, which is why the system tolerates daily thermal cycling over long runs. Exposed-fastener panels are pinned through the face, which restrains that movement, and the restrained load has to go somewhere. Over years it works fasteners loose. This is not a defect in exposed-fastener systems, it is a characteristic of them, and it converts into a maintenance schedule of roughly twice-yearly inspection.

Aluminium raises the stakes on movement specifically, because it expands about twice as much as steel for the same temperature change. On longer runs that pushes the design toward expansion clips as a requirement rather than an option.

Fasteners are the second determinant, and the rule is to match the fastener to the substrate rather than to a habit. The 300-series stainless grades are the coastal answer alongside aluminium panels. The martensitic grade commonly stocked is rated by manufacturers for urban, rural and industrial exposure and explicitly not for coastal service. Electroplated zinc is not an exterior fastener. Galvanized fasteners against aluminium cladding are discouraged. Where coated steel screws are used, the salt-spray benchmark is several hundred hours without red rust. And the useful counter-example: at least one manufacturer of steel roofing systems discourages stainless fasteners with its own product, which is why "always use stainless" is not a rule either.

Washing is the third and the least intuitive. Airborne chlorides are hygroscopic, meaning they attract and hold moisture, so a salt-contaminated surface stays electrolytically active long after the rain has stopped. Corrosion therefore tracks deposition and wash-off rather than distance alone. The consequence on a real building is that the rain-washed field of a roof often outlasts the sheltered undersides, eave returns and equipment shadows where salt accumulates and never rinses. Freshwater rinsing of those areas is legitimate maintenance.

As for service life, one field study of long-service low-slope unpainted aluminium-zinc standing seam roofs, some in place for decades, projected service life beyond sixty years, with the butyl seam sealant identified as the limiting component at around that mark. That is a useful anchor precisely because it names what fails first.

## How do material wind ratings relate to actual wind?

Loosely, and the standards themselves say so.

Two shingle standards are in common use. One is a fan-induced test with classes topping out at 110 miles per hour. The other measures uplift force against calculated resistance, with classes reaching up to 190. Both are legitimate, they measure different things, and the governing documents state that the results do not directly correlate to wind speeds experienced in service. A shingle rated to a high class is not a promise about a particular storm.

For panel systems the hierarchy is more informative. One widely cited test is pass/fail against a threshold and tells you only that a system cleared a bar. Another loads to failure but excludes the deck from the assembly, which is a meaningful omission on a re-roof where the deck is often the weak link. A third produces ratings that already incorporate a safety factor of two, so the headline number and the allowable load differ by half. The fourth tests the complete assembly to failure, including anchors and panel deflection, and is generally regarded as the most reliable basis for designing standing seam.

The reason to know the differences is that bids quote whichever number flatters the product. Asking which standard produced a figure, and whether the deck was included, moves a conversation from marketing to engineering in one question.

One local derivation is worth carrying because it applies without exception across Cameron County. Fastening requirements for strip shingles increase in high-wind zones, and since the lowest zone assigned anywhere in this county already sits at that threshold, the higher fastening pattern governs county-wide. There is nowhere in Cameron County where the lower pattern is the correct specification. Nail placement rules travel with it: penetration through both the overlay and underlay portions, minimum head size and penetration depth, and no fastener placed within two inches of a joint or cutout in the shingle beneath.

## How is anything chosen for this library?

By whether it changes a decision, and whether it can be sourced.

An entry earns a place if knowing it would change what an owner specifies, approves or asks about. Material that is merely interesting does not qualify. Neither does material that exists to rank for a phrase, which is most of what is published in this trade.

Sourcing is the second filter and the stricter one. Every claim traces to a primary document: a soil series description, a federal technical bulletin, a manufacturer's actual warranty text, a published research summary, a code section, a weather service report. Where sources conflict, the more authoritative one wins and the conflict gets noted. Where a figure could not be retrieved from a primary source, the entry says so and works around it.

That last rule costs us content. There are figures we would like to publish and cannot: certain county wind zone assignments, specific freeboard requirements, drainage district rates pending re-verification against an updated plan, regional soil property ranges. Each of those would be easy to assert and impossible to defend.
