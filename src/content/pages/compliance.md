Most of Texas builds under one set of rules. The coast builds under several at once, administered by authorities that do not talk to each other and do not run on the same calendar. A project can satisfy every requirement the city inspector has, pass final, and still be missing the document that decides whether the structure can be insured against wind.

That is not a technicality. It is the single most expensive misunderstanding available to a property owner on this coast, and it is almost always discovered after the work is closed in, when the cheapest remedies have already expired.

## Why does the coast run two compliance tracks instead of one?

Because two different bodies of law are doing two different jobs.

The building permit is a construction authority function. A city, or the county in unincorporated areas, adopts an edition of the International Residential Code and International Building Code, reviews your plans against it, inspects the work, and signs off. That process asks whether the structure is built correctly.

The windstorm certificate is an insurance function. It exists because the state operates an insurer of last resort for wind and hail on the coast, and that insurer needs evidence that a structure conforms to applicable building codes before it will write the risk. The Texas Department of Insurance administers it. Your city has no role in it.

These two tracks can sit on different code editions at the same moment, and right now they frequently do. Work begun on or after 1 April 2026 is certified to the 2024 IRC and 2024 IBC. Work begun between 1 September 2020 and 1 April 2026 was certified to the 2018 codes. A city's adopted edition is set by local ordinance and changes on its own schedule. Port Isabel, for instance, adopted the 2018 I-codes. So a Port Isabel project permitted today is reviewed by the city against one edition and certified by the state against a newer one.

The practical consequence is simple. Satisfying your inspector is not the same as satisfying the certificate, and nobody in the permit process is responsible for telling you that.

## Who is actually allowed to certify a roof for windstorm?

Three parties, and a contractor is not one of them.

A WPI-8 Certificate of Compliance is issued by the Texas Department of Insurance. The underlying inspection and certification come from a TDI inspector, a TDI appointed qualified inspector — usually shortened to AQI — or a Texas-licensed professional engineer. That is the complete list.

This matters because self-certification is a common and confident-sounding claim in this market. It is not a grey area or a matter of interpretation. No roofing company in Texas can issue its own windstorm certificate, and a company offering to is either misunderstanding the process or describing something that will not exist when you need it.

What a contractor can legitimately do is build to the certifiable specification, file the paperwork in the right order, and coordinate the inspection so it happens while inspection is still possible. That is the whole of the contractor's role, and it is enough, because the sequence is where most certificates are lost.

Worth knowing on cost: TDI inspectors charge no fee for the inspection. If you are quoted a state inspection fee, ask what it is actually for. And once TDI issues a certificate, it may not rescind it.

## What has to be filed before anyone touches the roof?

A WPI-1 application goes to TDI before work begins. Not during. Not at completion. Before.

This is statutory, under Texas Insurance Code §2210.2515, and it is the step that quietly decides the outcome of everything downstream. The application is what puts the project into the inspection system while the assembly is still open and observable.

The order is the entire point. Once a roof is closed in, an inspector cannot see the deck, the fasteners, the flashing laps or the attachment pattern. Those are precisely the things certification is about. A finished roof that looks immaculate from the driveway tells a windstorm inspector nothing about how it is attached.

So when a contractor asks to file before the tear-off starts, that is not administrative throat-clearing. It is the difference between a routine inspection and an expensive reconstruction of evidence.

## What happens if the work is already closed in?

Your options narrow to one, and it is the costly one.

Once work is closed in, TDI inspectors and appointed qualified inspectors can no longer inspect it. The only remaining path runs through a Texas-licensed professional engineer, via a WPI-2E application leading to a WPI-8-E certificate. The engineer has to establish, after the fact, what is underneath a finished surface.

That means opening work back up, or engineering documentation, or both. You pay for the investigation, the remediation of anything it finds, and the certificate. On a re-roof that was otherwise complete and correct, this is money spent purely on sequence.

This is the strongest argument in the entire build for calling somebody before the first shingle comes off rather than after the last one goes on. It is also the reason to be sceptical of a low bid that treats the paperwork as an afterthought. The paperwork is not adjacent to the work. It is part of it.

One more piece of housekeeping that still catches people out: the WPI-3 is dead. TWIA stopped accepting it on 31 May 2020. It continues to appear on Texas roofing websites as though it were live guidance, which tells you something useful about how recently those pages were reviewed.

## What does TWIA actually say happens without a certificate?

Less than the scare version, and still enough to take seriously.

TWIA's own language is that without a certificate it lacks evidence that the structure conforms to applicable building codes, and the structure may be considered uninsurable and ineligible for coverage with TWIA.

Read the hedge, because it is doing real work. The phrasing is "may be considered." It is not a statement that your existing policy will be cancelled the moment a certificate is missing, and anyone selling you a roof on that claim has upgraded the source to make the sale. What it does mean is that you are relying on an evidentiary gap staying unexamined, on a coast where wind claims are the reason the insurer exists.

The honest framing is about optionality. A certificate is cheap to obtain in the right sequence and expensive to reconstruct afterwards. It travels with the structure when you sell. Without it you are not necessarily uninsured today, but you have a documentation problem that surfaces at the worst possible moment, which is after a storm.

## Which counties are inside the catastrophe area, and where does the line fall?

The designated catastrophe area covers fourteen first-tier coastal counties. Cameron and Willacy are both inside it. Hidalgo County is not.

Hidalgo is second-tier, which is a different thing that sounds similar. The practical effect is a hard change in process at a line on a map. A roof in San Benito is in the program. A roof in Weslaco is not. Same crew, same materials, same week, different compliance obligations.

This is why it is worth being precise and never writing that "the Valley" is in the windstorm area. The Valley is not a regulatory unit. The counties are. If you are getting advice from a contractor who describes coverage by region rather than by county, they are working from a mental model that will eventually put a certificate on the wrong side of a line.

Inside Cameron County the department splits the map three ways, and the assignments are worth reading as a reference table rather than as prose:

- **Seaward** — South Padre Island, and nothing else.
- **Inland I** — Brownsville, Port Isabel, Los Fresnos, Laguna Vista, Laguna Heights, Rio Hondo, Bayview, Arroyo City, Boca Chica, Indian Lake, Lozano, Southmost, Port Brownsville.
- **Inland II** — Harlingen, San Benito, La Feria, Combes, Primera, Palm Valley, Rancho Viejo, Olmito, Santa Rosa, Los Indios, Santa Maria, Stuart Place.

Two assignments in that table routinely surprise people. Port Isabel sits in Inland I rather than Seaward, because the boundary follows U.S. 77 instead of distance from open water. Brownsville is placed in Inland I by special rule, whatever the line does nearby. Nearness to the coast predicts the zone poorly, and the map settles it.

For Willacy County, we do not publish a zone. The county is confirmed inside the catastrophe area, but the specific zone assignments are not something we have verified from the primary map, and a wrong zone is worse than an acknowledged gap. Ask the department or your engineer.

## What do the wind zone numbers mean, and why are there two sets?

Because the same buildings have been described in two different measurement systems, and the numbers were never converted for the public.

Under the legacy 2006 code, using ASCE 7-05 nominal speeds, the zones were 130 mph Seaward, 120 mph Inland I and 110 mph Inland II. The ASCE 7-10 ultimate equivalents for those same buildings are 168, 155 and 143 mph.

Nothing about the buildings changed. The unit changed. A homeowner who hears "155" from one source and "120" from another is not hearing a disagreement, and a contractor who cannot explain which convention a number belongs to should not be quoting numbers at all.

For a project being permitted today, we do not publish a design wind speed, because there is no longer a single per-zone figure to publish. Design wind speed is determined per structure under ASCE 7, based on the building's characteristics and site. That determination belongs to an engineer looking at your specific project. Any site handing you a single current mph figure for your town is giving you a number it cannot support.

One derived requirement is worth stating plainly, because it applies everywhere in the county. Code requires at least four fasteners per strip shingle, and six in high-wind zones of 110 mph and above. Since the lowest zone anywhere in Cameron County is 110, the six-nail pattern applies across the entire county. There is no part of Cameron County where a four-nail shingle roof is the correct specification.

## How high does a pad have to sit, and who decides?

Flood zone first, local ordinance second, and the two answer different questions.

Under federal floodplain management criteria, A and AE zones require the lowest floor to be at or above base flood elevation. V and VE zones — the coastal high-hazard areas exposed to wave action — are considerably stricter. There, the lowest horizontal structural member must be elevated to or above BFE, the structure sits on piles or columns, and anything enclosed below has to be breakaway construction.

The V and VE rule that governs dirt work is the one most people have never heard: participating communities must prohibit the use of fill for structural support of buildings. On the Gulf side, you cannot solve elevation by building up a pad. The building goes up on piles and the fill is not permitted to be what holds it there. That single sentence changes the entire scope of a site preparation job, and it is the reason a bid that assumes a fill pad can be wrong before anyone arrives.

Above the federal minimum, communities add freeboard, which FEMA defines as a factor of safety usually expressed in feet above a flood level. Most communities choose one to three feet. The specific figure is local, and we do not publish freeboard numbers for South Padre Island or for Cameron County because we have not verified them from a current primary source.

Harlingen, by contrast, publishes a standard we can cite. Under its subdivision ordinance, foundations in Zone X sit no less than 18 inches above top of curb; inside a Special Flood Hazard Area the minimum is 24 inches above top of curb; and a lot within 100 feet of a drainage inlet has its finished floor set six inches higher than neighbouring lots. That is the level of specificity worth asking your own jurisdiction for, because somebody there has an answer even when it is not on a website.

## Who else has to approve water leaving your site?

Frequently a drainage district, and this is the approval most often discovered late.

Cameron County contains drainage districts numbered 1, 3, 4, 5 and 6, alongside irrigation districts including Bayview, Brownsville, Cameron County No. 2, No. 6 and No. 16, Harlingen, Hidalgo and Cameron Counties No. 9, and La Feria. Under Texas Water Code Chapter 58, districts hold rulemaking authority over their easements. A district can tell you what you may build near its channel, how you may cross it, and what you may discharge into it.

The requirement categories are consistent even where the numbers are not. Districts set an allowable run-out rate and a permitted net increase in discharge, which together decide whether your site needs detention. They specify how a utility line may cross an easement, including crossing angle, depth below the existing flowline and protection of the pipe. They charge application and plat review fees, require a minimum right-of-way width, and require submission a set number of days before a regular meeting. Approvals commonly expire if the project is not developed within a defined window. And open ditch connections to existing channels are prohibited outright.

We are deliberately not publishing the specific figures here. Cameron County Drainage District No. 1's Master Drainage Plan carries them, and an updated plan has been issued since the version those numbers came from. Quoting a superseded rate on a page that carries a review date would be exactly the kind of confident error these pages exist to avoid. Get the current figures from the district that governs your tract, and get them before the site plan is finished rather than after.

Separately, larger sites trigger construction stormwater authorization through the TCEQ, with erosion and sediment controls that have to be in place and maintained. Receiving waters here are not neutral about it: the tidal segment of the Arroyo Colorado carries a dissolved oxygen impairment, and the segment above it carries a bacteria impairment.

## What does Texas actually require of a roofing contractor?

Less than most owners assume, which is why the question matters.

Texas does not license roofing contractors. The Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation administers dozens of regulated programs, and roofing is not among them. There is no state roofing licence to hold, to check, or to lose.

What does exist is a voluntary industry credential. The Roofing Contractors Association of Texas runs a Licensed Roofing Contractor program with two trade categories, residential and commercial, and two tiers, Principal and Manager. It carries insurance minimums of $300,000 residential and $500,000 commercial, a 70 percent exam pass mark, and eight continuing education units annually. It is a real credential with real requirements. It is not a state licence, and the word "licensed" inside its name does a lot of work in advertising.

Cities may separately require local contractor registration, which is a different obligation again and genuinely varies by jurisdiction.

The reason to spell this out on our own site is that the ambiguity is usually exploited rather than explained. If you are comparing bids, the useful questions are who carries what insurance, who is accountable if the certificate is not obtained, and who files the WPI-1 and when. Those have checkable answers. "Licensed" on its own does not.

## What order does a compliant coastal project actually run in?

Sequence is the whole discipline here, so it is worth setting out end to end.

Scope gets established first, because scope decides which tracks apply at all. A repair under 100 square feet that does not involve decking or framing members does not require inspection. A full replacement does. That threshold is measured in square feet, not in roofing squares, and the difference between the two readings is a factor of one hundred. It is one of the most commonly mangled figures in Texas roofing, usually in the direction that lets someone skip a step.

Jurisdiction comes next: which city or county reviews the permit, which zone the property sits in, whether a flood zone or a drainage district easement is in play. Those answers change the specification, not just the paperwork.

Then the filings go in, before demolition. The WPI-1 reaches TDI before work begins. Any floodplain or drainage approval is secured before the site plan is final rather than after.

Only then does work start, built to the specification that can be certified, with the inspection scheduled while the assembly is still open.

Close-out is last, and it is where the documents get collected: the certificate, the permit records, the manufacturer paperwork for the system actually installed. Those travel with the property when you sell it, and reconstructing them years later is nobody's good afternoon.

## Where does our authority stop?

At the point where a determination is somebody else's to make, and we would rather draw that line ourselves than have you discover it.

We do not issue windstorm certificates. We do not perform windstorm inspections. We do not make floodplain determinations, set base flood elevations, approve drainage plans, or decide which code edition your jurisdiction has adopted. We are not engineers, and where a project needs a professional engineer's determination — a post-construction certification, a design wind speed for a specific structure, a fill or foundation recommendation on difficult ground — that work belongs to a licensed engineer.

What we do is build to the specification that can be certified, sequence the filings so the inspection happens while it still can, and tell you plainly when a question belongs to someone else.
