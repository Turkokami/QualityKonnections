Two divisions, one company, and a deliberate reason for the pairing: on this coast the ground and the roof are the two places a building is most likely to fail, and they fail for related reasons. Water that does not leave a site properly is the same problem as water that does not leave a roof properly. Both are solved by getting slope, drainage and materials right before anything is closed up.

The rest of this page is about how the work actually runs and what decides which service you need. Pricing here is deliberately about drivers rather than numbers, because a number quoted without seeing your ground or your roof is a guess wearing a suit.

## Why does one contractor do both the ground and the roof?

Because on a new build they are the same schedule, and the handoff between them is where projects lose weeks.

From the ground underneath your building to the roof protecting everything inside it is the span, and the practical benefit is not philosophical. When the pad and the roof are two contracts, the pad contractor leaves, the builder frames, and the roofer arrives to find a site that was graded for someone else's convenience. Access, laydown, drainage during construction and the finished elevation all get decided by whoever moved dirt first, and the roofer inherits those decisions without having been asked.

Running both means one schedule and one point of accountability. It also means the site work is done by people who know what the roof drainage is going to do to the ground next to the building, which is not a detail most pad crews are asked to think about.

The honest limit: this is a benefit on new construction and on projects where both scopes genuinely exist. If you need a re-roof on an existing house, you need a roofer, and the dirt work division is irrelevant to you. We would rather say that than pretend every job needs both.

## What does site preparation actually cover?

Everything between raw ground and a surface something can be built on.

Clearing removes vegetation, stumps and debris. Excavation cuts material out. Cut and fill moves it from where it is to where it is needed, which is usually cheaper than importing, when the on-site material is suitable. Grading and levelling establish the surface. Building pads are the engineered platform a structure sits on. Trenching opens the runs for utilities, and backfilling closes them properly rather than just filling the hole. Compaction is what makes any of it hold. Drainage and site shaping decide where water goes for the life of the property. Road and driveway preparation builds the base course that keeps a surface from rutting.

At larger scale the same operations run as commercial site development and large-scale earthmoving, where thousands of yards move across a site and the sequencing matters as much as the equipment.

The order is not arbitrary and the compaction step is not optional. A pad that was placed correctly but compacted casually will look identical on the day it is finished and behave very differently in three years.

## How do you know whether you need a pad, a grade, or full earthwork?

Start with what is failing or what is being built, not with the equipment.

If you are putting up a new structure, you need a pad, and the pad needs an engineer's specification if the ground is difficult — which, across most of this region, it is. If water stands where it should not, or runs toward a building instead of away, that is a grading and drainage question and it may not require a pad at all. If you are opening a new site, subdividing, or building anything commercial, you are in full earthwork with permitting and drainage-district involvement attached.

A useful diagnostic: walk the property after a real rain rather than on a dry day. Where water sits for a day or more, where it cuts a channel, where it arrives from a neighbouring lot. That tells you more about what the site needs than any amount of dry-ground inspection, and it is free.

The counter-case worth stating: not every drainage complaint is a dirt-work job. Some are a gutter and downspout problem, some are a neighbouring property's discharge, and some belong to a drainage district whose easement runs across the back of the lot. Moving dirt to solve a problem that originates somewhere else is an expensive way to relocate it.

## What does the ground here actually do to a building pad?

It moves, and the amount it moves depends on which soil series you are on.

Much of this region sits on Vertisols — clays that shrink and swell with moisture. Harlingen clay is a very-fine smectitic Sodic Haplusterts, moderately well drained, with very slow permeability and cracks that stay open 150 to 210 cumulative days in most years. Benito clay is the same great group but poorly drained, with runoff ranging from very slow to ponded, slickensides between 8 and 63 inches, cracks up to three inches wide reaching 20 inches deep, and a seasonal high water table 4 to 6 feet down from September through May.

Nearer the bay the chemistry gets aggressive. Lomalta clay is a Typic Natraquerts, poorly drained, semi-marshy, sitting only slightly above sea level, with electrical conductivity from 12 to 50 dS/m and exchangeable sodium percentage above 15 in every horizon.

Shrink-swell potential shows up in the COLE values: Chargo silty clay runs 0.09 to 0.14, Olmito silty clay 0.07 to 0.15, Raymondville clay loam 0.07 to 0.17. All of those are very high shrink-swell soils.

Not everything here is difficult. Laredo silty clay loam, Willacy fine sandy loam and Hidalgo sandy clay loam are well drained, moderately permeable, non-vertic and low shrink-swell. Those are the straightforward sites, and it is worth knowing whether you are on one before you budget for the hard version.

Two corrections worth carrying, because both appear in local bids. South Padre Island is Mustang fine sand, a Typic Psammaquents with a seasonal water table 11 to 30 inches down. But Point Isabel is a clay, not a sand, despite the name — fine, mixed, active, hyperthermic Typic Calciustepts on vegetated dunes, with slow permeability and a wide salinity range. And the Rio Grande soil series is coarse-silty, so its phases are silt loam and very fine sandy loam. "Rio Grande silty clay loam" is not a real phase, and seeing it on a report is a reason to ask who wrote the report.

## Why is compaction specified as a percentage, and what does the number mean?

Because density is the only property of placed fill you can actually verify in the field.

Ninety-five percent standard Proctor means 95 percent of the maximum dry density established by ASTM D698 for that material. It is not an abstract quality rating. It is a measurement against a laboratory maximum for the specific soil being placed, which is why the same percentage on two different materials describes two different results.

For fill supporting buildings, FEMA Technical Bulletin 10 treats 95 percent standard as roughly equivalent to 90 percent modified under ASTM D1557, and caps lifts at 12 inches. Lift thickness matters as much as the percentage: material placed too thick does not densify through its full depth no matter what the roller does on the surface.

The governing principle from Texas engineering practice is blunt, and worth quoting because it settles most arguments on site: foundations shall not be supported by non-engineered fill. Texas Section ASCE defines expansive clay as a weighted plasticity index above 15 with potential volume change over one percent, and potential vertical rise is calculated per the TxDOT method over a 15-foot column.

What we will not tell you is how deep your select fill cap needs to be, because there is no code-mandated select fill depth in Texas. It is engineer-specified per project, and any contractor quoting you a standard depth without an engineer's number is quoting a habit rather than a specification. "Select fill" itself has no statewide codified definition; in Gulf Coast geotechnical practice it typically means a low-plasticity sandy clay or imported caliche blend, placed in lifts of eight inches or less.

## What is caliche, and why is it under most driveways here?

Caliche is secondary calcium carbonate — carbonate leached from upper soil and reprecipitated lower down, developing through six recognised morphologic stages and mapped in this area as a distinct geologic unit.

In the Valley it is commercially quarried and crushed as a base material to improve drainage and traction on unpaved roads. That is its job: it is a base course, not a surface and not a structural fill by default.

Base material is judged on plasticity and durability rather than on what it is called. The TxDOT flexible base specification sets limits on liquid limit, plasticity index and wet ball mill loss by grade, with the higher grade held to the tighter plasticity limit, and requires compaction to full specified density. Caliche is normally supplied against the lower of those grades.

The practical point for an owner: base course quality is where a driveway or a laydown yard is won or lost, and it is invisible once the surface goes on. A thin or high-plasticity base under a good surface fails early and looks like a surface problem. Ask what grade of base is being placed and how thick, and ask before the surface covers the evidence.

We do not publish which pits are active or what any specific pit's material tests at. Those change, and a stale answer is worse than no answer.

## Which roofing systems make sense on which buildings?

Slope decides more than preference does, and the code minimums are the starting filter.

Asphalt shingles require at least 2:12, and between 2:12 and 4:12 they require double underlayment. Metal panels with lapped, non-soldered seams and no sealant need 3:12; with sealant they can run to 1/2:12. Standing seam goes to 1/4:12. Built-up roofing, modified bitumen, and thermoset and thermoplastic single-ply systems all run to 1/4:12, which is 2 percent.

Below those minimums a system is not merely a worse choice, it is outside its own installation instructions, which is where warranty conversations end badly.

Above the slope filter, the building type does the rest. R-panel and exposed-fastener metal is the right answer at 3:12 and above on agricultural buildings, structural applications and simpler residential roofs, and pretending otherwise to upsell standing seam is not a service. Standing seam floats on slider and expansion clips, which is why it tolerates thermal movement that pinned systems do not. Exposed-fastener panels are pinned, and that limits how much the roof can expand and contract, which over time can back the fasteners out. Those roofs need inspection on the order of twice a year, and an owner who will not do that should not buy one.

For low-slope commercial, TPO and modified bitumen dominate for different reasons, covered below.

## Why is aluminium the coastal metal rather than Galvalume?

Because the substrate warranties say so, and the common advice has this exactly backwards.

Galvalume is an aluminium-zinc alloy coated steel, and it is an excellent product away from saltwater. Within 1,500 feet of a salt water environment it carries no substrate warranty, and manufacturer guidance is direct that neither Galvalume nor galvanized will stand up to coastal conditions. Aluminium is the oceanfront and bayfront substrate. It holds 25-year coastal PVDF paint warranties where the steel products hold none.

Note the distance, because this is where most published advice drifts. The exclusion is 1,500 feet, not a number of miles. McElroy's Kynar 500 warranty is void for material installed on property 1,500 feet or fewer from a salt water environment, and Galvalume substrate warranties exclude goods installed within 1,500 feet of a salt-water or other marine environment. Every "within five miles" claim online appears to be drift from that figure.

Aluminium is not free of trade-offs and we will not pretend it is. It expands roughly twice as much as steel, so runs beyond about 15 to 20 feet need expansion clips. It dents more easily. It costs somewhere around 15 to 25 percent more.

Fasteners have to match the substrate. For aluminium panels on the coast the answer is 300-series stainless, typically 304. The 410 stainless commonly stocked is explicitly not coastal-rated — manufacturers list it for urban, rural and industrial exposure only. Electroplated zinc screws are not exterior fasteners at all, and galvanized fasteners against aluminium cladding are not recommended. Where organic-coated steel screws are used, the benchmark is no red rust after 500 hours of salt spray testing.

The counter-example is worth knowing, because "always stainless" is not universally right either: DECRA discourages stainless fasteners with its own steel systems. Match the fastener to the system in front of you, not to a rule of thumb.

One mechanism explains a lot of premature coastal failure: airborne chlorides are hygroscopic. They hold a film of electrolyte on the metal long after the rain stops, and that breaks down the passive oxide layers that protect zinc and aluminium. So exposure is really about deposition and wash-off rather than distance alone. Sheltered, unwashed undersides and eaves corrode faster than rain-washed field areas, and fresh-water rinsing is a genuine maintenance item rather than a sales line.

## What actually limits a low-slope membrane's life here?

For TPO it is heat, not ultraviolet, and this is one of the most widely repeated errors in commercial roofing.

Research published through NRCA puts utility-grade TPO at roughly 15 years at 160°F sustained, standard grades near 25 years at 180°F, and the most heat-stable formulations beyond 35 years at around 200°F. The same work found that UV ageing does not appear to be an issue for TPO membranes. TPO is also a varied product: heat-ageing performance differs materially between manufacturers, so the category name on a bid tells you much less than the specific membrane does.

For modified bitumen we do not publish a service life figure, because no association-published number exists. The ranges that circulate online trace back to contractor marketing rather than to research. Modified bitumen earns its place on redundancy, puncture resistance, tolerance of foot traffic and ease of repair, and those are the grounds to argue it on.

Ponding deserves precision too. Manufacturer warranty language defines ponding as water that does not drain or dissipate from the roof surface within 48 hours after precipitation ends. But "ponding voids your warranty" is not universally true: at least one major manufacturer's premium NDL commercial guarantee does cover leaks in ponding areas on TPO and PVC. On Galvalume metal, by contrast, ponding is an absolute exclusion. The rule depends on the system and the document, which is why the document matters.

Climate drives the rest. This is Climate Zone 2A, hot and humid, with a prescriptive ceiling insulation value of R-38, and low-slope roofs over cooled conditioned space subject to aged solar reflectance and thermal emittance minimums. Vapour drive here is inward, not outward, which flips assumptions imported from colder markets: Class I vapour retarders are prohibited on the interior side of air-permeable insulation in this zone. Unvented attic assemblies need a vapour diffusion port sized to at least 1:600 of ceiling area, placed within 12 inches of the ridge, and rigid board above the deck needs a minimum R-5 for condensation control.

## What do the storms here actually break?

Roof coverings on lower-quality construction. Consistently, across every major event on record.

Beulah came ashore in 1967 as a Category 3 between the mouth of the Rio Grande and Brownsville, cutting 31 new channels across South Padre Island and spawning roughly 115 tornadoes. Allen in 1980 made a Category 3 landfall near Port Mansfield with a 12-foot surge, 68 new cuts on Padre Island and four feet of water in Brownsville buildings. Dolly in 2008 was a Category 1 at mainland landfall with about $1.05 billion in damage, described as mainly moderate structural damage, primarily to roofs, on South Padre Island. Hanna in 2020 was a Category 1 with roughly $1.2 billion in damage, a peak gust of 103 mph at Rincon del San Jose, more than 250,000 customers without power, and hundreds of poorly built structures suffering roof damage.

The pattern across all four is the same, and it is the honest argument for spending money on the assembly rather than the appearance: coverings, cladding and light-frame roofs failed. Engineered buildings did not collapse.

Day to day the threat is not hurricanes. The Valley averages fewer than 30 thunderstorm days a year and severe storms on under a third of those, with 540 hail and wind events recorded between 1996 and 2021, about 21 a year, peaking in May. Downbursts are the primary wind threat here and occur far more often than tornado damage.

Hail is real but concentrated differently. The 2012 mid-Valley storm hit McAllen, Edinburg, Mission and Pharr with baseball-sized hail, damaged more than a thousand structures and ran to roughly $600 million insured. Regional wind and hail losses since then exceed $750 million. On the coast, though, wind is the everyday driver.

Which brings up the impact-resistance question. UL 2218 Class 4 means a two-inch steel ball dropped from 20 feet, twice on the same spot, with failure judged on the underside of the shingle rather than on cosmetic damage on top. It is a test of new material. Manufacturers state plainly that impact-resistant shingles are not warranted to withstand hail damage, and that insurance credits vary by state and by company. Many insurers do offer a credit. Ask yours rather than taking a contractor's word for the discount.

Wind ratings on shingles deserve the same scepticism. ASTM D3161 runs to Class F at 110 mph; ASTM D7158 runs to Class H at up to 190 mph. The standards themselves state that the results do not directly correlate to wind speeds experienced in service. For metal panel uplift, the useful hierarchy is that UL 580 is pass/fail, UL 1897 tests to failure but ignores the deck, FM 4471 ratings carry a safety factor of 2, and ASTM E1592 tests to failure including anchors and panel deflection, which is why it is considered the most reliable basis for designing standing seam panels.

## What drives the price on either side of the business?

On dirt work: how much material moves, how far it travels, what has to be imported versus reused, how difficult access is, what the compaction specification requires, and whether an engineer's design governs the fill. Haul distance is usually the driver people underestimate.

On roofing: the size and complexity of the roof, the number of penetrations and transitions, the condition of the decking underneath, the system specified, the fastener and substrate requirements the site's exposure imposes, and the certification track the work sits in.
