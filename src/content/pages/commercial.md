A commercial project is not a large residential one. The difference is not square footage, it is that somebody has to answer for the building years after the crew leaves — to an insurer, a lender, a corporate parent, an auditor, or a buyer's inspector. That changes what a contractor has to produce alongside the work.

On this coast it also means a set of obligations that simply do not attach to a house: windstorm certification on an accelerated commercial schedule, drainage district approval before a site plan is final, stormwater authorisation on anything of size, and materials decisions that a salt atmosphere makes non-negotiable.

## What does a commercial project ask that a house does not?

Documentation, phasing, and somebody who will still be reachable.

On a residential re-roof the deliverable is essentially the roof. On a commercial project the deliverable is the roof plus a record of what was installed, to what specification, inspected by whom, and warranted under what terms. That record is what gets requested during a refinance, an insurance review, a sale or a claim, and reconstructing it after the fact ranges from expensive to impossible.

Phasing is the second difference. Most commercial buildings cannot stop operating for the convenience of a roof. Work gets sequenced around shifts, deliveries, tenants, patients or production, which means the schedule is a design problem rather than a calendar entry. A bid that does not mention phasing on an occupied building has not thought about the building.

The third is accountability over time. Roof systems here are specified in decades. The relevant question is not only who installs it but who is answerable when a detail fails in year seven, and whether the paperwork will support the conversation.

## Who actually carries the risk on a commercial roof?

The owner does, and the contract decides how much of it gets transferred.

That sounds obvious and it is routinely mishandled. Risk on a commercial roof sits in four places: the assembly's performance under wind, its performance under water, the certification status of the work, and the warranty documents that govern what happens when something fails.

Wind is the one this coast prices most heavily, and the testing hierarchy is worth understanding because bids quote it selectively. UL 580 is pass/fail and tells you a system met a threshold. UL 1897 tests to failure but ignores the deck, which is a significant omission on an existing building. FM 4471 ratings incorporate a safety factor of two, so a 1-90 rating corresponds to 45 pounds per square foot allowable. ASTM E1592 tests to failure including anchors and panel deflection, and is considered the most reliable basis for designing standing seam panels.

A bid citing a rating without saying which standard produced it is not telling you very much. Ask which standard, and ask what the deck is, because on a re-roof the deck is often the limiting component and nobody wants to be the one to raise it.

Certification risk is the quiet one. A commercial building without windstorm certification in the catastrophe area has a documentation gap that surfaces during a transaction or a claim rather than during construction. The [compliance](/compliance/) pages cover the mechanics; commercially, the point is that the gap is an asset-level problem, not a maintenance item.

## What do an owner and an auditor actually look for?

Different things, and a good project produces both without being asked twice.

An owner looks for the roof to stop leaking, the site to drain, the schedule to hold, and the number at the end to be the number at the beginning. Mid-project price revision is the complaint that damages contractors most in this market, and the defence against it is scope specificity rather than assurances.

An auditor or an insurer's reviewer looks for evidence. What system was installed, by which manufacturer, to which specification. Whether the fasteners match the substrate and the exposure. Whether inspection happened at the right stage and who performed it. Whether the warranty is registered and what it excludes. Whether the certification track was followed in the correct order.

The documents that matter most are the boring ones: the submittal for the system actually installed rather than the one originally quoted, the manufacturer's paperwork, the inspection records, and a clear statement of what is and is not covered.

One caution on credentials, because commercial bid packages often ask for a licence that does not exist. Texas does not license roofing contractors at state level. What exists is a voluntary industry credential from the state roofing association, with insurance minimums of $300,000 residential and $500,000 commercial, exam and continuing education requirements. It is meaningful and it is not a state licence. When you are evaluating bidders, ask for insurance certificates and for the name of whoever is accountable for the certification filing. Those are checkable in a way that adjectives are not.

## How does industrial earthwork differ from a residential pad?

In scale, in tolerance, and in who has to approve it before it starts.

A residential pad is one platform. An industrial site is a system: building pads, laydown yards, truck routes, drainage that has to work under load, and utility corridors that cross other people's easements. Large-scale earthmoving moves thousands of yards, and the sequencing determines whether material gets handled once or three times.

Tolerance tightens considerably. Compaction on a laydown yard carrying loaded trailers is not the same specification as compaction under a slab, and both are measured against a laboratory maximum for the specific material rather than against a general standard. The governing principle in Texas practice is that foundations shall not be supported by non-engineered fill, and on a commercial site that principle is enforced by an engineer rather than assumed.

The base course carries more weight commercially, in both senses. A yard that ruts under traffic is a base failure showing up as a surface complaint, and on an operating site it is also a safety and downtime issue. Flexible base is specified by plasticity and durability limits rather than by material name, and asking what grade is being placed and to what thickness is the single most useful question an owner can ask about a yard.

Approvals are the other difference. A commercial site of any size will involve a drainage district's allowable discharge rate, a stormwater authorisation with erosion and sediment controls that have to be installed and maintained, and often a floodplain question. Those get resolved before the site plan is final. Discovering a district's discharge limit after grading is designed is one of the more expensive sequences available.

## What drives a low-slope roofing decision beyond price?

Heat, drainage, traffic and what happens at the details.

Start with slope, because it filters the options before preference does. Built-up roofing, modified bitumen, and thermoset and thermoplastic single-ply systems all run down to a quarter-inch per foot, which is two percent. Below a system's minimum, it is outside its own instructions, and no warranty conversation survives that.

Then heat. The most persistent error in commercial roofing is that ultraviolet exposure limits TPO. It does not. Research published through the roofing association found that UV ageing does not appear to be an issue for TPO membranes, and that sustained heat is the governing factor: utility-grade material around 15 years at 160°F, standard grades near 25 years at 180°F, and the most heat-stable formulations beyond 35 years at around 200°F. TPO is a varied product, and heat-ageing performance differs materially between manufacturers. Specifying "TPO" without specifying whose is not a specification.

Modified bitumen competes on different ground. We do not publish a service life figure for it, because no association-published number exists and the ranges circulating online trace back to marketing. Its real arguments are redundancy from multiple plies, puncture resistance, tolerance of foot traffic, and ease of repair — which matter enormously on a roof that carries mechanical equipment and the people who service it.

Ponding needs care in a commercial contract. The manufacturer definition is water that has not drained or dissipated within 48 hours after precipitation ends. The blanket claim that ponding voids a warranty is not universally true: at least one major manufacturer's premium no-dollar-limit guarantee does cover leaks in ponding areas on TPO and PVC. On Galvalume metal, ponding is an absolute exclusion. Read the actual document rather than the general rule.

Energy code applies here too. This is Climate Zone 2A, hot and humid, and low-slope roofs over cooled conditioned space are subject to reflectance and emittance minimums — three-year-aged solar reflectance of at least 0.55 with thermal emittance of at least 0.75, or an aged solar reflectance index of at least 64. Exceptions exist, and the applicable code edition is a local question.

## What does deferred maintenance actually cost on a low-slope roof?

More than the repair, because the failure migrates downward.

A membrane leak rarely stays a membrane problem. Water reaches the insulation, the insulation loses thermal value and stops drying, the deck begins to corrode or rot, and the fastener pull-out values the wind rating depends on quietly degrade. At that point the roof has become a deck project, and the deck is the expensive part.

The regional climate makes this faster than owners expect. Vapour drive here is inward rather than outward, which reverses assumptions imported from colder markets and means a wet assembly does not dry the way northern intuition suggests. Class I vapour retarders are prohibited on the interior side of air-permeable insulation in this zone for exactly that reason.

The other cost is optional and self-inflicted: exposed-fastener metal roofs are pinned rather than floating, which limits expansion and contraction and over time can work the fasteners loose. Those roofs want inspection on the order of twice a year. An owner who buys one and inspects it never has bought a maintenance obligation and declined to perform it.

Coastal exposure adds one more line. Airborne chlorides are hygroscopic, holding an electrolyte film on metal long after rain stops and attacking the passive layers on zinc and aluminium. Because it is about deposition and wash-off rather than distance alone, the sheltered and unwashed parts of a building — undersides, eaves, anywhere rain does not reach — corrode faster than the open field of the roof. Fresh-water rinsing is a genuine maintenance item on coastal metal, not an upsell.

Warehouse and distribution, industrial and port facilities, retail and restaurant, multifamily and apartments, agricultural and ranch buildings, hospitality and RV resorts, medical and institutional properties, and work as a subcontractor to builders and general contractors.

That list reflects what this region's economy is actually producing. The industrial expansion around it — LNG construction, launch operations at Boca Chica, the logistics that follow a deeper channel — generates warehouse, laydown, industrial pad and large low-slope roof work.

Agricultural and ranch buildings belong on the list for a different reason: R-panel and exposed-fastener metal at 3:12 and above is genuinely the right specification for a lot of those structures, and pretending otherwise to sell standing seam is not a service to an owner who needs a barn.

What we do not do is worth stating with equal clarity. The service list is closed and traces to work the company performs. There is no concrete division, no framing, no plumbing, no solar, no inspection service and no in-house engineering. Where a commercial project needs a geotechnical recommendation, a foundation design or a professional engineer's certification, that is engineering work and it belongs to a licensed engineer.

## What should a commercial bid actually contain?

Enough specificity that two bids can be compared without a translator.

The system, named by manufacturer and product rather than by category. "TPO" is a category. The membrane thickness, the insulation type and thickness, the attachment method and the cover board are the specification, and they are where the price differences actually live.

The attachment detail, stated. Fastener type, spacing pattern and — on the coast — the grade of stainless or coated steel being used, with the substrate it is being paired with. A bid that omits fastener grade near saltwater has either priced the right ones or the wrong ones, and you cannot tell which.

The deck. What is assumed about its condition, what happens if it turns out to be worse, and at what rate. Deck surprises are the most common source of change orders on a commercial re-roof, and the honest way to handle them is a stated unit rate agreed in advance rather than a conversation held once the roof is open.

The edge and the penetrations. Perimeter and corner zones carry higher uplift loads than the field, and edge metal is a frequent failure origin. Curbs, drains, vents and equipment supports are where leaks begin. A bid that prices a field area and hand-waves the details is pricing the easy part.

The warranty, by name and term, with its exclusions attached. Manufacturer warranty and workmanship warranty are two different documents with two different obligors.

The certification path, with a named party responsible for the filing and a stated point in the schedule when it happens.

Anything a bid leaves out is not free. It is simply unpriced, and it becomes a conversation later under worse conditions.

## How do you assess a roof that is already failing?

By finding out where the water actually is, rather than where the stain is.

Interior staining is a poor locator. Water travels along deck flutes, purlins and insulation joints before it appears, and the visible mark can sit a considerable distance from the breach. Chasing stains is how owners end up paying for three repairs that each fixed a real leak and none of which was the one causing the damage.

The assessment worth paying for establishes a few things. Whether the membrane has failed generally or locally. Whether the insulation below is wet, and over how much area. Whether the deck has been compromised. Whether the details — edges, curbs, drains, penetrations — are the origin, which they usually are. And whether drainage is functioning, because a roof that no longer drains within the manufacturer's 48-hour window has a different problem from one with a puncture.

That evidence decides the honest recommendation, and sometimes the honest recommendation is a repair. A localised failure on a sound assembly with dry insulation is a repair, and selling a replacement into that situation is the behaviour this market already has too much of. Conversely, widespread wet insulation is not a repair no matter how small the visible damage is, because the thermal value is gone and the fastener holding capacity is going.

The other reason to document condition properly is timing. A roof assessed before a storm gives you a baseline. A roof assessed only after one leaves you arguing about what was pre-existing, with an adjuster who has no reason to take your word for it.

## What should an owner do first after storm damage?

Protect, document, then get an opinion that is not attached to the repair.

Temporary protection comes first, and it is genuinely urgent on a low-slope roof, because standing water finds an open seam quickly and interior damage compounds faster than roof damage. Dry-in the opening, get water out of the building, and photograph everything before it is disturbed.

Documentation is the step owners under-invest in and later need most. Date-stamped photographs of the exterior, the roof surface if it can be reached safely, the interior damage and any equipment affected. Keep the record of what temporary work was done and when. If you already hold roof documentation from the original installation, that becomes far more valuable at this moment than it seemed when it was filed.

Then get the roof looked at by someone who will tell you if it does not need replacing. Storm events attract volume operators, and the documented regional failure mode is roof-covering loss on lower-quality construction rather than catastrophic structural failure. That means a lot of storm-damaged roofs genuinely need work, and a lot of others need a repair being sold as a replacement.

One sequencing point specific to this coast, and it is the expensive one to get wrong. If the damage is severe enough to become a replacement, the windstorm filing has to be in before the tear-off starts. Storm urgency is exactly the condition under which that step gets skipped, and skipping it converts a routine inspection into an engineer's after-the-fact certification.

## How does a project get scheduled around a working business?

By deciding the awkward parts before mobilisation rather than during it.

The questions that need answers up front: which areas can be out of service and when, where the crew stages material, how deliveries reach the site without crossing operations, what happens to the interior if weather arrives mid-tear-off, and who on your side can make a decision at eight in the morning without a meeting.

Weather planning here is specific rather than general. This region averages fewer than 30 thunderstorm days a year, with severe conditions on under a third of those, and roughly 21 hail and wind events a year recorded across a 25-year window, peaking in May. Downbursts are the primary wind threat and occur far more often than tornado damage. That pattern means the risk is not constant background rain; it is a small number of sharp events. Open roof area at the wrong moment is the exposure, so tear-off is sized to what can be closed in the same day.

On price stability, the commitment is straightforward and it is the one this market most needs. We are not trying to win projects by being the lowest bidder; we are trying to be the best value. The practical form that takes is scope written specifically enough that there is nothing to revise mid-job.
