Two things decide whether a building on this coast holds up: what it sits on and what covers it. Both are unglamorous, both are hidden once the work is done, and both are where corners get cut because nobody will see the difference for years.

That is the whole of what this company does, and the reason it does both.

## What kind of contractor is this?

A Texas construction company doing dirt work, site preparation and complete roofing for residential, commercial and industrial projects. One contractor, two core capabilities, organised around getting the specification right rather than getting the bid low.

The aim is stated plainly and we would rather be held to it than to something more impressive: do the job right, build it to last, and be the contractor our customers can depend on.

What that means in practice is less about promises and more about the parts of a job that are checkable. Whether the fill under a pad was engineered or merely placed. Whether the fastener on a coastal roof matches the substrate it is driven into. Whether the windstorm paperwork went in before demolition or got remembered afterwards. Those are the decisions that separate a building that performs from one that looks the same on handover day and does not.

The two divisions are genuinely separate disciplines, and we do not pretend that every customer needs both. If you have a leaking roof on an existing house, you need a roofer and the earthwork side is irrelevant to you. Where both do apply — new construction, commercial development, a property being reworked from the dirt up — running them together removes the seam where projects usually lose time.

## Why does this coast take more than a crew and a truck?

Because three separate conditions here punish generic construction, and none of them is visible from a photograph of a finished job.

The ground moves. Much of this region sits on expansive clays that swell when wet and crack when dry, with cracks that stay open for a large share of the year and, in the poorly drained series, runoff that ranges from very slow to ponded. Building a pad on that without an engineered approach produces a structure that looks correct and behaves badly. Texas practice is blunt about it: foundations are not to be supported by non-engineered fill.

The air corrodes. Airborne chloride is hygroscopic, so it holds moisture against metal long after rain has stopped, and it attacks the protective layers on the common roofing metals. Manufacturer warranties respond to this by excluding steel-based substrates within a defined distance of saltwater, measured in feet rather than in miles. Getting that boundary wrong means installing a roof with no substrate warranty behind it and no way to know until it fails.

The insurance regime is separate from the building regime. Cameron and Willacy counties sit inside the state's designated catastrophe area, which layers a windstorm certification process on top of the ordinary permit. It runs through its own authority, on its own code edition, and it has to be started before work begins. A contractor who treats it as paperwork to be filed at the end has already cost you the cheap version.

None of that is exotic. It is just specific, and specificity is what generic construction does not carry.

## What are the two capabilities, and how do they fit together?

Site preparation covers everything between raw ground and a surface something can be built on: clearing, excavation, cut and fill, grading and levelling, building pads, trenching, backfilling, compaction, drainage and site shaping, road and driveway preparation, and at larger scale commercial site development and heavy earthmoving.

Roofing is deliberately not limited to one system. Rather than working with a single roof type, the company works across a wide range of residential and commercial systems — asphalt shingle, standing seam metal, exposed-fastener panel, and the low-slope families including TPO and modified bitumen — plus repairs, leak detection, decking and structural work, flashing and penetrations, storm damage, and preventative maintenance.

The reason the breadth matters is that a contractor who installs one system will recommend that system. When the only product is standing seam, every barn becomes a standing seam candidate, and the owner pays for an answer to a question they did not have. Being able to install the right system across the range is what makes it possible to specify honestly, including specifying the cheaper option when it is correct.

Where the two divisions genuinely combine is new construction. The pad and the roof are the first and last structural decisions on a build, and when different contractors own them the site gets graded for one crew's convenience and inherited by another. One schedule and one point of accountability closes that gap. That span — from the ground underneath a building to the roof over everything in it — is the shape of the business rather than a slogan about it.

## What do we not do?

A short list, stated up front, because a contractor's limits are more useful to you than their claims.

We do not do concrete, framing, plumbing or solar. We do not offer inspections as a service, and we do not have in-house engineering. The service list on this site is closed, and every item on it corresponds to work the company actually performs. A service page for work an owner cannot deliver is a liability rather than an asset, and this site does not carry any.

We do not issue windstorm certificates, because no contractor in Texas can. Certification comes from the state insurance department, one of its appointed qualified inspectors, or a Texas-licensed professional engineer. What a contractor can do is build to the certifiable specification and sequence the filings correctly, and that is what we do.

We do not make regulatory determinations. Floodplain status, base flood elevation, drainage district approval and adopted code editions are decisions belonging to authorities, and where a project needs a geotechnical recommendation or a foundation design, that is a licensed engineer's work.

And we do not describe ourselves as licensed without qualifying it, because Texas does not license roofing contractors at all. The state's licensing agency regulates dozens of trades and roofing is not among them. There is a voluntary industry credential that includes the word in its name, and cities may require their own registration, but there is no state roofing licence for anyone to hold. Any company in this market implying otherwise is relying on you not to check.

## Why are we not the cheapest bid you will get?

Because the cheapest bid on this coast is usually cheapest for a reason you cannot see until later.

The honest position, in the company's own terms: we may not always be the cheapest option, and we do not try to be. The competition is on quality, reliability, communication and a finished product worth putting a name on. The goal is best value rather than the lowest number.

What that looks like in a real estimate is scope specificity. A bid that names the manufacturer but not the membrane thickness, the fastener grade, the base course depth or the compaction specification is not cheaper than a bid that does. It is smaller. The difference reappears as a change order once the roof is open or the ground is cut, at a moment when you have no leverage and no time.

A price that changes after work begins is the risk worth guarding against, and it is worth naming without naming anyone. The defence against it is not a promise. It is a scope written specifically enough that there is nothing left to revise. Deck condition handled with an agreed unit rate up front. Fastener and substrate stated. Certification responsibility assigned to a named party at a stated point in the schedule.

You should also expect to be told when the cheaper answer is the right one. A localised failure on a sound roof with dry insulation underneath is a repair, and selling a replacement into that situation is how this trade lost its reputation. Where R-panel is the correct specification for an agricultural or simple residential roof, that is what gets quoted, not an upgrade to something with a better margin.

## What happens between your first call and the last inspection?

The sequence is deliberate, and most of the expensive mistakes in this region are sequencing errors rather than workmanship errors.

It starts with scope and jurisdiction. What you are building or what is failing, the address, and whether the property sits inside a city or in unincorporated county. That last answer decides which authority reviews the work, and it is the thing owners most often do not know.

Then assessment. On a roof, that means establishing where water is actually getting in rather than where the stain appears, and whether the insulation and deck below have been affected. On ground, it means what the soil is, how the site drains after real rain, whether a survey or geotechnical report exists, and whether a drainage district easement crosses the tract.

Then the filings, before demolition or excavation. Windstorm application in first where it applies. Permits, floodplain and drainage approvals resolved before a site plan is finalised rather than after it is drawn.

Then the work, built to the specification that can actually be certified, phased around whatever has to keep operating, and sized so that open roof area can be closed the same day. That last point is climate-driven: this region sees relatively few storm days a year, with the peak in May and downbursts as the main wind threat, so risk arrives as a small number of sharp events rather than steady weather.

Then close-out, which is the part that matters years later. The certificate. The permit records. Manufacturer documentation for the system actually installed rather than the one originally quoted. Those travel with the property, and reconstructing them during a sale or a claim is nobody's good week.

## What does a repair actually buy you?

Time, when it is the right call, and a wasted year when it is not.

The economics of a small roof repair are better than most owners assume, because the alternative is not a replacement later at today's price. It is a replacement later plus everything the water did in the meantime. On a low-slope roof, a leak that reaches the insulation costs you the thermal value, and wet insulation does not dry in a climate where vapour is being driven inward most of the year. From there it reaches the deck, and the deck is the expensive part of any roof.

The same logic runs on the ground. Water standing against a slab or running toward a building instead of away from it is a grading problem that costs a fraction of the foundation problem it eventually becomes.

The honest limit on repairs is equally worth stating. A localised failure on an assembly that is otherwise sound and dry is a genuine repair, and the right recommendation. Widespread saturation underneath a roof is not a repair no matter how modest the visible damage looks, because the insulation is already gone and the fastener holding capacity that the wind rating depends on is going with it. A contractor who patches into that situation is selling you a year.

Knowing which situation you are in requires somebody to establish where the water actually is, rather than following the stain on the ceiling. Water travels along deck flutes and insulation joints before it appears inside, so the visible mark is frequently nowhere near the breach.

## What should you do before each storm season?

A short annual routine, most of which you can do yourself, and one item you should not.

Clear the drainage. Blocked drains, scuppers and gutters are the most common cause of water sitting where it should not, and standing water on a low-slope roof is measured against a manufacturer definition of 48 hours after rain stops. On the ground, check that the paths water took last year are still open.

Look at the details rather than the field. Roof failures start at edges, curbs, penetrations, flashings and equipment supports far more often than in the middle of a sound surface. On exposed-fastener metal, this is also when you check for backed-out fasteners, since pinned panels work their fixings loose over time and those roofs genuinely want looking at about twice a year.

Rinse the sheltered metal. Salt accumulates where rain does not reach — undersides, eave returns, the shadowed side of equipment — and because chloride holds moisture against the surface, those unwashed areas corrode faster than the parts the rain cleans. Freshwater rinsing is real maintenance rather than an upsell.

Photograph the building while it is undamaged. A dated set of images of the roof, the exterior and the interior is the cheapest insurance documentation available, and its value is entirely determined by having taken it beforehand. If you hold installation paperwork or a windstorm certificate, know where it is now rather than after a storm.

The item not to do yourself is walking a roof you are not equipped to walk, particularly a wet low-slope membrane or an aged deck. Damage assessment from the ground, with binoculars, tells you whether to call somebody. It does not require you on a ladder in August.

## What covers the work after the crew leaves?

Two separate documents, from two different parties, and knowing which is which matters more than any adjective attached to either.

The manufacturer's warranty covers the product. It is issued by the company that made the membrane, the panel or the shingle, it is subject to that company's own exclusions, and on most systems it depends on the material having been installed to their published instructions by someone they recognise. It frequently has to be registered after completion, and an unregistered warranty is a document that does not exist yet.

The workmanship warranty covers the installation, and it is issued by the contractor. That is a different obligor with a different term and different exclusions. When somebody quotes you a single warranty figure without saying which of the two it refers to, that is worth a follow-up question rather than a nod.

Exclusions are where the real information sits. Ponding is treated differently across systems, and on one common metal substrate it is excluded absolutely while some premium single-ply guarantees do cover leaks in ponding areas. Impact-resistant shingles are, by their own manufacturers' language, not warranted against hail damage. Coastal proximity voids substrate coverage on steel-based metals entirely.

What we will not do is publish an unqualified promise of coverage for life. That language is common in this trade, it is rarely backed by a document anyone will produce on request, and a warranty is only worth the terms written into it. Specific terms for this company's work are stated in writing on the estimate rather than advertised as a slogan, and you should read them the same way you would read anyone else's.

## How should you check any contractor here, including us?

With questions that have specific answers, rather than with adjectives.

Ask who files the windstorm application and at what point in the schedule. A contractor who is vague about this either does not do it or does it late, and late is the version that requires an engineer.

Ask what substrate and what fastener grade, and how far the property is from saltwater. If the answer does not distinguish between the metal options by their coastal warranty status, the person quoting has not thought about corrosion.

Ask what happens if the deck is worse than assumed, and get the rate in writing before work starts.

Ask for insurance certificates rather than assurances about being licensed and insured, since the licensing half of that phrase does not mean what it appears to mean in this state.

Ask what the compaction specification is and against which standard, and how thick the lifts will be. On a pad, that single question separates people who move dirt from people who build ground.

And ask what they will not do. A contractor who cannot name their limits has not found them yet.
