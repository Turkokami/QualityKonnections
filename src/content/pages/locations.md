This is a coastal plain that looks uniform from a car window and is not. Inside an hour's drive the ground goes from well-drained sandy loam to sodic clay that ponds, from a wind zone rated at one design speed to one rated at another, and from a city building department to a county desk with a drainage district sitting behind it.

That is why this site has a page per town instead of one page with the names swapped. The pages that matter are the ones that tell you what your ground does, not the ones that repeat a service list with a different heading.

## What defines the territory, and where does it stop?

Cameron County, Willacy County, and the eastern edge of Hidalgo County. Beyond that we stop.

That boundary is not arbitrary and it is not a coverage-map flourish. It follows the line that actually changes how the work is done. Cameron and Willacy sit inside the state's designated catastrophe area for windstorm. Hidalgo County does not; it is second-tier, which sounds adjacent and is a different regulatory world. A structure on one side of that line goes through a certification process that a structure on the other side has no equivalent for.

So when you see McAllen, Edinburg, Mission and Pharr absent from the list below, that is deliberate rather than an oversight. Those are real cities with real construction, and they are outside the territory we have confirmed we work. Claiming them would cost nothing on a website and would eventually cost somebody a schedule.

The same restraint applies to the far end. We work the Texas side of this region. We do not work across the bridges.

## Why does one county line change the whole job?

Because it decides whether a second compliance track exists at all.

Inside Cameron and Willacy, a full roof replacement carries a windstorm certification process with its own authority, its own filing sequence and its own code edition. That process has to be started before the work begins, and starting it late is expensive rather than merely inconvenient. Outside those counties, none of it applies.

The result is that identical houses, built by the same builder in the same decade, get different scopes of work depending on which county they landed in. A homeowner comparing quotes across that line is not comparing like with like, and a contractor who works both sides has to be able to explain why.

The detail is covered properly on the [compliance](/compliance/) pages. What matters at territory level is simpler: know which county your property is in before you accept anyone's price, because the county is doing more work in that number than the square footage is.

## How does the ground change between the river and the bay?

It gets wetter, saltier and less willing to drain.

The western and inland parts of this territory include genuinely straightforward soils. Laredo silty clay loam, Willacy fine sandy loam and Hidalgo sandy clay loam are well drained and moderately permeable, with low shrink-swell behaviour. Sites on those series behave the way an out-of-region contractor expects them to.

Moving east and toward the bay margins, the profile changes. The Vertisols take over — clays that swell when wet and crack when dry, with very slow permeability and, in the poorly drained series, runoff that ranges from very slow to outright ponded. Seasonal high water tables sit within a few feet of the surface for much of the year in places.

At the bay margin the chemistry becomes the problem rather than the drainage. These are ponded, sodic, hypersaline clays with the water table within a few feet of grade, in a region where groundwater is mostly brackish. The Laguna Madre itself is a hypersaline lagoon of roughly 280,910 acres averaging about four and a half feet deep, which tells you what the surrounding flats are dealing with.

Underneath much of the Brownsville area the mapped geology is Holocene muddy floodplain alluvium and low-permeability Beaumont mud and clay. That is the parent material behind a lot of the drainage behaviour people experience as a mystery in their back yard.

One naming trap worth carrying into any conversation about a soils report: Point Isabel is a clay series, despite what the name suggests to anyone thinking about the coast, while South Padre Island genuinely is sand. Getting those two backwards changes a pad design.

## What do the resacas do to a site?

They are the defining feature of building in and around Brownsville, and they are frequently misdescribed.

Resacas are former channels of the Rio Grande, cut off as the river was controlled. Falcon Dam and the associated flood-control works both severed the existing channels and prevented new ones from forming, which is why the resaca system is a fixed inheritance rather than a living floodplain feature.

The scale has changed enormously. Against an estimated 70,000 acres before agricultural expansion in the 1870s, roughly 3,500 acres remain. The Corps of Engineers evaluated 66 resacas near Brownsville, ranging from under one acre to over 50, averaging about 18. Three cross the urban core: Resaca de la Guerra, Resaca del Rancho Viejo and the Town Resaca. Further out you have Resaca de los Fresnos at San Benito, Resaca de los Cuates between Los Fresnos and Bayview, and Resaca de la Palma.

They are not decorative. Brownsville Public Utilities Board manages over 50 miles of the city's historic resaca waterways, holding something like four million cubic yards of sediment, under a 75-year maintenance plan begun in 2013. The system carries irrigation, raw water supply, stormwater drainage and recreation at the same time.

For a property owner the practical effect is that a resaca near your lot is simultaneously an amenity, a drainage constraint and somebody else's easement. Where your water goes, and whether you may alter the ground near the bank, is not purely your decision.

A note on names, because two places share one: Resaca de la Palma Battlefield is a National Park Service unit, and Resaca de la Palma State Park is a separate Texas Parks and Wildlife site. They are different places and directions given for one will not get a crew to the other.

## What separates the barrier island from the mainland?

Almost everything that governs a site.

South Padre Island is Mustang fine sand, a poorly drained barrier-island soil with a seasonal water table between 11 and 30 inches of the surface. It is nonsaline as a soil, sitting in one of the most corrosive atmospheres in the state.

It is also the only part of Cameron County in the Seaward wind zone. And the flood rules there are categorically different: in coastal high-hazard V and VE zones, communities must prohibit the use of fill for structural support of buildings. Elevation is achieved with piles or columns, and anything enclosed below has to be breakaway construction.

That last rule ends a certain kind of conversation before it starts. On the mainland, raising a building often means bringing in dirt. On the Gulf side of the island, bringing in dirt is not an available answer to the elevation problem, and a site-prep bid written on mainland assumptions is wrong on arrival.

The corrosion environment compounds it. Coastal metal substrate warranties are void within 1,500 feet of saltwater, which on a barrier island is most of it. That is a materials decision with a warranty consequence rather than a preference.

Port Isabel, just across the water, is a useful contrast: a clay soil, in the Inland I wind zone rather than Seaward, under its own municipal code adoption. Two towns you can see from each other, running different specifications.

## Which authorities actually set the rules where you are?

More of them than most owners expect, and they do not coordinate.

Inside a city, the municipal building department reviews and inspects against whichever code edition that city has adopted, on its own schedule. Outside city limits, the county takes that role, and the answer to "what is required" changes accordingly.

Layered across both are the water authorities. Cameron County contains drainage districts numbered 1, 3, 4, 5 and 6, plus irrigation districts including Bayview, Brownsville, Cameron County numbers 2, 6 and 16, Harlingen, Hidalgo and Cameron Counties number 9, and La Feria. Under state water law these districts hold genuine rulemaking authority over their easements, which means a district can govern what you build near its channel, how you cross it and what you may discharge into it — regardless of what the city told you.

Then there is the regional drainage picture. The Arroyo Colorado is an ancient distributary of the Rio Grande running roughly 90 miles from Mission to the Laguna Madre, draining a watershed of about 706 square miles and functioning as an integral part of a major floodway system. Of 105,000 cubic feet per second diverted at Anzalduas, about 21,000 goes to the Arroyo. It is not a drainage ditch, and it is not in good health: the tidal segment carries a dissolved oxygen impairment and the segment above it a bacteria impairment.

Local elevation standards vary and are worth asking for by name. Harlingen publishes a citable one — foundations in Zone X no less than 18 inches above top of curb, 24 inches inside a Special Flood Hazard Area, and finished floors six inches above neighbouring lots within 100 feet of a drainage inlet. Most jurisdictions have an equivalent answer even when it is not published; somebody at the counter knows it.

## What kind of market is this, honestly?

A steady re-roof and repair market on an older housing stock, with a genuine industrial surge sitting alongside it. Not a residential boom.

The census numbers support that reading rather than the promotional one. Brownsville was estimated at 192,957 as of 1 July 2025, up 3.6 percent since 2020. Cameron County stood at 433,946, up 3.0 percent. That is real growth and it is modest — roughly three percent across five years.

The housing profile matters more than the growth rate. The county holds 166,569 housing units at 65.2 percent owner-occupied, with a median home value of $136,500; in Brownsville the median value is $139,900 with owner-occupancy at 61.7 percent. High owner-occupancy on moderate-value, older stock is the signature of a repair and replacement market. People here fix what they own.

We say this plainly because the alternative is to write the version where the Valley is exploding and every page is about new construction. That version is easy to write, and every owner who lives here knows it is wrong.

## Where is the growth actually happening?

On the industrial side, and it is substantial.

The Port of Brownsville completed the Brazos Island Harbor Channel Improvement Project on 25 June 2026, deepening the main channel from 42 to 52 feet and the entrance and jetty channels from 44 to 54, at roughly $295.2 million, with about $50 million of landside work planned. It is the largest land-owning public port authority in the county at more than 40,000 acres, supporting over 6,000 direct jobs and handling 458,800 truck movements and 85,216 rail cars.

Out at Boca Chica, Starbase incorporated as a city following a 3 May 2025 election carried 212 to 6, taking effect on 20 May 2025 as a Type C municipality of just over 500 people, with Bobby Peden as mayor. State legislation in 2025 moved authority to close Boca Chica Beach and State Highway 4 to the city, and SpaceX is authorised for 25 launches a year, up from five.

On the LNG side, Rio Grande LNG took a final investment decision on Train 4 on 9 September 2025 at roughly $6.7 billion, followed by Train 5 in October 2025, with something like 7,500 construction and trade jobs at peak, around 700 permanent, and first LNG targeted for 2027. Texas LNG issued a limited notice to proceed on 2 July 2026 with Kiewit as EPC contractor, but has not reached a final investment decision — a distinction worth keeping straight. Annova LNG cancelled its Brownsville project back in March 2021 and should not appear on anyone's list of active developments.

What that means for construction here is warehouse, laydown, industrial pad and commercial roof work rather than a subdivision wave. It is why the [commercial](/commercial/) side of this business is not an afterthought.

## What does Willacy County change about a project?

Less than the map suggests, and one thing more than most people realise.

Willacy sits north of Cameron and carries the same fundamental status: it is inside the designated catastrophe area. A roof replacement in Raymondville, Lyford, Sebastian, San Perlita or Port Mansfield is a certified project the same way a Cameron County one is, with the same requirement to file before work starts.

What we will not do is publish a wind zone for a Willacy town. The county's inclusion in the program is confirmed. The specific zone assignments are not something we have verified against the primary map, and a zone is exactly the kind of number that gets built into a specification and discovered wrong later. Ask the department, or ask the engineer who will be certifying, and get it in writing.

The ground up there is its own conversation. Raymondville clay loam is a very high shrink-swell soil, in the same family of behaviour as the Cameron County Vertisols, which means pad design is an engineering question rather than a matter of hauling in enough material.

Port Mansfield is worth separating from the rest of the county. It is a small coastal community on the Laguna Madre, and it has the distinction of being where Allen came ashore in 1980 as a Category 3, with a 138 mph gust recorded there. The exposure at that end of the county is not the same as the exposure in Raymondville twenty-odd miles inland, and treating the county as one uniform condition is how you underspecify a building.

Access and haul distance are the practical difference on dirt work. Willacy is thinner country with longer runs between a site and a source of material, and that shows up in an estimate as haul time rather than as a different rate.

## What is worth knowing about getting around this region?

Enough that a crew arrives at the right place, which is less trivial here than it sounds.

The region has two commercial airports, Valley International at Harlingen and Brownsville–South Padre Island International. The international crossings are the Gateway International Bridge, the Veterans International Bridge at Los Tomates, the Brownsville & Matamoros International Bridge, and the Free Trade International Bridge at Los Indios. Those names appear on shipping paperwork and delivery instructions constantly, and getting one wrong sends a materials truck to the wrong side of a city.

The link to the barrier island is formally the Queen Isabella Memorial Bridge. "Queen Isabella Causeway" is normal local usage and nobody will misunderstand you, but the formal name is the one that appears on official documents.

The landmarks worth knowing by their correct names include Palo Alto Battlefield National Historical Park, the Sabal Palm Sanctuary, Gladys Porter Zoo, the Historic Brownsville Museum, the Stillman House and Heritage Museum, Museo Casa Mata, the Brownsville Museum of Fine Art, the South Texas Ecotourism Center, Laguna Atascosa National Wildlife Refuge, the Lower Rio Grande Valley National Wildlife Refuge, Isla Blanca Park, and the Port Isabel Lighthouse State Historic Site — an 1852 structure standing 72 feet, the last Texas lighthouse open to the public, with its Fresnel lens reinstalled in 2022.

The reason a construction company bothers to get these right is simple. Directions given by landmark are still how work gets found out here, and a company that writes the local names correctly is usually a company that has been to the places.

## What should you have ready before you call about a property?

A short list, and having it turns a vague conversation into a real one.

The address and, if you have it, whether the property sits inside city limits or in the unincorporated county. That single answer determines which building authority applies and is the most common thing nobody knows.

For roofing: roughly how old the current roof is, whether it has been replaced before, what the slope looks like, and whether there is any documentation from previous work. If there has been a leak, where it shows up inside and after which kind of rain. A leak that only appears in wind-driven rain is telling you something different from one that appears in any downpour.

For dirt work: what you are building or what is failing, whether you have a survey, whether you have a geotechnical report, and whether anyone has told you about a drainage district easement on the tract. If water stands somewhere after rain, knowing where and for how long is worth more than any photograph of dry ground.

For anything insurance-related: your carrier, and whether you already hold a windstorm certificate for the structure. If you do not know, that itself is useful information, and it is better established before work is scoped than after.

None of that is a prerequisite for a phone call. It just changes how much of the first visit is spent finding things out that you already knew.

Because a thin page is worse than no page, and we would rather admit the gap than fill it with filler.

Each location page here has to carry material a competitor could not produce by find-and-replace: the soil series under that specific town, its wind zone assignment, which authority reviews its permits, what the drainage does, what the housing stock is actually made of. That takes research per town rather than per region.
