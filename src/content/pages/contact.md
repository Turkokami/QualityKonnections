An estimate is only as good as what it was based on. The fastest way to get a number that stays the number is to give the right information at the start, and to accept that some projects cannot be priced accurately without somebody standing on the property.

What follows is what makes an estimate accurate here, what can be settled without a visit, and what to ask before you accept anyone's bid.

## What makes an estimate accurate rather than a guess?

Specific answers to a small number of questions, most of which you already know.

The address, first, and whether the property sits inside a city or in unincorporated county. That single distinction decides which authority reviews the work and which set of requirements applies, and it is the thing most owners have never had reason to establish. It changes scope, not just paperwork.

What you are building, or what is failing. "The roof leaks" and "the roof has been leaking for three years and there is a soft spot in the ceiling" are different projects. On the ground side, "I need a pad" and "I need a pad and water stands at the back of the lot after any real rain" are different projects too, and the second one is the honest version.

Age and history. When the roof was last replaced, whether it has been repaired since, and whether any documentation survives. On a site, whether anything has been done to the drainage, and whether anyone has mentioned an easement across the tract.

Constraints. Access for equipment and deliveries, anything on the property that cannot be disturbed, and whether the building has to keep operating during the work.

That is enough for a useful first conversation. None of it requires you to have technical knowledge, and all of it reduces the number of surprises that later turn into change orders.

## What can be settled without a site visit?

More than you might expect, and less than you would like.

Answerable remotely: whether the work is something this company does at all, whether the property is inside the service area, roughly what process the job will have to go through, whether a windstorm certification track applies, what documentation you will eventually be asked for, and whether what you are describing sounds like a repair or a replacement.

Also answerable remotely, and worth asking early: whether your project needs an engineer. Foundation design, geotechnical recommendations, post-construction certification and design wind speed for a specific structure are engineering determinations. Knowing that before you gather bids saves you from comparing quotes that assume different things about who is producing that work.

Not answerable remotely: price, on almost anything. A roof number depends on the actual slope, the number and type of penetrations, the condition of the deck underneath, access, and the system genuinely appropriate for the exposure. A dirt work number depends on how much material moves, how far it travels, what can be reused, and what the compaction specification requires.

Be wary of a firm price given sight-unseen. It is not generosity. It is a number that has room built into it, or room to be revised once the work is open and you have no leverage.

## What happens on a first site visit?

Looking at the things that decide the specification, which are not always the things that prompted the call.

On a roof, that means slope and how it drains, the condition of the covering, the details where failures start — edges, flashings, curbs, penetrations, equipment supports — and, where it can be established, what is happening below the surface. Interior staining is a poor locator because water travels along deck flutes and insulation joints before it appears, so the mark on the ceiling is often nowhere near the breach. Establishing whether insulation is wet, and over how much area, is what separates a repair recommendation from a replacement recommendation honestly.

On ground, it means what the soil actually is, how the site sits relative to what is around it, where water goes now, and where it would go after the work. Signs of ponding, existing drainage structures, and any easement or channel adjacent to the tract. If you can point out where water stood after the last real rain, that is the single most valuable thing you can contribute.

Both visits include the question of access, because on infill lots, occupied commercial sites and island properties, what can physically reach the work determines the method and therefore the schedule.

Expect to be asked what you want out of the project, including how long you intend to own the property. That is not sales technique. A property being sold in two years and a property being kept for thirty justify different specifications, and the honest answer changes.

## How does a roofing estimate differ from a dirt work estimate?

They have different unknowns, and each hides its risk in a different place.

A roofing estimate's main unknown is the deck. Everything above it can be assessed from the surface; the deck cannot be fully known until the covering is off. The correct way to handle that is a stated unit rate for deck repair agreed before work begins, so that if the deck is worse than assumed there is already a number rather than a negotiation. An estimate that is silent on deck condition has not eliminated the risk. It has left it with you.

The other roofing variable is the system itself, and this is where estimates diverge legitimately. Substrate, thickness, attachment method and fastener grade are the specification. Two bids naming the same manufacturer can describe materially different roofs. Near saltwater the substrate choice carries warranty consequences that do not appear on a line item at all.

A dirt work estimate's main unknown is the material. What is on site, whether it can be reused, what has to be imported, and what the engineer requires the fill to be. Haul distance is usually the cost driver people underestimate, because it is billable movement rather than visible work.

Compaction is the other. A specification stated as a percentage means nothing until you know which laboratory standard it references and how thick the lifts will be, since material placed too deep does not densify through its full depth regardless of how many passes the roller makes.

Both estimates should also say who is responsible for permits and, where it applies, for filing the windstorm application, and at what point in the schedule.

## How is urgent damage handled differently?

By protecting the building first and deciding the scope second.

Active water intrusion is a different problem from a roof that needs replacing. The immediate objective is to stop water entering, get it out of the building, and prevent the interior damage from compounding, which on a low-slope roof happens quickly once a seam is open.

Documentation belongs in that first window too, and it is the step owners most often skip and later need. Dated photographs of the exterior, the roof surface where it can be viewed safely, the interior damage and any equipment affected, plus a record of what temporary work was done and when.

Then the scope conversation, and it should happen with somebody willing to tell you the damage does not warrant a replacement. Storm events attract volume operators, and the documented regional failure pattern across major storms has been loss of roof coverings on lower-quality construction rather than structural collapse. That means many storm-damaged roofs genuinely need replacing and many others need a repair being marketed as a replacement.

One sequencing point matters more under urgency than at any other time. If damage is severe enough that the work becomes a replacement, the windstorm application has to be filed before the tear-off starts. Urgency is exactly the condition under which that gets skipped, and skipping it converts a routine inspection into an after-the-fact engineering certification at your cost.

For anything actively leaking or storm-related, a call is better than a written request, because it gets triaged rather than queued.

## What should you ask before accepting any bid?

Questions with specific answers. Adjectives are not answers.

Ask who files the windstorm application, and when in the schedule. Vagueness here means it happens late or not at all, and late is the expensive version.

Ask what substrate and what fastener grade, and how far the property is from saltwater. If the response does not distinguish between the metal options by their coastal warranty status, corrosion has not been considered.

Ask what happens if the deck is worse than assumed, and get the rate in writing before anyone starts.

Ask which warranty is being quoted. The manufacturer's warranty on the product and the contractor's warranty on the installation are separate documents from separate parties with separate exclusions, and a single headline number that does not say which one it refers to is not information.

Ask for insurance certificates rather than an assurance about being licensed and insured. Texas does not license roofing contractors at state level at all, so that half of the phrase does not carry the meaning most people assume. Certificates are checkable.

Ask, on a pad, what the compaction specification is, against which standard, and at what lift thickness. That one question distinguishes contractors who move dirt from contractors who build ground.

And ask what they will not do. A contractor who cannot name their own limits has not reached them yet.

## How should you compare two bids that are far apart?

By finding what the cheaper one left out, because on this coast that is almost always the explanation.

A large gap between bids on the same building is rarely about margin. It is about scope, and the differences cluster in predictable places. Deck repair, priced or excluded. Fastener grade and substrate, specified or generic. Membrane thickness and insulation, named or implied. Edge metal and penetration details, itemised or absorbed into a field price. Permit and certification responsibility, assigned or unmentioned. Tear-off and disposal, included or extra.

Lay the two documents side by side and mark every line one contains that the other does not. That exercise usually accounts for most of the difference without anyone having to argue about labour rates.

Then check the parts that are not line items at all. Whether the system named is actually appropriate for the slope, since every system has a minimum below which its own instructions no longer apply. Whether the metal specified carries a substrate warranty at that property's distance from saltwater. Whether the fastening pattern quoted meets the requirement that applies county-wide here rather than the lighter pattern used inland.

A bid can be cheaper for legitimate reasons. Lower overhead, closer to the site, better material pricing, a simpler approach that genuinely suits the building. Those reasons survive being asked about. Scope gaps do not.

The last comparison is the least tangible and the most predictive: which contractor told you something you did not want to hear. A bidder who says a repair will do, or that a question belongs to an engineer, or that part of what you asked for is unnecessary, is demonstrating the behaviour you actually want later, when the deck is open and the decision is theirs to make honestly.

## What does a commercial enquiry need that a residential one does not?

More parties named, and the documentation question settled at the start.

Name who decides. On commercial work the person requesting the estimate is frequently not the person who approves it, and neither is always the person on site when a decision is needed at seven in the morning. Establishing that chain before mobilisation prevents the most common source of lost days.

Operational constraints matter as much as physical ones. Which areas can be out of service and when, whether work can happen outside business hours, how deliveries reach the site without crossing operations, where material can stage, and what happens to the interior if weather arrives mid-tear-off. Those answers shape the price legitimately, so withholding them does not produce a lower number, only a less accurate one.

State the documentation you will need at the end, at the beginning. Insurers, lenders, corporate parents and auditors ask for specific things: the system specified and the system installed, manufacturer paperwork, inspection records, warranty registration, and certification status. Producing those is straightforward when it is planned and painful when it is retrofitted.

Exchange insurance requirements early. You will want certificates from any bidder, and bidders will need to know what limits and endorsements your contract requires. Getting that mismatch discovered at award rather than at contract signing saves a week.

Finally, flag anything already known about the site: a geotechnical report, a survey, a drainage district easement, floodplain status, or prior roof work. On commercial ground those documents usually exist somewhere in a file, and they are worth more to an estimate than any amount of description.

## What if you are not sure you need a contractor at all?

Then say so, and expect to be pointed somewhere else if that is the right answer.

A fair number of enquiries here turn out to belong to somebody other than a roofing or earthwork contractor. Water appearing at a wall may be a gutter and downspout issue rather than a roof failure. Standing water in a yard may originate on a neighbouring property or inside a drainage district's easement, in which case moving dirt relocates the problem rather than solving it. A question about whether a structure can be built at a given elevation is a floodplain determination, which is an authority's decision and not a contractor's opinion.

Some questions belong to an engineer outright. Foundation design on expansive clay, the required depth and composition of engineered fill, design wind speed for a specific structure, and any post-construction certification are engineering determinations. There is no code-mandated select fill depth in Texas to look up; it is specified per project by the engineer, and a contractor quoting a standard depth without one is quoting a habit.

Others belong to a building department, and the fastest route is usually to ask them directly. Which code edition applies, what a local elevation standard requires, and whether a particular scope needs a permit are all answers somebody at that counter can give you.

There is no cost to asking, and no obligation attached to an estimate. Being told that what you have described does not need the work you assumed is a legitimate outcome, and it is a better use of everyone's time than a proposal for work that will not fix the problem.

## What if the work has to wait?

Then it is worth knowing what deteriorates while it waits, because not everything degrades at the same rate.

Some things hold. A roof that is aged but sound, draining properly and dry underneath will generally keep doing that for a while, and deferring it to a better budget year is a defensible decision. So is a grading project on a lot where water is inconvenient rather than reaching a structure.

Some things do not hold, and these are worth separating out honestly. Any active water entry compounds, because once moisture reaches insulation in this climate it does not dry — vapour is being driven inward for most of the year, so the assembly cannot shed it in the direction the weather is pushing. From there it reaches the deck, and a membrane problem becomes a structural one. Deferring an active leak does not defer the cost; it changes which cost you eventually pay.

Water standing against a foundation on expansive clay belongs in the same category. These soils change volume with moisture, and repeated wetting and drying at one edge of a structure is precisely the condition that produces differential movement.

Backed-out fasteners on exposed-fastener metal are cheap now and expensive after a wind event, because each one is a hole.

Where a project genuinely has to be staged, it can usually be phased sensibly: protect what is failing, defer what is merely old, and sequence the rest around budget years. That conversation is worth having explicitly rather than by default, and it produces a plan rather than a series of emergencies.

What we would rather not do is quote a full replacement to somebody who needs a repair and a schedule.

## What happens after you approve an estimate?

Filings first, then work, then the documents that outlive both.

Where a windstorm certification track applies, the application goes in before demolition or excavation. Permits and any floodplain, drainage district or stormwater approvals get resolved before the site plan is finalised rather than after, because a district's allowable discharge rate discovered late means redesigning a site that has already been graded on paper.

Scheduling is agreed with the constraints already known: which areas can be out of service, where material stages, how deliveries reach the site, and who on your side can make a decision without convening a meeting. Open roof area is sized to what can be closed the same day, which in this climate is a weather decision rather than a productivity one, since the regional pattern is a small number of sharp storm events rather than steady rain.

Then close-out, which is the part that matters years later and the part most commonly done badly. The certificate where applicable. Permit records. Manufacturer documentation for the system that was actually installed, which is not always the system originally quoted. Warranty registration completed rather than assumed.

Those documents travel with the property. They get requested during a sale, a refinance, an insurance review or a claim, and reconstructing them long after the fact ranges from expensive to impossible.
